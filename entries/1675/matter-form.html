Snapshot c. 1675 · Natural philosophy

Matter and form

What teaching texts printed at Paris, Wittenberg, Bologna and London between 1664 and 1686 said about the first principles of natural things. All four still speak of matter and form, but they mean different things by both words: for the two Cartesians matter is extension and form is the size, shape and motion of its parts; for the two schoolmen form is a substance of its own, and they quarrel between themselves over first matter and where forms come from.

First cut, from one text per region, on matter, form and privation, the origin and end of forms, real qualities, and whether the principles of nature must be clear. The void, place and the elements are left to their own topics.

Physics, first part, disputation I, on the principles of natural being: question I, article III, pp. 21–42; question II, on first matter, pp. 42–86; question III, on substantial form, pp. 87–112.

Where matter and form sat among the sciences

All four put the subject at the head of their physics, but they lead into it differently. Rohault comes to it straight from five chapters on knowledge and method: the principles of natural beings, then matter, then its corollaries, and only after motion and the rest does he turn to forms, which open his account of particular bodies, beginning with the elements.R 36R 141 Sperling’s first book is on the causes of the natural body, internal and external: matter and form, then the efficient and final causes.S 27S 47 Goudin’s first disputation, on the principles of mobile being, has four questions: principles in common, first matter, substantial form and the composite of the two; the first of them carries a long refutation of René Descartes (1596–1650).G 9G 22 Le Grand, having proved that material things exist, defines matter, treats its properties and the three elements, and then clears away substantial forms and real qualities to make room for what he calls the intelligible forms.L 165L 180

What the texts taught in common

  • Matter alone does not make a thing what it is. Rohault takes wood turned into fire as his pattern: something common to the two remains, the matter, and something else joined to it makes it wood rather than fire, the form, “qui ne donne pas absolument l’Estre à la matiere, mais qui luy donne un tel Estre”, which gives the matter not being outright but such a being.R 36 The form of a being is what makes it in particular what it is.R 141 Sperling: neither matter alone nor form alone makes a man, nor a form with any matter whatever, but with a matter worked up according to nature’s laws.S 48S 49 Goudin: matter is indifferent to every kind of thing, as wax to every figure, so there must be besides it a principle that determines it to be a man, fire or a tree.G 39 Le Grand, having left matter inert, must “clothe it with some forms” from which it takes its variety and distinction.L 185
  • Matter of itself is passive. Sperling: “materia in ordine ad formam activè se non habet, sed passivè”, matter does not stand actively towards form, but passively.S 82 For Goudin it is pure potency, with no act of essence of its own.G 49 Le Grand: “Corpus passivum duntaxat sit principium, & omnis propemodum actionis expers”, body is a merely passive principle, almost without any action.L 185 Rohault does not take the question up in the chapters read.
  • The rational soul stands apart from all other forms. Rohault: we know it is “une substance réellement distincte du corps auquel elle est unie”, a substance really distinct from the body it is united to, which does not depend on the body for its being; but it is of another order than the forms of bodies and proves nothing about them.R 142R 143 Sperling: no form survives its separation from the body except the rational soul, which is a spirit, without extension or division.S 117S 126 Goudin: it is not educed from matter but created and infused by God, and that men’s souls are immortal was “the constant opinion of nearly the whole world”.G 99G 100 Le Grand: the rational soul is created by God because it is a substance, and human souls are the only substantial forms there are.L 181L 182

The disputes

What a body is

Rohault sorts what we find in matter. Hardness, heat, weight, colour and the like are accidents, since there are material things without each of them; extension, divisibility, figure and impenetrability never leave it. Extension is conceived before the other three, and they presuppose it, so it is the essence.R 38R 41

“and we shall conclude, according to what reason has already made known to us, that the essence of matter consists in extension”R 41

& conclurons suivant ce qu’elle nous a déja fait connoistre, que l’essence de la matiere consiste dans l’estenduë

If someone objects that God may have put into matter something no man can know, Rohault leaves that to “those of a higher profession than a mere physicist” and keeps to what reason shows.R 41 Le Grand says the same in the same terms: physical matter, or natural body, is “substantia in longum, latum & profundum extensa”, a substance extended in length, breadth and depth, and nothing else. Take away hardness, weight or colour and the body remains; if bodies drew back as fast as our hands came at them, we would feel no hardness, and they would still be bodies.L 166L 167

For the two schoolmen a body is made of matter and form. Sperling defines them as its internal causes: “Materia est causa interna, ex qua corpus naturale constituitur”, matter is the internal cause from which the natural body is constituted, and form the cause through which it is constituted.S 47S 62 Goudin: the principles of the thing once made are two only, matter and form.G 41 He takes on Descartes by name. Extension cannot be the constitutive of material substance, first because of the Eucharist: after the consecration the substance of the bread passes into the body of Christ, yet the quantity of the bread remains with its other accidents, so

“quantity is not constitutive of substance, but only an accident separable from it”G 31

quantitas non est constitutiuum substantiæ, sed solùm accidens ab ea separabile

and second because things that differ in their operations need different principles, while quantity is of one kind in all.G 31

Whether there is a first matter

Goudin proves first matter from substantial change. When an animal dies, its sense, motion and every vital operation stop at once and for good; when wood burns, all its properties go and the fire’s take their place; so the substance itself has changed, and something must underlie the change.G 44G 45

“There is a first matter, or a certain thing that can be turned into every kind of substance, as wax can be varied into every figure.”G 45

Datur materia prima, seu quædam res in omnium substantiarum genera versatilis, sicut cera in omnes figuras variari potest.

Of itself it is pure potency: it holds no act of essence, as wax holds no figure of its own.G 49

Sperling, a Lutheran schoolman, throws it out. He sets out the proofs of the Coimbra commentators and the rest, from mutual transmutation, generation, pure act, composition, contrariety and subordination,S 50S 51 and answers that

“that first matter is found only in books and in men’s little brains”S 55

Primam illam materiam non nisi in libris & hominum cerebellis deprehendi

for anyone free of preconceived opinions. No generation was ever made in it: men, beasts, plants, metals and all mixtures are generated from a second matter, from seeds or bodies answering to them. The schoolmen’s surest refuge is the transmutation of the elements, air into water and water into air; but the elements never change into one another, as his fourth book will prove.S 55S 56 The other proofs show a matter, not a first matter. It is “figmentum”, a fiction, “nondum probatum, nedum satis explicatum”, not yet proved, still less explained.S 58S 59 He allows it one use. Some keep the name for the most general substance, abstracted by the intellect from all individuals and species, the matter of the most general natural body; of this he says “Certè non malè & non absque acumine”, certainly not badly said, and not without acumen.S 59S 60

The two Cartesians reject the schoolmen’s first matter for another reason. Le Grand cannot conceive a thing that is neither what, nor how much, nor of what kind: matter stripped of all quantity and qualities is a mere fiction, and nothing in the heavens, the earth, the sea or fire was ever explained by it; nor need it be presupposed in substantial change, since corruption is only the change of some parts, some lost and others coming in their place.L 165L 166 “Dicendum igitur talem materiam, qualem Aristotelici fingunt, non posse ullo modo admitti”, such a matter as the Aristotelians feign can in no way be admitted.L 165 But both keep a matter common to all bodies: whatever subsists in the wood and then in the fire is what Rohault calls matter, “le sujet commun de tous les Estres”, the common subject of all beings, and it is extended substance.R 36R 43

Whether matter exists of itself

Goudin follows Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274): first matter has no existence of its own, but exists only by the existence of the whole composite, which it receives through the form, as the body lives by the life of the whole animal.G 63 He goes further:

“It cannot be done, even by the absolute power of God, that matter should exist without form.”G 78

Fieri non potest etiam de potentia Dei absoluta, vt materia existat sine forma.

for matter would then exist and not exist at once, existence being the effect of the form.G 78

Rohault notes that most Aristotelians read Aristotle (384–322 BC) to mean that matter is not extended and has no existence at all. Aristotle more likely spoke of matter taken in a very general way; and if he did not, one should leave him, for it is reason, not authority, that judges of the truth, and

“what does not exist differs in no way from nothing, and can have no property”R 43

ce qui n’existe point ne differe en aucune façon du neant, & ne peut estre capable d’aucune proprieté

Le Grand turns the schoolmen’s own order against them: if first matter comes before the form, and the form is educed from its potency, the form cannot give it existence, mobility and impenetrability, for “nullus effectus causam suam nobilitate antecedit”, no effect goes before its cause in nobility.L 165 Sperling, who reports the schoolmen’s quarrel over whether first matter has an act of its own without taking a side in it,S 52S 53 has a pure matter of his own. Simple bodies have a matter “whose essence no form enters”: the matter of the heaven, of fire and of all simple bodies.S 47 “Sic cœli materia nuda puraque est, omni carens mistione, omni concretione”, so the heaven’s matter is bare and pure, lacking all mixture and concretion.S 49

Whether there are substantial forms

Goudin: there must be in nature a substantial form, which with matter constitutes natural beings, “so all philosophers now commonly hold, the opinions of the ancients having been exploded”. Pure potency cannot by itself make a substance; and the properties that follow a thing’s essence come back of themselves when interrupted, as water grows cold again once the fire is taken away and an animal recovers its motion after an apoplexy, so they have a root in the thing besides its matter.G 89 For Sperling every species has its specific form, which gives the thing its being, its distinction from other kinds and its own operations:

“Hence the dog barks, the horse neighs, the pig grunts, man laughs.”S 75

Hinc canis latrat, equus hinnit, sus grunnit, homo ridet.

There are as many specific forms as there are species, as Ulisse Aldrovandi (1522–1605) says the souls of animals differ in species.S 76

Le Grand’s article VIII is headed “Nullæ dantur formæ materiales, seu substantiales in Corpore”, there are no material or substantial forms in body. Whoever denies them is accused of something like heresy in the schools, but reason obliges him to say openly that they are of no use in physics. No substance can begin to exist without being created by God, and the schools hold that substantial forms begin every day without being created; they were invented to give the causes of actions, but their defenders admit they are occult, so nothing certain follows from them; and nothing is to be admitted in philosophy that is not clearly and distinctly known.L 180L 181 Animals differ in species as two clocks or automata do, by the different arrangement and motion of their parts.L 182

“We must conclude that substantial forms are to be thrown out of the treasury of philosophy altogether, since they are obscure, and the effects of nature are best explained without them.”L 182

Igitur concludendum est substantiales formas esse penitus è Philosophico Thesauro deturbandas, cum obscuræ sint, & sine illis, optime effectuum naturalium effectus explicentur.

Rohault does not go so far. Whether there are substantial forms, forms that are true substances with an existence distinct from that of matter, depends on knowing each thing in particular: “j’ose asseurer que la solution de cette difficulté dépend de la connoissance particuliere de chaque chose”, I dare say the answer depends on the particular knowledge of each thing.R 142 The rational soul proves nothing for the rest, and it would be rash, on its example alone, to assert substantial forms in purely material beings. What can be said boldly is that some forms are essential, as liquidity to water, and others accidental, as cold to water, which is still water when hot; and Aristotle’s Greek word may as well mean essential forms as substantial ones.R 143R 144

Where forms come from

Goudin: the forms of things that are generated and perish are educed from the potency of the matter. Before they come to be they are in the matter not in act, as Anaxagoras (c. 500–c. 428 BC) thought, like money in a chest, but in potency, as every figure is in wax; to be educed is to be made out of matter by its change, emerging from its potentiality.G 97

“The corruptible forms of generated things come to be by eduction from matter, or are educed from the potency of matter.”G 97

Formæ corruptibiles rerum generatarum fiunt per eductionem à materia, seu educuntur de potentia materiæ.

So they are not made from nothing, and not created, but made from something, their own potency.G 99 The forms of the heavens and the elements are the exception: at the first creation they were not educed but created together with their matter.G 103

Sperling attacks eduction at length. It cannot be explained, proved, squared with accepted rules or freed from absurdities; its defenders, Francisco de Toledo (1532–1596) and the Coimbra commentators among them, cannot say whether the form comes from the substance of the matter, from its accidents or from nothing.S 76S 80 Matter is passive and cannot produce an effect nobler than itself, and eduction leaves the form without a proximate principal cause.S 82S 94 He follows Daniel Sennert (1572–1637) and Sebastian Basso against them, to an answer that sounds like the opposite of Goudin’s: “Formæ omninò ex nihilo fiunt”, forms come to be wholly from nothing, though this is not creation, which is the making of a whole thing.S 89 Nor are forms brought into prepared matter by the heavens, as Jean Fernel (1497–1558) taught: the heavens act on everything below the moon with one universal, indifferent influence, are themselves without soul and cannot give souls; the sun in spring only helps the seeds lying in the earth.S 94S 98 The forms of the first bodies were created with them; after the six days God gave animals, plants and other corruptible bodies the power to multiply themselves, so that like begets like, and the parents communicate the form through the seed, a new form, the same in species.S 104S 105

“Not from matter, not out of matter, but in matter; nor yet out of the form, but from the form.”S 105

Non à materia, non ex materia, sed in materia: neque etiam ex forma, sed à forma.

This he calls traduction, a word carried over from the propagation of trees by grafting.S 116

For Le Grand the question falls with the forms. Since “dari non potest aliqua substantia, quæ esse incipiat in natura rerum, & à Deo non creetur”, no substance can begin to exist in nature without being created by God, forms that the schools say are educed every day, like material accidents, are by their own account no substances.L 180L 181 Nothing is ever shown to perish wholly; it is changed into something else and takes a new figure.L 181 Size, figure, situation, motion and rest are the forms of all bodies, on which their origin, nature, properties and end depend, and he gives them in two lines of verse:L 186

“Mind, measure, rest, motion, position, figure are, with matter, the beginnings of all things.”L 186

Mens, mensura, quies, motus, positura, figura, / Sunt cum materia, cunctarum exordia rerum.

Rohault does not ask where forms come from in the chapters read.

What becomes of a form when its thing perishes

Goudin answers in the same breath as he answers where forms come from:G 99

“Likewise, when a form ceases, it is not annihilated, because it always leaves something of itself, that is, its potency, or the matter.”G 99

Similitèr quando forma desinit, non annihilatur, quia semper relinquit aliquid sui, idest, potentiam suam, seu materiam.

Sperling, having made forms come from nothing, sends them back there. They do not go back into privation, for then the horse would die and not die in the same moment, nor into some potency of matter: when they perish they are annihilated, the rational soul excepted. When the annihilation happens is a deeper question, full of nature’s mysteries: uprooted plants do not die at once, and frogs lie as if dead in winter and revive in spring.S 117S 121S 122 Le Grand uses the same difficulty against the forms themselves. If they perish when separated from matter, as all admit, by what force are they annihilated? Annihilation, like creation, belongs to God alone; a simple thing stays as it is unless changed from outside; and no example can be shown of a substance in nature perishing wholly.L 181 Rohault does not take the question up.

Whether privation is a principle

Goudin keeps Aristotle’s three principles, as all philosophers have done since:

“The principles of the generation of natural things are matter, form and privation.”G 38

Principia generationis rerum naturalium sunt materia, forma, & priuatio.

Generation is a passage from not-being to being, and privation, the want of a form in a subject apt to receive it, belongs to it on the side of the subject; once the thing is made, the privation is gone, so the principles of the thing made are matter and form alone.G 39G 41G 42 Rohault grants that a chicken must come from what is not yet a chicken, but to call that not-being a principle makes the word equivocal, since “la privation n’est pas dans les choses, & qu’elle ne concourt point à leur composition”, privation is not in things and does not enter their composition; and it explains nothing, so there are two principles only.R 37 Sperling, answering the argument for first matter from contraries: the contrariety turns into privative opposition, “Quæ privatio cum non detur, nihil probat”, and since there is no such privation, it proves nothing.S 58 Le Grand does not take privation up.

Whether qualities are real beings

Goudin’s case against extension as the essence of body rests on the Eucharist, where “remanet adhuc quantitas panis cum cæteris accidentibus”, the quantity of the bread remains with its other accidents after the bread itself is gone; quantity is therefore an accident, separable from its substance.G 31 Sperling, arguing about the cause of forms: heat is an accident, “ergò substantiæ opera & est, & operatur”, and so exists and acts by the work of a substance, as its instrument.S 94

Le Grand’s article IX answers the schools. To make accidents real beings, distinct from their subjects and able to exist apart from them, is to confuse the notion of accident with that of substance:

“Whatever can exist without a subject, even by the infinite power of God, deserves the name of substance.”L 183

Quicquid etiam per infinitam Dei potentiam, absque subjecto esse potest, Substantiæ nomen meretur.

The senses perceive only surfaces, which are modes of the substance. Heat is the substance of fire itself, the flow of its particles through the wood; figure, hunger, health and beauty are modes, dispositions or arrangements of the parts; and he does not believe so great a philosopher as Aristotle meant anything more by his qualities.L 183L 184L 185 Rohault calls hardness, heat, weight and colour accidents of matter, but in the chapters read does not ask whether they are real beings.R 38

Whether the principles of nature must be clear

For the Cartesians the obscurity of the schoolmen’s principles is the charge. Rohault: those who deny that extension is the essence of matter cannot say distinctly what they mean by matter, “ils establissent pour principe une chose obscure”, they set up an obscure thing as a principle, from which no consequence can be drawn that brings light to the mind, and so their physics is sterile.R 44 Le Grand sets conditions any form must meet, the fourth that it be defined as clearly as matter itself:

“For such must be the nature of principles, that they are so clear and evident that the human mind, attending to them, cannot doubt their truth.”L 186

Nam ea debet esse principiorum natura, ut tam clara ac evidentia sint, ut dum Mens humana ea attente considerat, de eorum veritate ambigere non possit.

The schoolmen accept that their principles are obscure, and say so. Sperling opens his explanation of form: we are at the bark and cannot reach the kernel, our knowledge creeps along the ground, and the mind knows almost no form; with Julius Caesar Scaliger (1484–1558), the knowledge of forms is crude and confused; “Et ubi non concessa nobis cognitio Distincta, contenti simus Confusâ”, and where distinct knowledge is not granted us, let us be content with confused.S 63 Goudin answers Descartes’s claim that extension, figure and motion are clearer principles than the Peripatetics’ obscure matter and form. Principles of reasoning must be clear and certain; principles of the constitution of things are another matter:

“and these are plainly not clear to us, but hidden and obscure, and are known only with great labour, and found by few”G 36

& hæc planè non sunt nobis clara, sed occulta, & obscura, nec nisi magno labore innotescunt, & à paucis inueniri

A way of philosophizing that offers for the principles of things what everyone knows and finds easiest is suspect for that very reason.G 36

Why they differed

What an explanation must give. The Cartesians judge a principle by what can be drawn from it. Rohault: a physics resting on an obscure principle cannot explain the least of nature’s effects.R 44 Le Grand: substantial forms are useless for explaining any effect, and size, figure, situation, motion and rest explain them better.L 180L 182 The schoolmen ask of a principle what a thing is and why it does what it does. Sperling: the form makes the thing not idle but busy, and as its being is, so is its operation.S 75 Goudin: the form is the root of the properties that follow a thing’s essence, and the reason they come back when interrupted.G 89

Descartes as Epicurus. For Goudin the new principles are old ones. Descartes’s particles, shaped and moved, differ from the Epicureans’ only in words, the Catholic doctors rejected the doctrine of Epicurus (341–270 BC) with one voice, and Augustine of Hippo (354–430) called him a raving philosopher, a lover of vanity rather than of wisdom; and life, sense and the passions of beasts cannot come from particles of quantity however they are shaped and moved.G 29G 33

“particles of quantity varied by division, figure and local motion cannot be the principles of natural things”G 29

particulæ quantitatis secundùm diuisionem, figuram, & localem motum variatæ non possunt esse rerum naturalium principia

The faith as a touchstone. Goudin answers the Cartesians who say that matters of philosophy are not to be proved from mysteries reason cannot grasp: the mysteries of faith are most true, so whatever follows from them about natural things is true too, and

“by no surer rule can we test our natural knowledge than by examining it against the truths of faith, as against a touchstone”G 32

ad nullam certiorem regulam possumus naturales cognitiones probare quam eas ad fidei veritates, tanquàm ad lydium lapidem examinando

as sailors correct their courses by the stars.G 32 Sperling admits Scripture, as Goudin admits the faith, but thinks little of anything else, and mocks the use of theology to prove first matter: “Physicus enim naturam & Scripturam admittit, cætera parvi æstimat”, the physicist admits nature and Scripture and thinks little of the rest; Scripture says nothing of first matter, the appeal to the Fathers and schoolmen is vain, and vainer still Pedro Hurtado de Mendoza’s (1578–1641) attempt to show it from the body of Christ in the three days of his death or from the relics of the saints.S 57S 59 Rohault sets reason against authority, and is ready to leave Aristotle where reason requires it.R 43

Two kinds of reform. Sperling and the Cartesians agree that the schoolmen’s first matter is a fiction, and their reasons differ. For Le Grand and Rohault it cannot be conceived, since matter is extension.L 165R 43 For Sperling it is not needed, since everything is generated from seeds, and God gave things at the creation the power to multiply themselves; he keeps substantial forms, and has them come neither out of matter nor back into it.S 55S 89S 105S 117

Questions the texts left open

  • Whether one body has more than one form. Sperling: a compound matter holds now one form, now several, as it is more or less compounded; “Sic hominis materia quot quæso includit formas?”, how many forms, I ask, does the matter of man include? They all serve the rational soul and take on the character of matter.S 49S 50 Rohault: the soul is the form of the human body as human, but not of the body as a body, nor of each part: each part has its own form, which stays while the part lasts, even after death, for flesh is still flesh and bone still bone.R 142R 143 Goudin and Le Grand do not take the question up in the parts read.
  • Whether a form is divided with its body. Sperling: material forms are extended and divided with their matter, whole in the whole but not whole in every part. Cut a worm or a wasp in two and both halves live; grafts take; a surgeon in Sicily restored a nose from the flesh of the arm. Only the rational soul, a spirit, has no extension.S 123S 127

Terms, for modern readers

In the textsNearest term today
First matter (materia prima); second matterNo modern counterpart. Second matter is a body already formed that serves as the material of another, such as a seed
Substantial formNo modern counterpart: the principle that makes a thing the kind of thing it is, and the source of its powers
Eduction; traductionTwo schoolmen’s accounts of how a new form arises: drawn out of the matter’s potency, or passed on from the parent
PrivationThe lack of a form in something able to have it
Real accident; modeA quality as a being of its own, against a quality as only a state or arrangement of the body
Intelligible forms (size, figure, situation, motion, rest)Roughly, the mechanical properties of bodies and their parts
Clear and distinctDescartes’s test of truth: known so plainly that the mind cannot doubt it
