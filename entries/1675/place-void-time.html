Snapshot c. 1675 · Natural philosophy

Divisibility, place, void and time

What teaching texts printed at Paris, Wittenberg, Bologna and London between 1664 and 1686 said about the division of matter, place, the void and time. All four deny that nature holds an empty space, each for its own reason. They part on what a place is, whether division ever comes to an end, whether God could make a void, why water follows a piston, and what time is.

First cut, from one text per region, on divisibility and the infinite, penetration, place, the void, the suction pump, and time and duration. Rarefaction, the weight of the air and the size of the world are left to their own topics.

Where the subject sat among the sciences

All four treat these questions in the general physics, but they come to them from different places. For the two Cartesians they are corollaries of a single doctrine. Rohault, having made extension the essence of matter, draws from it at once that the void is impossible, what place is, how rarefaction happens and that the world is indefinite, then turns to divisibility as matter’s first property, before motion.R 45R 51 Le Grand puts divisibility straight after the nature of matter, and place and the void after rarefaction and weight.L 169L 195 Sperling’s second book treats the affections of the natural body: quantity, quality, place, time and motion. He strikes the infinite and the void off the list as non-beings, since a privation is no affection.S 277 Goudin follows the order of Aristotle’s last books of the Physics: time comes before place because it is the measure of motion, and place because motion is exercised in it, with the void, the infinite and the continuum as further conditions of motion.G 328 Only Le Grand and Sperling treat time for its own sake; Goudin treats it as the measure of motion, and Rohault does not treat it at all.

What the texts taught in common

  • There is no empty space in nature. Rohault: “le vuide des Philosophes est impossible”, the void of the philosophers is impossible, since with the Cartesians space, or extension, and matter are one thing, and to ask for a space without matter is to ask for matter without matter.R 45 Sperling: nature, a lover of unity, labours everywhere to fill the void, for even the least void left unfilled would take away the unity of the world, and “fingere vacuum cerebri est vacui, & ingenii inanis”, to feign a void is the mark of an empty brain and a hollow wit.S 277S 358 Goudin: “Naturalitèr dari non potest vacuum”, naturally there can be no void, as all the Peripatetics hold against some who trust in empty experiments; natural agents abhor it as a schism that would break the union of things.G 345G 346 Le Grand: a void in the universe implies a contradiction, since every space involves extension and extension belongs only to body; one can no more conceive a space without matter than a mountain without a valley.L 199 The reasons differ, and that difference is taken up below.
  • Two bodies cannot be in one place by nature. Rohault calls impenetrability the property of matter “de laquelle tout le monde convient”, which everyone grants (from the machine-read text).R 76 Sperling: bodies have length, breadth and depth and their dimensions exclude each other, so Peter cannot go through a wall; light, sound and the soul pass freely because they are not bodies.S 285 Goudin: two bodies cannot naturally penetrate each other, “possunt tamen divinitùs”, though they can by God’s power, which all hold against Durandus of Saint-Pourçain (c. 1275–1334).G 335 Le Grand: to be extended is to have parts outside parts, so a foot added to a foot makes two feet.L 171 Only Goudin raises God’s power here.
  • What seems empty, or seems to be penetrated, is filled with a subtler body. A vessel full of ash takes nearly as much water as an empty one: for Sperling because the subtle gives way to the gross, for Goudin because the water drives out the air in the gaps.S 287G 336 Goudin fills the pores of all mixed bodies with their subtler parts, and denies any void even within them.G 348 Rohault explains rarefaction by a very subtle matter that enters a body and spreads its parts, as air fills the eyes of bread.R 47 Le Grand: “Vulgi error est, vacua credere, quæ corporibus repleri non videntur”, it is a popular error to believe empty what is not seen to be filled with bodies.L 200

The disputes

What a place is

Goudin keeps Aristotle’s definition: “Locus est superficies concava corporis ambientis”, place is the concave surface of the surrounding body. It contains the located body, is filled by it, and, if it is the surface of a friendly body, preserves it; immobility is not of its essence but a kind of property.G 330 He rejects the view that place is the space filled by the body, taken as something real, as Epicurus (341–270 BC) and Democritus (c. 460–c. 370 BC) made all things of the full and the void, and as René Descartes (1596–1650) made such space a true body.G 328G 329

Sperling takes that very view. The surface fails every condition of place: it has two dimensions where the body has three; it moves in the wind round a tower that stands still; it perishes when the air flows away.S 345S 348 The commentators’ replies are evasions, and Diego Mas OP (c. 1553–1608) says he would gladly take the interval for place if Aristotle and Thomas did not compel him. Sperling’s comment: “Hoc scilicet est pro veritate disputare”, this, no doubt, is disputing for the truth.S 355 Place is the space God created with the world and equal to it: “Spatio omnes veræ Loci conditiones competunt”, all the true conditions of place belong to space.S 342S 355

The Cartesians deny that the space differs from the body. Rohault: “le lieu interieur, ou l’espace que chaque corps occupe, n’est point different de ce corps”, the internal place, the space each body occupies, is not different from that body; when a body changes place, it is the external place that changes, the surface of the bodies around it.R 46 Le Grand: place, properly speaking, is a relation of the body’s situation among other bodies. To think place a space existing before the body comes and after it goes was “born of a popular error”, the belief that empty spaces were given before there were bodies; a space distinct from the body would have to penetrate it.L 195L 196

“it is plain that this was born of a popular error”L 195

illud ex populari errore natum esse constat

Whether there is anything beyond the world

Goudin: when we measure the spaces said to lie beyond the heavens, it does not follow that they are anything real, “sed solùm quamdam carentiam quantitatis corporeæ”, but only a lack of bodily quantity, which God could fill. They are called imaginary because the imagination takes negations for beings, as it does death, shadows and night, and as it imagines time flowing before the creation.G 329G 330 Le Grand: whatever is extended in length, breadth and depth, with parts at distances, “non imaginarium, sed verum & reale corpus dici debeat”, must be called not an imaginary but a true and real body.L 196 Rohault draws the same conclusion: whatever bounds we set to the world we imagine extension beyond them, so the world is indefinite, and there cannot be several worlds (from the machine-read text).R 49 For Sperling there is nothing beyond: the space of place was created with the world and is equal to it (from the machine-read text).S 342

Whether God could make a void

Rohault will not answer whether God could annihilate the air of a room and let no other in, since it is not for us to say how far God’s power reaches. He answers only what we conceive would follow: “nous dirions que les murailles s’approcheroient, en sorte qu’il ne resteroit plus entre-elles aucun espace”, we should say that the walls would come together, so that no space would remain between them. The walls exist independently of what they hold, but their being a room depends on the extension between them.R 45R 46 Le Grand says the same and is equally careful: “Absit ut Omnipotentiæ Divinæ aliquid denegare audeam”, far be it from me to dare deny anything to the divine omnipotence; he asserts only that God made his mind such that he cannot conceive a mountain without a valley, or one and five not making six.L 199

Goudin names Descartes and contradicts him. No angel can make a void, since nature’s inclination against it is stronger than its readiness to obey an angel; but God can, for a void implies no contradiction, and is only forbidden by a law of providence for the good of the whole. If the walls must meet because things cannot be apart with nothing between, let God destroy the universe and keep one man: there would be a distance between his hands with no body in it.G 348G 349 Sperling does not ask the question.

“a void can be given by divine power”G 349

diuinitùs dari potest vacuum

How bodies move in a full world

Rohault: since the world is full, a body moving straight pushes another and that one another, but not to infinity; some turn aside into the place the first has left, so that whenever a body moves, matter moves in a ring. For want of seeing this, philosophers brought in attraction, sympathy and the fear of the void (from the machine-read text).R 76 Goudin answers the objection that all is full with Aristotle: some bodies are fluid, as air, and give place to the more solid, the parts in front taking the place left behind.G 348 Sperling turns the argument round. Whoever grants motion must grant a void in his sense, a space: a falling stone descends through space, not through the air, or body would pass into body. He quotes Julius Caesar Scaliger (1484–1558): “Idemq; esse vacuum & locum: neq; differre nisi nomine”, the void and place are the same and differ only in name. It is not the void of the ancients, empty of body, but the space in which body is.S 356 Le Grand’s articles on motion were not read.

Why water follows a drawn piston

Sperling and Goudin give the old answer. Sperling: nature foresees the void from afar and fills it. Nothing runs from a full cask holed below until a hole is made above; beer rises through a pipe as the air in it is drawn out, though it is heavy; stopped bellows cannot be opened; cupping glasses raise the flesh.S 358S 359 Goudin: all bodies renounce their own inclination to prevent a void, and nature fills a deserted place by motion, by breaking, or by rarefying bodies into vapour.G 346G 347

Goudin knows the moderns’ answer and rejects it. Some put these effects down to the weight of the air; but even if the whole mass of air were heavy its parts would not weigh on the parts of water, which bear each other up, as divers at the bottom of the sea feel no weight, and weight cannot make flesh swell in a cupping glass. As for the tube full of mercury, the mercury does not fall all at once, as is told on trust; as each particle drops into the water below, water rises to take its place, “sicuti proprijs oculis exploravimus”, as we have examined with our own eyes.G 346G 347

Rohault makes the same case into his lesson in reasoning. The ancients said the water rises so that there may be no void, then from fear of the void, then from nature’s horror of it, as if nature could feel horror. At best this gives a final cause where an efficient cause was asked, like saying that wood comes to Paris from fear of the cold; and it does not even agree with the fact, for a suction pump raises water only thirty-one and a half feet. He then shows, from a syringe held in the air, that the gross air is heavy and moves the water by impulse (from the machine-read text).R 78R 79R 82 Le Grand’s article on the motions referred to the flight of the void was not read.

Whether division comes to an end

Three of the four say it does not. Rohault: since matter is nothing but extended substance and alike everywhere, it can be divided at every point the mind can assign, and geometry shows that the points assignable in any quantity are indefinite in number. God could make parts that no agent could divide, which would be Epicurus’s atoms, but that would be an arbitrary supposition that does not change their nature: “nous devons tenir pour certain que toute la matiere de ce monde est divisible”, we must hold it for certain that all the matter of this world is divisible.R 51R 53 Le Grand: “materiam, sive Corpus, non componi ex indivisibilibus”, matter, or body, is not composed of indivisibles, for a thing cannot be understood to be extended and indivisible; atoms too fine for any creature to divide may exist, but God has not deprived himself of the power to divide them.L 169 Goudin opens his question by calling it “Mysterium philosophicum”, a philosophical mystery in which reason proves more than it can understand. Against Zeno of Elea (c. 490–c. 430 BC) and some moderns who leave Aristotle (384–322 BC), the continuum is not made of indivisibles but of parts always divisible, since an indivisible with no extension, added to another, makes nothing larger.G 351G 352

“The continuum is not made of indivisibles alone, but of parts always divisible.”G 351

Continuum non constat solis indiuisibilibus, sed partibus semper diuisibilibus.

Sperling denies it. The question is the most vexed in the schools, and he answers it in a syllogism: what is finite cannot be divided to infinity; a quantity is finite; so it cannot. The physicist asks what nature does, not what minds can do: “frangatur lapis centies, millies”, let a stone be broken a hundred, a thousand times, and of each particle ask whether it is least; if not, divide again, “& tandem quiescere jubebit Natura”, and at last nature will bid it stop.S 282S 283 Nature has its greatest body and its least, which Martin Weinrich (1548–1609) calls atoms, and each species its least size.S 280

Whether a continuum has real indivisibles

Goudin: there are real indivisibles in the continuum, both bounding and joining its parts, though they do not compose it (from the machine-read text); in time, as in every continuum, there are parts and indivisibles, instants in time as points in a line.G 363G 316 Le Grand: nothing forces us to indivisibles, and beings are not to be multiplied without need; an extended thing is bounded by the mere denial of further extension, and the parts of matter are joined of themselves without a third thing to join them.L 169 Rohault does not take up the question, and Sperling dismisses the schools’ noise about points, lines and surfaces as physicists speaking unphysically.S 282

Whether anything created is without limit

The schoolmen say no. Goudin: only God is infinite in act; “Nulla Creatura actualitèr est infinita actu, & categorematicè”, no creature is actually and categorematically infinite, for God made all things in number, weight and measure, and every creature is bounded by its genus and difference. Quantity is infinite only in potency, since God can always add more, and that potency can be brought to act only successively.G 288G 289G 290 Sperling: “infinitum in Physicis purum putum non ens est”, the infinite in physics is a pure and simple non-being.S 277 The Cartesians put another word in its place. Le Grand: “solus Deus est infinitus”, God alone is infinite; matter, which after any division is still divisible, is not infinite but indefinite.L 170 Rohault speaks of division and of the world as indefinite, and holds that equal and unequal are properties of finite things, which the mind can embrace, and do not apply to indefinite magnitudes (from the machine-read text).R 55

What time is

Goudin keeps Aristotle: “Tempus rectè definitur: numerus, seu mensura motus secundùm prius, & posterius”, time is rightly defined as the number, or measure, of motion according to before and after.G 313 Sperling and Le Grand reject the definition for opposite reasons. Sperling: it gives words, not the thing, “Ast verba audimus, rem nondum habemus”, and whoever hears it still does not know what time is; time is an affection of the body and must be defined by the body, not by motion.S 368 His own definition makes time a real affection of the natural body, by which its duration, undivided in itself, is divided according to the motions of the heavens, as Peter’s life is divided into years, months, days and hours by the sun and moon; it is a real being, though its parts exist one after another, as do those of motion and rivers.S 361S 366 Le Grand calls the definition “manca”, defective, because time measures rest as well as motion: a dog that runs three miles in half an hour and a tortoise that goes one pace have the same measure of time.L 232 For him time is only the duration of a created thing, and duration is not really distinct from the thing’s existence, so that time is “merum cogitandi modum”, a mere mode of thinking, a being of reason. He cites Lucretius (c. 99–c. 55 BC), and Pierre Gassendi (1592–1655) on Epicurus.L 232L 233

“it follows that time is not a property of created things but a mere mode of thinking”L 233

Ex quo sequitur Tempus non esse rerum creatarum affectionem, sed merum cogitandi modum

Whether the present is only an instant

Goudin: “Tempus non est præsens, & existens ratione partis, sed solùm ratione instantis”, time is not present by reason of a part, but only by reason of the instant, as Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) and Aristotle say; Durandus and some moderns hold the contrary.G 316 Sperling is one of them. The schools commonly deny that the present time is true time, but without sense: motion happens neither in the past nor in the future, so it happens in the present, “Ergò præsens tempus verum tempus est”, therefore the present time is true time, and has its breadth.S 366 Le Grand reaches the same answer from Descartes’s matter: no moment can be grasped that has no parts, and as a particle of matter can be divided into infinitely many others, so the present time is made of parts and is indefinitely divisible.L 233

By what time is measured

Goudin: every motion has its own time, but there is one universal time by which all are measured, the revolution of the first movable, completed in twenty-four hours.G 314 Sperling divides duration by the sun and moon, which God made to make seasons, days and years; clocks stand in for them, a clock being an artifice that imitates the motion of the sun.S 361S 363 The first movable is no help: “Figmentum hoc primum mobile est”, this first movable is a figment, and a figment can have no affection.S 368 Le Grand: there is succession in every created thing, and we measure it by motion properly so called, or at least by the thoughts of our own mind, which do not exist together.L 232

Why they differed

Three reasons for a full world. The four agree that nature has no void, but not why, and the reason decides what God can do. For the Cartesians the void is a contradiction, because space is extension and extension is body; so they cannot say what God could do except that they cannot conceive it, and the walls of an emptied room would touch.R 45L 199 For Goudin the void is forbidden by a law of providence, made for the common good of the universe, which lies in the union of things; a law God set he can suspend.G 346G 349 For Sperling nature loves unity and fills every space, but the space itself is real and distinct from the body that fills it.S 356S 358 The same Cartesian premise gives Rohault and Le Grand their place, their indefinite world and their divisible matter; they draw all three from it in so many words.R 46R 51L 196L 169

Geometry and nature. Rohault and Le Grand prove that matter is divisible without end by the same piece of geometry, the side and diagonal of a square, which have no common measure. Both even divide the side in the same way: Rohault into a hundred thousand parts and each of those into a hundred thousand more, for a whole century; Le Grand into a thousand and a thousand, for a hundred years.R 54L 170 Sperling will not let geometry decide: if Aristotle’s magnitude divisible to infinity is a mathematical magnitude, those are principles foreign to physics, and by Aristotle’s own rule one may not conclude from mathematical principles to natural things.S 284 The physicist breaks the stone.S 283

The imagination. Each side charges the other with trusting the imagination. Rohault calls it “une erreur fort grossiere & indigne d’un Philosophe”, a very gross error and unworthy of a philosopher, to call a thing absurd because the imagination cannot grasp it, and shows from the goldbeaters and wire-drawers of Paris how far gold is in fact divided.R 56R 58 Goudin calls the spaces beyond the world imaginary, the imagination measuring a lack as if it were something.G 329 Le Grand calls the space distinct from body a popular error.L 195

Aristotle’s definitions. Goudin defines place and time in Aristotle’s words and says so.G 313G 330 Sperling, a Lutheran Aristotelian, rejects both, and mocks a Dominican who kept the surface only because Aristotle and Thomas said it.S 355S 368 Le Grand rejects the definition of time because it leaves out rest.L 232 The two who reject Aristotle on time then disagree with each other: Sperling makes time a real affection of bodies, Le Grand a being of reason.

Experiment. Goudin and Rohault appeal to the same kind of evidence and read it oppositely. Goudin says he watched the mercury fall from the tube drop by drop, with water rising in its place, and draws from it how little the experiments brought in for new opinions are to be trusted.G 347G 348 Rohault cites the pump that stops at thirty-one and a half feet as the fact the fear of the void cannot explain.R 79

Questions the texts left open

  • The kinds of duration. Sperling and Goudin give three: eternity, God’s, without beginning or end; aevum, that of created spirits; time, that of bodies.S 362G 311 Goudin adds that the succession of an angel’s thoughts makes a spiritual time, not continuous but discrete.G 316 Le Grand gives only God a duration that is all at once: in the thoughts of an angel or of a man’s mind we clearly see succession.L 232L 233
  • Motion in a void. Goudin: if there were a void, motion could happen in it, “non tamen in instanti”, though not in an instant, since the body would keep its own place and its distances.G 349 The others, who deny the void, do not ask.
  • How far gold can be divided. Rohault weighs it: an ounce of gold beaten into 2,730 leaves, and the gold on drawn silver wire, which divides the height of an ounce cube into 651,590 parts, and nature has agents far finer than men’s tools (pp. 57–59 from the machine-read text).R 56R 59
  • Whether bodies act by their size. Sperling: not by quantity but by quality; fire heats by its virtue, not its bulk, and the magnet draws iron by a peculiar quality, not by its length or breadth (from the machine-read text).S 287

Terms, for modern readers

In the textsNearest term today
Void (vacuum); plenumA space with no body in it at all; a world entirely full. Not the same as a modern vacuum, which may hold fields and radiation
Internal and external placeThe space a body takes up, and the surface of the bodies around it
Imaginary spacesThe schoolmen’s name for the seeming extension beyond the outermost heaven
Indefinite; infinite in act; infinite in potency (syncategorematic)Descartes’s word for what has no limit we can find, keeping “infinite” for God; a completed infinite; an endless process, as in counting
Fear, or horror, of the voidExplained today by the pressure of the atmosphere, which can hold up a column of water about 10 metres (32 feet) high
The tube full of mercuryTorricelli’s experiment of 1643, the first barometer
First movable (primum mobile)The outermost sphere of the old astronomy, whose daily turn carried the heavens round
AevumThe schoolmen’s middle duration, between eternity and time, of angels and souls
Subtle matterThe Cartesians’ fine matter filling the pores of bodies and the heavens; no modern counterpart
