Snapshot c. 1675 · Natural philosophy

Motion and its communication

What teaching texts printed at Paris, Wittenberg, Bologna and London taught about what motion is, what moves a body and keeps it moving, how one body passes motion to another, and, in the two Cartesian courses, how a body rebounds and bends.

First cut, from one text per region. Reflection and refraction belong to this topic only in the two Cartesian courses, which treat them as applications of the laws of motion.

The four texts

  • RParis · FrenchJacques Rohault (1618–1672), Traité de physique, third edition (Paris, 1675), vol. 1. Part I, chapters X–XI (motion and rest; the continuation and stopping of motion) and XIII–XV (determination; the composition of motions; reflection and refraction), pp. 60–132.
  • SWittenberg · LatinJohann Sperling (1603–1658), Institutiones physicae (Wittenberg, 1664). Book II, chapter VII, “De Motu et Quiete”: precepts, explanations and six questions, pp. 370–398.
  • GBologna · LatinAntoine Goudin OP (1639–1695), Philosophia juxta inconcussa tutissimaque Divi Thomae dogmata, vol. 2 (Bologna, 1686). Physics, first part, disputation III: question I, on motion, action and passion, pp. 260–297, and question VI, on the conditions of motion, pp. 366–378; with the article on violence in disputation II, p. 141.
  • LLondon · LatinAntoine Le Grand (1629–1699), Institutio philosophiae secundum principia D. Renati Descartes (London, 1675). Part IV, general physics, articles XVI–XVIII and XX–XXII, pp. 202–224; Part V, articles V–VI, on God as the cause of motion and the communication of motions, pp. 248–258.

The texts are the most-reprinted teaching course printed in each region in 1660–1690, chosen by the project’s stated rule (see the year page). Rohault and Le Grand teach the physics of René Descartes (1596–1650); Sperling is a Lutheran professor at Wittenberg who quarrels with the Aristotelian schools; Goudin is a Dominican who follows Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274).

Goudin’s course is the Catholic German text, from its Cologne printing of 1681, but that printing’s second volume, which holds the physics of motion, has not been found. The Bologna printing of 1686 is read in its place, under the rule’s clause for the edition read.

Where the subject sat among the sciences

All four treat motion in the general physics, but in different company. Rohault comes to it straight after divisibility: since every real division of matter supposes motion, he says, Aristotle (384–322 BC) was right that whoever does not know motion must be ignorant of all natural things.R 60 Sperling puts motion and rest last among the affections of the natural body, after quantity, quality, place and time.S 370 Goudin follows the order of the last books of Aristotle’s of the Physics: motion, with action and passion, first, then the infinite, time, place and the continuum as its conditions, and the conditions of the mover last; he leaves violence to his disputation on nature and art.G 260G 141 Le Grand takes motion after the causes, and moves the conservation of motion and the rules of impact into his cosmology, just before the systems of the world.L 202L 252

Only the two Cartesians treat reflection and refraction here, before and apart from light, as cases of a body whose direction is changed by another body or by a new medium; Rohault calls them a use of what he has just established.R 121L 216 Sperling and Goudin have neither in their sections on motion.

What the texts taught in common

  • God is the first mover. Rohault: as God is the creator of matter, so he is its first mover; since motion is no essential property of matter, there is no use asking how it was first produced.R 68 Sperling: the first mover is God, the second nature; when a man moves, God and his soul concur.S 379 Goudin: there must be a first unmoved mover, God, who as pure act is in no potency to be moved; lower bodies are moved by higher, the higher by the intelligences, the intelligences by God.G 367G 371 Le Grand: the general principle of all the motions in the world can be nothing but God.L 204 The two Cartesians add that God keeps in the world the same quantity of motion he put there at the creation, so that what one body loses another gains; Le Grand compares it to the sum of money in a kingdom.R 69L 205L 249L 250 Rohault will not make God the cause of each motion: “ce ne seroit pas philosopher que de luy faire faire à tous momens des miracles”, it would not be philosophising to make him work miracles at every moment.R 68 Sperling and Goudin do not ask whether the quantity of motion stays the same.
  • Nothing moves a body without reaching it. Rohault: impulsion is the only way we clearly conceive one body moving another, and attraction, sympathy, antipathy and the fear of the void, “qui au fonds ne sont que des chymeres”, which at bottom are nothing but chimeras, explain nothing.R 76 Sperling: union between mover and moved is necessary, so the hand cannot move a stone it no longer touches.S 393 Goudin: “Non datur actio indistans”, there is no action at a distance; the mover must be joined to the moved, by itself or by its virtue spread through the medium.G 372 Le Grand does not raise the question in these articles; he has his own account of how motion passes, below.
  • In a full world a moving body sets a ring of matter moving. Rohault and Le Grand, who deny the void, answer how anything can move at all: a body pushes another, and that one a third, until one of them turns into the place the first has left, so that every motion is in a circle.R 76L 203 Both reckon the quantity of motion by the amount of matter moved and the length of its line, so that a body twice as large moving half as fast has as much motion; Rohault makes this the reason of the balance and the lever.R 64R 65L 223 Sperling and Goudin do not take these up.
  • The direction of a motion is distinct from the motion. Rohault calls it the determination: a racket’s force gives the ball its motion, its slant the ball’s direction, and a stone thrown sideways reaches the ground no sooner than one dropped.R 110R 111 Le Grand: the determination can be taken away while the motion stays whole, as when a body is stopped and goes back with all its motion.L 213L 214 From this both draw that a rebounding body never rests at the point of reflection, “comme quelques Philosophes se l’imaginent”, as some philosophers imagine, since the reflected motion only continues the direct one.R 114L 215 Sperling and Goudin do not take these up.
  • Reflection keeps one part of the determination, refraction changes it. A ball striking a hard surface obliquely keeps the part of its determination along the surface and loses the part towards it, so it rebounds at an angle equal to the angle of incidence.R 123R 125L 217 A ball passing obliquely from air into water, which resists it more, bends away from the perpendicular; into a medium that resists less, towards it; struck too obliquely, it does not enter but skips, like the stones boys throw and the cannon balls that bounce off the sea in sea fights.R 127R 132L 219L 221 Rohault calls the cause of refraction “une des choses que l’Antiquité a ignorées, & dont la découverte est deuë à l’un des premiers hommes de ce siecle”, one of the things antiquity did not know, whose discovery is owed to one of the foremost men of this century, without naming Descartes; Le Grand names him and follows his construction in the second chapter of the Dioptrique.R 126L 217 Le Grand adds that the angles are unequal when the ball’s speed changes at the surface, below. Sperling and Goudin do not take these up.

The disputes

What motion is

Goudin keeps Aristotle’s definition: “Motus est actus entis in potentia prout in potentia”, motion is the act of a being in potency, as in potency. It looks a little obscure at first sight, he grants, but Thomas makes it clear: water has a potency to being hot and a potency to becoming hot; the becoming, the gradual acquiring of the heat, is the motion, an act because it actualises the second potency, of a being still in potency to the heat it has not yet got. More simply, motion is the actual tending of the mobile to its term. Motion, action and passion are one entity, distinguished only in their relations.G 261G 262G 263

Sperling defines motion by place: “Motus est affectio disjuncta corporis naturalis, qua illud alium atque alium acquirit locum”, motion is a disjunct affection of the natural body, by which it acquires one place after another, as Peter moves when he goes from place to place.S 370 The schools’ definition and their division of motion into three, four or six kinds “partim obscura, partim falsa, partim aliena sunt”, are partly obscure, partly false, partly beside the point; Jean Bodin (1530–1596) was right that the definition is more obscure than motion itself, and no motion exists in which part of a form is present and part absent.S 378

The Cartesians define motion by neighbours. Rohault reaches his definition by elimination, from a man walking down an avenue: his desire to walk is a thought, his effort at most the cause of the motion, so what is left is “cette application successive d’un corps, aux diverses parties de ceux qui l’avoisinent immediatement”, the successive application of a body to the different parts of those that immediately surround it.R 62 Le Grand gives Descartes’s wording, unnamed: motion is the transfer of one part of matter from the neighbourhood of the bodies that immediately touch it, and are regarded as at rest, into the neighbourhood of others. Both make motion a manner of being of the body, not a thing.R 64L 203 Le Grand finds no other definition intelligible: to call motion the act of a being in potency as in potency is to call it the act of what is not in act, as not in act, “quod certe magnam obscuritatem parit, si non implicantiam”, which surely breeds great obscurity, if not a contradiction.L 204

Which changes are motions

For Goudin, motion runs between two positive terms with a range between them, as a ship sails from France to America. So there is motion in quantity, quality and place, but none to substance: forms have no range between them, and a new form comes in an instant. Even nourishment changes food into flesh in an instant, after a gradual alteration.G 274G 275G 277 Sperling and Le Grand keep local motion only, for different reasons. Sperling: local motion and rest between them cover everything a natural body can be, and growth, alteration and coming to be belong to the particular kinds of body, not to the general physics.S 372 Le Grand: quantity is not distinct from extended matter, which is substance, and the real qualities have been thrown out, so change of place is all that is left.L 202 Rohault treats only local motion without raising the question.

Whether whatever is moved is moved by another

Goudin: “Omne quod mouetur, ab alio mouetur. Ita D. Tho. & Aristoteles, immò tota antiquitas”, whatever is moved is moved by another, as Thomas and Aristotle say, indeed all antiquity, except the Epicureans, who made their atoms move themselves. Nothing can be in potency and in act in the same respect at once. The axiom is the ladder by which Aristotle rose to the knowledge of one God, and those who deny God’s premotion usually deny it too.G 367 Animals move themselves only part by part, the heart moving the limbs; and a falling stone, “non mouet se ipsum, dum cadit, sed hoc habet à generante”, does not move itself when it falls, but has this from what generated it, which gave it the property as an appendage of its nature.G 371

Sperling turns the canon round. In violent motion whatever is moved is moved by another; but “Motu naturali, quicquid movetur, à se ipso movetur”, in natural motion whatever is moved is moved by itself, since its principle is internal: rising fire, falling stones and a walking horse move themselves.S 386 The defenders of the canon, he says, hold it because Aristotle taught it. Francisco de Toledo SJ (1532–1596) himself calls it only most probable, and the soul that Jacopo Zabarella (1533–1589) has pass into new flesh without moving is an evasion, as Sébastien Basson (c. 1573–after 1625) showed.S 380S 383

Rohault agrees with Goudin only in this: nothing at rest “ne commencera jamais à se mouvoir, si quelque chose ne le meut”, will ever begin to move unless something moves it.R 70 Le Grand goes further than Goudin: no body can move itself, and none can move another, since the force that moves is distinct from the body moved. The movers are God and created minds, such as the human mind, to which God has given a power to move bodies.L 207L 208

Natural and violent motion

Sperling divides motion, by its mover, into natural, from an internal cause, as the elements go to their natural places, and violent, from an external cause, as arrows and stones go up; violent motion is pushing, pulling, carrying or whirling.S 370S 374 Goudin defines violence as an outside principle the patient does not cooperate with but resists, as a stone pulled upward resists by its weight.G 141 Le Grand denies the division: “nullum motum in Natura esse violentum”, no motion in nature is violent, for it is as natural to bodies to push one another, when it happens, as to rest; the word refers only to a will that suffers what it struggles against.L 204 Rohault does not take up the division.

Why a thrown stone keeps moving

The Cartesians need no mover at all. Rohault: nothing tends of itself to its own destruction, and things stay in their state unless an outside cause changes them, so what has once begun to move cannot of itself stop: “voilà la veritable raison pourquoy une pierre continuë de se mouvoir”, that is the true reason why a stone keeps moving after it leaves the hand.R 70 Aristotle’s saying that whatever moves tends to rest is an error. The heavens have shown no stopping in thousands of years, and nobody thinks a cannon ball wants to rest after piercing three or four feet of masonry; bodies stop because the bodies they meet, the air among them, take their motion.R 70R 71 Le Grand gives it as the first law of nature: “omne illud quod movetur semper moveri, & illud quod quiescit, semper quiescere”, whatever moves always moves, and whatever rests always rests. A stone thrown up falls back only because the air resists and the subtle matter round the earth thrusts it down; otherwise a man who jumped would go on rising.L 205L 206

Sperling keeps a mover, and finds it in the air. The hand cannot be the cause, since it no longer touches the stone; of an impressed virtue “nec quid ea virtus sit, nec quomodo imprimatur, constat”, it is clear neither what it is nor how it is impressed. Julius Caesar Scaliger (1484–1558), who as a boy had told Girolamo Cardano (1501–1576) that the bowstring’s force stays in the arrow, admitted these were words.S 393 Nor does the air give way and the stone follow to prevent a void, which would drive a very heavy thing through the air against its nature. Of those who make the air push, “soli rem tetigisse videntur acu”, they alone seem to have hit the nail on the head: the stone splits the air, whose parts rush together again to restore their union and drive it on, more weakly each time, which is why violent motion is fastest at the start.S 395S 396 Goudin does not treat thrown bodies in the parts read.

What moves a falling body, and why it speeds up

For Sperling a falling stone moves itself, as the elements go to their natural places, the centre of the world.S 370S 375 For Goudin it has its motion from what generated it.G 371 For Le Grand, heaviness and impressed qualities explain nothing unless one can say how they act; the subtle matter round the earth thrusts bodies down.L 206L 208 Rohault does not take up the cause of heaviness in these chapters.

Only Sperling asks why a falling body speeds up. Simplicius (c. 490–c. 560) and John Philoponus (c. 490–c. 570) asked how the fact was ever found out, and Martin Weinrich (1548–1609) denied it, since the stone weighs the same at every point; but the senses settle the fact, for a stone dropped from a roof hurts the hand more than one dropped from a table.S 389 The weight explains why the stone goes down, not why it goes faster, and the cause is the one Benito Pereira SJ (1535–1610) gave: “Hanc cum Pererio & aliis dicimus esse diversitatem medii”, with Pereira and others we say that this is the difference of the medium. The air at rest resists at the start, and then, set moving by the stone, helps it on.S 390S 391 Rohault and Le Grand say only that experience shows heavy bodies falling more slowly at the start than later.R 119L 216

How motion passes from one body to another

For Rohault a striking body gives part of its own motion to the other and loses as much; impulsion is the only way of moving a body we clearly conceive, and it follows from the impenetrability of matter.R 71R 76 For Sperling the air pushes the thrown stone because it touches it.S 393 Goudin: the mover must be joined to the moved, and the action, being one with the motion, is received in the moved, not in the mover.G 268G 372

Le Grand denies that anything passes. Motion is a mode, and a mode cannot leave its subject. When a hard body is said to move another, it gives it no real quality; it only determines the universal cause, God, to apply his power to the other body, which he would not otherwise have done, so that motion is as it were created anew by God at every moment.L 207L 250

What happens when bodies collide

Rohault gives no rules of Descartes’s kind. A moving body that strikes a body at rest and pushes it must lose as much motion as it gives, so that the two go on together at one speed, “de mesme que si ces deux corps ne composoient plus qu’une seule masse”, just as if they made up a single mass: a body three times the other loses a quarter of its motion. He works it in degrees: twelve degrees shared with a body half as large become eight and four. A body that cannot shake the body it meets keeps its speed and reflects.R 72R 73R 114

Le Grand prints Descartes’s seven rules, for perfectly hard bodies cut off from all others and moving in an empty or unresisting space. Two equal bodies at equal speeds both rebound; a resting body even slightly larger than the one that strikes it is never moved, “quacumque velocitate E moveatur versus A, nunquam ipsum A movebit”, with whatever speed the striker comes, since a resting body resists great speed more than small (the printer has swapped the letters); a smaller resting body is carried off with the share of motion that brings both to one speed, which Johannes de Raey (1622–1702) put in whole numbers; an exactly equal one takes a quarter of the motion and sends the striker back with three quarters.L 252L 253L 254L 255L 256L 257 Sperling and Goudin give no rules of impact.

What is contrary to a motion

Goudin takes the contrariety of motions from their terms: motions between contrary qualities, or between opposite places, are contrary, and a body cannot have contrary motions at once.G 294 The Cartesians allow no motion to be contrary to another. Le Grand: two equal bodies meeting at equal speeds lose none of their motion but rebound, so rest alone is contrary to motion, and a slow motion is opposed to a quick one only so far as slowness shares in rest; it is the determinations that are contrary.L 204L 214 Rohault: the reflected motion is only the continuation of the direct one, with no contrariety between them.R 114 Sperling opposes rest to motion but does not ask whether motions are contrary to each other.S 376

In what line bodies tend to move

Sperling has two simple motions: straight, along a line to or from the centre of the world, as fire rises and a stone falls, and circular, keeping the same distance from the centre, as the stars move; winds, clouds, dogs and men move in mixed or wandering ways.S 370S 371 The Cartesians have only the straight line. Rohault: the only determination natural to a moving body is a straight line, so a body moving in a circle is forced aside at every moment; set free, as when a sling’s string breaks, it flies off along the tangent, and bodies moving round tend always to go away from the centre, the more strongly the faster they go.R 111R 112R 113 Le Grand gives it as the second law of nature, with Descartes’s sling, and derives it from the definition of motion and from the immutability of God’s working.L 206L 207 Goudin does not ask.

Why they differed

What makes a definition clear. The quarrel over the definition is a quarrel over what a clear definition is. Le Grand rejects Aristotle’s because a thing cannot be act and potency at once, and holds that in physics nothing may be said to happen in a way the imagination cannot conceive.L 204L 208 Sperling rejects it as more obscure than the thing defined, with Bodin.S 378 Goudin grants that it looks obscure and answers that Thomas explains it, and that its terms are what make the axiom of the mover demonstrable: since to be moved is to be in potency and to move is to be in act, nothing moves itself.G 261G 367

Forms and the mover. Sperling and Goudin both keep forms, and draw opposite conclusions from them. For Sperling a form intends its motion, as a bird’s soul moves its body and a light thing tends upward, so the form moves itself and the body with it.S 387S 388 For Goudin the motion of heavy and light bodies, like the heartbeat, is a property following from the form, and so comes from whatever gave the form.G 371 The Cartesians, who have no forms, have nothing in a body that could move it. Rohault therefore puts all motion down to a quantity God gave at the creation and a law that keeps it.R 68R 69 Le Grand goes further and leaves God the only mover of bodies.L 207L 208

Two reasons for persistence. Both Cartesians ground the stone’s going on in the same two places: nothing tends of itself to its own destruction, and God works constantly and unchangeably.R 69R 70L 205L 206 Sperling allows neither, because violent motion needs an outside mover in contact, so the mover of a thrown stone has to be the air.S 393S 395 The same air then serves to explain why a falling body speeds up.S 390

Two Cartesian rule-books. Rohault and Le Grand agree on the conservation of motion and differ on what it implies for collisions. Rohault works from the conservation alone: what the striker gives, it loses, and the two go on as one mass.R 71R 72 Le Grand derives Descartes’s rules from the law that each thing stays in its state and from a balance of the force of acting against the force of resisting, which a resting body has in proportion to the speed that strikes it; he admits the rules hold only for perfectly hard bodies cut off from all others.L 222L 252L 254

Questions the texts left open

  • Whether anything is truly at rest. Rohault judges motion and rest by the bodies that touch a body, not by points imagined fixed beyond the heaven, where it is very uncertain that any matter is more immobile than near us: a log carried down a river between two waters is at rest, a fish holding its place against the current moves.R 63R 64 Sperling knows a rest that no motion ever preceded or will follow, the rest of the earth and the heaven.S 376
  • Unequal angles. Le Grand: “Non tamen necessum est, ut Angulus reflexionis semper sit æqualis angulo incidentiæ”, yet the angle of reflection need not always equal the angle of incidence. A ball that loses half its speed at the surface rebounds at a smaller angle, one that gains speed at a larger.L 218
  • Seeming actions at a distance. Goudin explains them all by virtue spread through the medium. The torpedo numbs the fisherman through the net and the line; the magnet moves iron through a board only after a delay, while its subtle virtue works through the pores; one lute string sets another trembling by a tremor of the air. As for the sympathetic powder said to cure the wounded at a distance, the experiments are uncertain, and if it works at all it works by spirits of vitriol and blood carried to the wound, as Kenelm Digby (1603–1665) notes. He doubts that it has any force.G 374G 377G 378
  • Why climbing tires a man. Sperling ends his chapter with the question and answers it partly from Cardano: the body is heavy and must be lifted step by step, and on a slope the foot cannot rest flat, so the muscles must hold the body up.S 397

Terms, for modern readers

In the textsNearest term today
Act of a being in potency (actus entis in potentia)Aristotle’s definition of change in the Physics, book III: the process by which something becomes what it could be
Local motion (latio)Change of place, the only kind of motion in modern mechanics
Impressed virtue, impetusThe medieval explanation of projectile motion by a force put into the thrown body; replaced by the principle of inertia
“What moves always moves”; the laws of natureDescartes’s first two laws (1644), forerunners of Newton’s first law of motion (1687)
Quantity of motionMass times speed, without regard to direction; close to modern momentum, which counts direction
DeterminationThe direction of a motion, kept separate from its quantity; in modern terms the direction of the velocity
Rules of impactDescartes’s rules for collisions (1644), most of them wrong for real bodies; the correct rules for elastic collisions were published by Huygens, Wren and Wallis in 1668–69
Subtle matterThe Cartesians’ fine matter filling all space, invoked here to push heavy bodies down; no modern counterpart
Refraction by unequal resistanceDescartes’s derivation (1637) of the law of refraction, now called Snell’s law (the sines of the angles in a constant ratio)
God applying his force at the impactThe view later called occasionalism: created bodies are not true causes of motion
Sympathetic powder (weapon-salve)A cure applied to the weapon or to the blood rather than to the wound, popularised by Kenelm Digby in 1658
