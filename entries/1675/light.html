Snapshot c. 1675 · Natural philosophy

Light and colours

What teaching texts printed at Paris, Wittenberg, Cologne and London between 1664 and 1681 said light and colours are. Paris and London teach Descartes’s pressure of a subtle matter; Wittenberg and Cologne teach a quality.

First cut, from one text per region, on the nature of light, its speed, and colours. Reflection, refraction and vision are not yet covered.

The four texts

  • RParis · FrenchJacques Rohault (1618–1672), Traité de physique, third edition (Paris, 1675), vol. 1. Part I, chapter XXVII, on light and colours, transparency and opacity, pp. 291–342.
  • SWittenberg · LatinJohann Sperling (1603–1658), Institutiones physicae (Wittenberg, 1664). Book III, chapter V, “De Luce”, pp. 495–526.
  • GCologne · LatinAntoine Goudin OP (1639–1695), Philosophia juxta inconcussa tutissimaque Divi Thomae dogmata (Cologne, 1681), vol. 3. Physics, second part, question II, article IV, on the properties of the heavenly bodies and chiefly on light, pp. 95–104.
  • LLondon · LatinAntoine Le Grand (1629–1699), Institutio philosophiae secundum principia D. Renati Descartes (London, 1675). Part V, article XIV, “De Lucis Natura”, pp. 282–285; Part VIII, article XVII, “De Coloribus”, pp. 511–513.

The texts are the most-reprinted teaching course printed in each region between 1660 and 1690, under the project’s rule for choosing texts. Goudin, a French Dominican, and Le Grand, a French Franciscan who taught in England, fill the places of the lands where their books were printed. Goudin’s article is on the light of the heavenly bodies; his treatment of light and colour as objects of sight, in the part of his physics on the soul, has not yet been read.

References give the text and its printed page. Linked references open the scan of that page. Quotations are given in translation, with the original beneath.

Where light sat among the sciences

The four texts put light in three different places. Rohault treats it in his general physics among the qualities that act on the senses, after sound and before vision, in one long chapter on light, colours, the transparent and the opaque.R 291 Sperling gives it a chapter of his third book, after the chapters on the heavens and the stars.S 495 Goudin treats it in his physics of the world, as the chief property of the heavenly bodies, in an article that goes on to the moon’s phases and eclipses.G 95G 102 Le Grand divides the subject: the nature of light comes in his part on the world and the heavens, straight after the sun, and colours in his part on man, as the object of sight, before vision.L 282L 511

What the texts taught in common

  • Light goes out from its source in straight lines. For Rohault the subtle matter tends “to go away in a straight line from the centre of the luminous body”.R 298–299 Sperling defines the ray as a quality leaping out from light “in rectum & acutum”, straight and sharp, while the light that fills the medium spreads sideways.S 495 For Le Grand luminous bodies push the subtle matter away from themselves “secundum lineas rectas”.L 282 Goudin’s article, on the light of the heavenly bodies, does not take the question up.
  • Some bodies shine without burning. Rohault names rotting wood, glow-worms and some flies, and the stone of Bologna, found by an Italian chemist: after six hours in the fire, set for a while in the light and carried into the dark, it glows like a coal under ash, and Rohault had seen such stones glow for nearly an eighth of an hour.R 301–304 Sperling tells of a king of Scotland who dressed men in shining fish scales, with rotten wood for staves, so that his nobles took them for spirits and fought the better next day.S 506 Le Grand uses worms and rotten wood to show that light need not be joined with heat.L 283 Goudin does not take the question up.

The disputes

What light is

The Paris and London courses give Descartes’s answer; the Wittenberg and Cologne courses give the answer of the schools. Rohault separates two things the word means. In the luminous body there is “primitive” light; in the space between, “secondary or derived” light:

“Thus primitive light will consist in a certain motion of the parts of the luminous body, which makes them able to push on every side the subtle matter that fills the pores of transparent bodies; and the inclination to move, or the tendency that this matter has to go away in a straight line from the centre of the luminous body, will constitute the essence of secondary, or derived, light.”R 298–299

Ainsi, la lumiere primitive consistera dans un certain mouvement des parties du corps lumineux, qui les rend capables de pousser à la ronde la matiere subtile qui remplit les pores des corps transparent ; & l’inclination à se mouvoir, ou la tendance qu’a cette matiere à s’éloigner en ligne droite du centre du corps lumineux, constituëra l’essence de la lumiere seconde, ou dérivée.

Le Grand says the same in fewer words, in the terms of Descartes’s elements: the matter of the first element at the centre of the sun strives to move away from it, presses the globules of the second element around it, and these press on outward.L 282

“I say that light is nothing other than a certain motion, or action, by which luminous bodies push subtle matter away from themselves in every direction along straight lines.”L 282

Dico, Lucem nihil aliud esse, quam motum quendam, vel actionem, qua corpora luminosa, materiam subtilem quaquaversum, secundum lineas rectas, à se propellunt.

Sperling opens his chapter with definitions. He too distinguishes the light in the source from the light in the medium, but by name and kind, not by motion: lux is in the luminous body, the ray (radius) comes from it, and lumen comes from both and fills the medium.S 495

“Light is the quality of a luminous body by which it is itself luminous and lights up other things.”S 495

Lux est qualitas corporis lucidi, quâ ipsum lucidum est, & alia illuminat.

All three are qualities, and accidents, not substances.S 506S 512 Those who make lumen a body, because it is reflected, wears the air and kindles fire, are answered: sound is an accident and is reflected too, in the echo.S 512 He then asks whether lumen is a real or an intentional quality, and answers with distinctions: it is real as opposed to a thing of reason, but in the sense in which real means material it is not real but spiritual, “just like the image in a mirror and all apparent colours”, and it is intentional because it is nothing but an image of lux. Here he follows Julius Caesar Scaliger (1484–1558), who calls lumen a species, a representation of lux, and Jacopo Zabarella (1533–1589) on what the word intention means.S 513–515

Goudin does not define light in the article read, but he gives it a place among the qualities. The heavens cannot have the qualities of the elements, heat, cold, dryness, moisture, heaviness and lightness, since these tend to corruption and the heavens are incorruptible. That the sun heats is no objection:

“for that it suffices that it be endowed with a certain higher quality in which heat is eminently contained, namely light.”G 96

Nec refert quod Sol calefaciat; ad id enim sufficit illum esse præditum qualitate quadam sublimiori, in qua eminenter contineatur calor, nempe luce.

Light as a body flowing from the source has no defender among the four. Le Grand answers those who say light is fire: two bodies would then be in one place, many things shine without heat, so great a body could not perish in a moment when the sun sets, and winds would stop it.L 283

Whether light is the act of the transparent

Rohault sets out the doctrine of Aristotle (384–322 BC) in the second book On the Soul: bodies such as air, water and glass are transparent only in potency at night, light alone brings that potency to act, and so “la Lumiere est l’acte du transparent, entant que transparent”, light is the act of the transparent in so far as it is transparent.R 294 He finds “not enough to be fully satisfied”: Aristotle leaves to be sought what the transparent body and the luminous body are, and what the presence of one does in the other.R 295 His own answer replaces the act of the transparent with a structure: a body is transparent when straight pores run through it in every direction, and opaque when they do not.R 299

Sperling rejects the definition outright. The transparent is transparent even without light, since a fire is seen far off on a black night through unlit air, and the stars from the darkness of night.S 518 His decisive case is the dark room:

“And it is well known that a man shut in a dark room can see the images of people walking outside, coming in through a small chink, painted on the white wall opposite. We therefore hold that light is in no way the act of the transparent.”S 518–519

Et notum est, hominem camera tenebrosa conclusum, per rimulam incidentes hominum foris ambulantium imagines in oppsito pariete albo videre posse depictas. Lumen ergò perspicui actum nullo modo esse censemus.

He supports it with a long passage from “the most celebrated mathematician of our academy”, Ambrosius Rhodius (1577–1633), whose Optics asks what the respect to sight adds to the nature of the transparent, since nothing in it is seen to change when light is present.S 519–520 Goudin and Le Grand do not take the definition up in the articles read.

Whether light takes time

For the two Cartesians the answer follows from the definition. Secondary light is not an actual motion but a tendency, and the world is full, so Rohault concludes:

“it follows necessarily that the luminous body, however far off it is, must transmit its action and make itself felt in an instant, because the matter it pushes is continuously extended; and that, like a very long stick in this, the luminous body cannot push the nearest part without the farthest being at the same time disposed to move and to advance.”R 305

il suit delà necessairement que le corps lumineux, pour éloigné qu’il soit, doit transmettre son action & se faire sentir en un instant ; à cause que cette matiere qu’il pousse est continuëment étenduë ; & que semblable en cela à un baston qui seroit fort long, le corps lumineux ne sçauroit pousser la plus proche, que celle qui est la plus éloignée ne se trouve en mesme-temps disposée à se mouvoir, & à avancer.

Le Grand: “Ita ut minimo temporis momento, Lux ad quamcunque distantiam diffundatur”, so that light is spread to any distance in the least moment of time.L 282 He admits one difficulty, how the light of the sun and the fixed stars can reach us in a moment, and answers it the same way: light consists not in the duration of a motion but in a sudden action, as a stick presses at both ends at once, and as at night we find water, stones and mud with a stick.L 283L 284

Sperling takes the other side, against what he reports as the common view. Others hold that the motion of light is “not in time, not successive, but momentary”, because light meets no positive contrary in its subject and because of its supreme excellence.S 520 He answers that every motion has tendency, progress and succession; that the arguments of Francisco de Toledo SJ (1532–1596) against a stone falling through a vacuum in an instant conclude just as strongly against light; and that twilight, in which light is lessened, shows it moving in time.S 520–521

“Certainly no one will ever deny that light reaches a nearer end sooner than a more distant one. It therefore moves in time, and suffers delay.”S 521

Certè terminum propiorem citiùs attingere lumen, quàm remotiorem, nullus unquam ibit inficias. In tempore ergò movetur, morasque patitur.

Goudin does not take the question up.

What colours are

For the Cartesians colours are light changed by the bodies it meets. Rohault: since light is only a certain motion of the little balls of the second element, “or at least an inclination to move” in a certain way, to know colours one need only run through the modifications this motion can take, and find what in coloured bodies causes them.R 322 A white body is one whose rough surface sends the light back as it came; goldsmiths whiten silver by roughening it with corrosives and take the whiteness off by polishing it with bloodstone.R 323–325 Red, yellow and blue come from rays whose balls have less inclination to go straight and have taken a turning about their own centres, as the prism shows.R 328 Le Grand: rays can be turned aside by the bodies they strike, like a ball thrown at a wall, and can take a circular motion as a ball does.L 511

“I say that colours in general are nothing other than the various ways in which bodies receive the rays sent out from a luminous body, and send them back to the eyes.”L 511

Dico, Colores in genere, nihil aliud sunt, quam varii modi, quibus corpora radios à corpore luminoso emissos, recipiunt, & ad oculos remittunt.

He adds that white and black suffice to compose all the others, since the colours called middle colours have something of whiteness and of blackness; red comes from rays set turning strongly about their centres.L 512 Sperling separates light and colour as two qualities: “Lucem colorem non esse, monstrat Sensus, dictitat Ratio”, that light is not colour, sense shows and reason declares. In darkness we see light but cannot see colours; light illuminates and colour cannot; light makes a thing luminous and colour makes it coloured. He reports that for Zabarella light is a colour, a kind of whiteness, and does not follow him.S 510 The colours of earthly bodies are not in Goudin’s article.

Whether the colours of the prism are true colours

The schools divided colours into true colours, which belong to bodies, and apparent colours, such as those of the rainbow, the prism and the mirror. Sperling keeps the division: lumen is spiritual, “Non secus ac imago in speculo, omnesq; colores apparentes”, like the image in a mirror and all apparent colours.S 514 Goudin applies it to the heavens. They are perfectly transparent and tinged with no colour, or they would stain the light of the stars, and what looks like colour in the stars is something else:

“In the stars light seems to be present in place of colour, either their own or reflected; so this diversity of colour is nothing but a different modification of light.”G 98

Sideribus verò pro colore lux inesse videtur, vel propria vel reflexa :Vnde hæc diversitas colori non est nisi diversa modificatio lucis

The Cartesians reject the division. Rohault:

“But I do not think there is any foundation for this division, since there is neither more nor less reality in the one than in the others. For if the sensation of colour one has in looking at a tapestry is real, the one had in looking through a prism is no less so; the prism is no less real than the tapestry; and finally, it is the same light that comes in to make us sense the colours of the prism as to make us sense the others.”R 338–339

Mais je n’estime pas qu’il y ait aucun fondement en cette division, puis qu’il n’y a ny plus ny moins de realité aux unes qu’aux autres ; Car si le sentiment de couleur qu’on experimente en regardant une tapisserie est réel, celuy que l’on a en regardant au trauers d’un prisme ne l’est pas moins ; le prisme n’est pas moins réel que la tapisserie ; Et enfin, c’est la mesme lumiere qui intervient, pour nous faire sentir les couleurs du prisme, que pour nous faire sentir les autres.

He rejects as well the newer division into fixed and passing colours, since the prism held still shows the same colours as long as a tapestry does.R 339 Le Grand knows that most distinguish apparent colours from true ones, and answers that the nature of colour consists only in appearing and being seen: “Repugnat enim, ut aliquid appareat, & falsum sit”, it is a contradiction that something should appear and be false.L 512

Why they differed

Two different questions

The Cartesians and the schools were not answering the same question. Rohault asks what in bodies causes the sensation, and warns against supposing that it resembles the sensation: a pin causes pain by its figure and hardness alone, with nothing in it like pain, and light in bodies may likewise be only the power to cause a very clear and lively sensation.R 296–298 Sperling asks what kind of being light is: substance or accident, quality, real or intentional, and whether it is the act of the transparent.S 506S 512–516 Goudin asks which qualities a heavenly body can have, and admits light because it can do the work of heat without being heat.G 96

Conjecture, the Author, and authorities

Rohault offers his account as a hypothesis to be tested by what follows from it:

“I do not claim for now that all this should be received otherwise than as a simple conjecture”R 299

Je ne pretens pas maintenant que tout cela soit autrement receu que comme une simple conjecture

If he can show that it holds nothing that is not true, and that the least properties of light can be deduced from it, he hopes it will be received as a very certain truth.R 299 Le Grand rests on his master: “A nullo quod sciam, melius quam ab Authore quem sequor, Lucis Natura explicatur”, by no one, so far as I know, is the nature of light better explained than by the Author whom I follow.L 283 Sperling argues question by question from sense, reason and named authorities, Aristotle, Zabarella, Scaliger, Toledo and Rhodius, and for the origin of light from Scripture: where light came from the Spirit of the Lord revealed when God said, let there be light, and God’s question to Job, where light dwells, was not meant to stop the inquiry but to show the depth of his wisdom.S 508–509

Why the Cartesians needed no time

The instant follows from the Cartesian definition: what travels is not matter but a pressure in a full world, and a pressure in a rigid stick is felt at both ends at once.R 305L 283 Sperling treats light as a motion and so brings it under the general rule that every motion is successive, with Toledo’s arguments against motion in an instant.S 520–521 The quality account here gives the answer that a moving matter would.

Why the colours of the prism mattered

If colours are light modified, every colour is made the same way, and Rohault puts any falsity in the judgment that follows sight, not in the sight itself: if referring the colours of the prism to objects where they are not made them false, all colours would be false, since every colour is referred to objects in the same way.R 339 For Goudin the division protects the heavens. Being incorruptible and perfectly transparent they can have no true colour, so the gold of the sun, the red of Mars and the blue of the sky must be apparent, light modified by the depth of the transparent.G 95G 97–98

Questions the texts left open

  • The light of the first day. Sperling: the light created before the sun was a body, since what is created is a substance and it was no spirit; it hung about the abyss of the waters as the stars are about the air, and by its motion made the first three days. Those who make it a quality are answered, but that it was a body does not make lumen now a body.S 513
  • Whose light the heavenly bodies shine with. Goudin: “Non modò Sol sed etiam Stellæ fixæ fulgent propriâ luce”, not only the sun but the fixed stars shine with their own light, since they are too far from the sun to borrow much and yet are most radiant.G 98 The planets borrow from the sun the light we see, as the moon, the phases of Venus in the telescope, Jupiter’s satellites in its shadow and the shadow of Saturn’s ring show; yet all of them, the moon included, seem to have some faint light of their own, since light is the property most akin to heavenly bodies.G 99–101
  • The secondary light of the moon. The faint light seen on the crescent moon is not its own, since it vanishes at the quarters and in a central eclipse, as Pierre Gassendi (1592–1655) often reports.G 100 Tycho Brahe (1546–1601) thought it came from Venus; the commoner and truer opinion is that it comes from the earth. A sunlit spot on a wall no bigger than a coin lights a dark hall forty feet across, so the sunlit half of the earth can light the moon. The red of the eclipsed moon comes from sunlight passing the earth through vaporous air.G 101
  • Why the sky is blue. Goudin: not from any colour in the heavens, but from the great depth of the transparent, in which sunlight is absorbed and degenerates into blue; for the same cause the sea and the air round distant mountains look blue.G 97 Le Grand gives the sea’s blue a Cartesian reason: few rays come back from the surface of deep, clear water and none of those that enter it return.L 512
