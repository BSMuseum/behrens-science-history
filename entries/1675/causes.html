Snapshot c. 1675 · Natural philosophy

Causes, nature, chance and fate

What teaching texts printed at Wittenberg, Bologna and London between 1664 and 1686 said about the causes of natural things, nature, fortune and chance, monsters and fate. The two schoolmen, a Lutheran and a Dominican, teach four causes and a nature that works for ends; the Cartesian keeps the four names, admits God alone as the efficient cause and shuts the final cause out of physics, and the Dominican names Descartes as the one who sinned against philosophy by doing so.

First cut, from one text per region. The French course, Rohault’s, has no section on the subject, so Paris is blank. Monsters come in as nature’s errors; the influence of the stars, generation and the soul are left to their own topics.

The texts

  • SWittenberg · LatinJohann Sperling (1603–1658), Institutiones physicae (Wittenberg, 1664). Book I, on the causes of the natural body: chapter I, the precepts, p. 27; chapters IV–X, on nature, the efficient cause, the end, fortune, chance, monsters and fate, pp. 128–260.
  • GBologna · LatinAntoine Goudin OP (1639–1695), Philosophia juxta inconcussa tutissimaque Divi Thomae dogmata, vol. 2 (Bologna, 1686). Physics, first part, disputation II, “De Natura, & causis Entis mobilis”, pp. 133–259, in seven questions: nature, violence and art; causes in common; the material and formal causes; the efficient cause; the final cause; the causes compared; fortune, chance and fate.
  • LLondon · LatinAntoine Le Grand (1629–1699), Institutio philosophiae secundum principia D. Renati Descartes (London, 1675). Part IV, general physics, article XV, “De rerum causis, & quod nullus sit causæ finalis usus in Physicis”, pp. 201–202, with article XVIII, on whether a body can move itself or another, pp. 208–209; Part II, natural theology, article IV, “De Fato”, pp. 90–92.

The texts are the most-reprinted teaching course printed in each region between 1660 and 1690, under the project’s rule for choosing texts. The sections are those of the courses’ own tables of contents, confirmed on the page images. Le Grand treats fate in his natural theology, not his physics; under the rule a passage with its own heading counts as the course’s section on the topic wherever it stands.

Paris is blank. Jacques Rohault (1618–1672), whose Traité de physique is the French text of the other entries, has no section on causes, nature, chance or fate: his chapter on the principles of natural beings, read for the entry on matter and form, goes straight to matter. Under the rule the topic would pass to the next French course in the count, but that is Goudin, who already fills the Catholic German place, and the courses after him had only one edition each in the window. The blank is itself a finding: a Cartesian course could leave this whole subject out.

Goudin’s course is the Catholic German text, from its Cologne printing of 1681; the Cologne volume holding the first part of the physics has not been found in a public scan, so this entry, like the one on matter and form, reads the same volume in the Bologna printing of 1686.

Where the subject sat among the sciences

Sperling and Goudin give it the same place, right after matter and form. Sperling’s first book is on the causes of the natural body: the internal causes, matter and form, then nature, which is both of them taken together, then the external causes, the efficient cause and the end, then the causes by accident, fortune and chance, then monsters, as nature’s errors, and last fate.S 27S 128S 247 Goudin’s second disputation, on the nature and causes of mobile being, comes between the principles and motion; it closes with fortune, chance and fate because these “seem to have the character of a cause”, and men often ascribe to them what they see happen in the world.G 133 He has no question on monsters, which come up under the final cause.G 238

Le Grand divides the subject. The causes get one short article of the general physics, between the void and motion, and most of it explains why he will not dwell on them.L 201 Fate is in the natural theology, among the articles on God, after his existence and before his attributes.L 90 There is nothing on nature as a principle, on fortune and chance, or on monsters.

What the texts taught in common

  • A cause is that upon whose being another follows. Sperling: the causes of the natural body are the principles that flow into its being.S 27 Goudin gives the definition he takes from Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), “Causa rectè definitur, ad quam sequitur esse alterius”, that upon which the being of another follows, and he means the following by a real, physical influx, not the logical one of a conclusion on its premisses.G 159 Le Grand has the same words without the name: “Id ad cujus esse aliquid sequitur”, as light follows the being of the sun.L 201
  • Causes are counted four: matter, form, the efficient cause and the end. Sperling divides them into internal causes, which enter the body’s essence (matter and form), and external ones, which make it from outside (the efficient cause and the end).S 27 Goudin: the mathematician demonstrates by the formal cause alone, the physicist argues from all four, because motions and moving things depend on all four causes and are known through them.G 133 Le Grand grants that they are “commonly counted four” and shows the number from the four ways of asking about a thing, but adds at once that in physics he admits no matter but extended body and no substantial form but the human soul.L 201
  • Nature acts for an end. Sperling: whoever now thinks nature does anything rashly is laughed at by the Aristotelians, hissed off by the Platonists and beaten on the Galenists’ orders. Whatever always acts in the same way acts for an end, and nature always does, as crops grow, metals form and plants are begotten; where there is much wisdom there is little chance.S 183S 184 Goudin: every nature acts for an end, at least as an arrow does, which tends to the mark without knowing it. This is against Leucippus (5th century BC), Democritus (c. 460–c. 370 BC) and Epicurus (341–270 BC), who gave nature’s works to chance and the concourse of atoms, and it is certain also by faith, which holds that God governs nature and orders everything to its ends. The seasons, the parts of plants and the build of animals show it.G 234G 235G 236 Le Grand does not ask the question; he asks only whether God’s ends may be sought in physics (below).
  • God’s wonders are above nature, not against it. Sperling: the virgin birth, the sun standing still and the iron that floated are brought from Scripture to prove that God acts against nature, but they confuse what is above nature with what is against it. What is against nature comes about with a struggle of natural things, what is above it without one, and nature does not resist God’s command but obeys.S 142 Goudin: with Augustine of Hippo (354–430), God, creator of all natures, does nothing against nature, since a creature’s inmost inclination is to obey its creator; the sun’s halt at Joshua’s word and the Jordan turned back were not violent to them, “tota enim natura est veluti artefactum Diuinæ Sapientiæ”, for the whole of nature is as it were a work of God’s art.G 144G 145G 146 Le Grand does not take it up. Whether God could ever act against nature is disputed (below).
  • Fortune and chance are causes by accident. Sperling: fortune is a cause by accident in actions men undertake by a set purpose, where some other outcome falls out than the one intended, as when a farmer digs for his crops and finds a treasure; chance is the same in the actions of natural things, as when a tile falls from a roof and wounds a passer-by. Both belong to the efficient cause, not of themselves but by accident.S 196S 197S 201S 203 Goudin, setting aside the heathen goddess, makes them causes by accident concurring, from which an unlooked-for effect follows beyond the intention, fortune in free causes, chance in natural ones. His examples are recent: the fire that almost burned down London (1666), and the good fortune of the Spaniards who sailed west under Christopher Columbus (1451–1506) and fell upon the rich regions of America; the sea that broke the dykes of the Low Countries drowned men by chance, since the end of its swelling is to cleanse the waters.G 253G 254 Le Grand gives no account of either.
  • Nothing is by fortune or chance with respect to God. Sperling: “DEO nihil potest esse fortuitum”, to God nothing can be fortuitous, since he is supremely wise. An event God foreknew and even intended stays fortuitous because it does not come from the intention of the particular cause, so that a man may be killed by chance and yet have been given by God into his slayer’s hands; our fortune is to be put under providence, not set against it.S 200S 201 Goudin: fortune and chance have place with respect to angels and the heavens too, as when a guardian angel prompts a man to go to church and he meets and kills his enemy, but not with respect to God, who foresees everything and either permits or preordains it.G 255G 256 Le Grand: fate is a higher cause from which come effects “qui respectu nostri fortuiti sunt”, which are fortuitous with respect to us.L 91
  • There is a fate, rightly understood: an order of things foreknown and preordained by God. Sperling defines it so and divides it four ways: theological fate, God’s eternal decree, by which men are rich or poor, handsome or ugly, wise or foolish; astrological fate, the stars’ influence on bodies below; physical fate, the order of all natural causes, under God, by which come cold, heat, winds and rain; and Stoic fate.S 247S 248 He will not use the name of fate for God himself, since fate means a series of causes, though authors such as Seneca (c. 4 BC–AD 65) use fate, nature and God without distinction.S 256 Goudin: “Datur fatum legitimo sensu explicatum”, there is a fate, rightly explained, which is the divine preordination impressed on contingent things; he gives the definitions of Boethius (c. 477–524) and Aquinas, and Augustine’s advice that whoever calls God’s will fate should keep his opinion and correct his tongue.G 257G 258 Le Grand: fate is the eternal decree of God’s providence, by which he sees, knows and governs everything.L 91
  • Fate lays no necessity on the human will. Sperling: to drag the stars’ fate over our will “est homini liberum arbitrium detrahere”, is to take free choice from man and to blame God, the stars’ ruler and creator; he quotes Philipp Melanchthon (1497–1560) against referring all events to the stars. Stoic fate, an absolute necessity binding even God, is impious and blasphemous.S 248S 257 Goudin, after Aquinas, rejects the Stoics because a cause, once posited, need not produce its effect, being hindered by a stronger cause that is itself contingent; the fate that ruled the gods in Homer is fable.G 256G 257 Le Grand: fate “nullam necessitatem actionibus humanis imponat”, lays no necessity on human actions, as our own experience of choosing shows, and God’s foreknowledge follows what will be, not the reverse.L 91L 92
  • The heavens govern the coming to be and lasting of things below the moon, but not the will. Sperling: the heavenly bodies, under God’s dominion, flow into the generations and corruptions of natural bodies; the astrological fate is “most true and most certain” within its limits, bringing the seasons, day and night, the waxing and waning of humours in plants and animals, and the eclipses, but beyond them it becomes “harmful and damnable”.S 148S 257 Goudin: each sublunary thing receives from the heaven at its first production a disposition by which it lasts and on whose loss it perishes; this may in some way be called fate, though not properly, since chance can hasten a death and art and remedies delay it. Those who made the heaven necessarily rule men’s wills are left to be answered under the heavens’ influence.G 256G 258 Le Grand does not take either up in the articles read.
  • Monsters are nature’s errors. Sperling: a monster is a natural effect produced with notable deformity by a cause that fails and errs, as a two-headed or one-eyed man; nature intends it “not by its primary intention but by its secondary one”, aiming at the imperfect only when it cannot reach the perfect. Monsters belong to physics indirectly, as bad syllogisms to the logician.S 207S 224S 244 Goudin, answering the objection that nature often sins, as in monsters, says with Aquinas that sins and monsters prove nature acts for an end, since “Peccatum enim, & monstrum est deuiatio à fine”, a sin and a monster is a straying from the end, as a good archer’s arrow is turned by the wind. Their causes are too much matter, too little, unfit matter and disturbance; they are rarer in brutes, which follow nature’s instinct, and more frequent in men, God also punishing men’s sins with such disgraces.G 238G 239 Le Grand does not take them up.
  • The offspring of two kinds, as the mule, are monsters. Sperling holds the mule “a monster abominable to God and to nature”, like both its parents and like neither.S 239 Goudin: unfit matter causes monsters above all in things born of two kinds, as of the lioness and the pard or the mare and the ass; in Africa animals of many kinds meet at the few waters and mix, whence the leopard and the proverb that Africa always brings something new.G 239

The disputes

What nature is

Goudin sorts the senses of the word, birth, matter and form, the essence of a thing, and keeps the fourth, the inner root from which a thing’s sensible motions first flow, as when a stone falls, air rises, the heart beats, water cools and fire heats. Of nature in that sense the definition of Aristotle (384–322 BC) stands.G 134G 135

“Nature is rightly defined: the principle and cause of motion and rest of that in which it is, first and by itself and not by accident.”G 135

Natura rectè definitur: principium, & causa motus, & quietis eius in quo est, primò, & per se, & non secundùm accidens.

Neither God nor the angels are nature in the physicist’s sense, being above all change; God is called natura naturans only by a kind of excellence, as author, preserver and governor of nature.G 140

Sperling puts his own definition at the head of the chapter: nature is the internal cause by which any natural body has its essence, properties, actions and passions, matter and form taken together, one nature of man, one of the horse.S 128S 140 Then he takes the schools’ definition apart. Aristotle left no definitions, and those who sell his collected words for them start quarrels they can never settle; and since all natural science hangs on the definition of nature, whoever errs here errs most dangerously.S 137

“The definition of nature they propose is faulty.”S 138

Naturæ definitio ab iisdem proposita vitiosa est.

It is ambiguous in almost every word, principle, cause, motion and rest; it gives nature motion and rest, though some natural things lack one or the other, and leaves out quantity, figure and quality, which no natural thing lacks; and “by itself” and “not by accident” say the same thing twice.S 138S 139 Le Grand gives no definition of nature.

Whether final causes belong in physics

Sperling: the end is the external cause for whose sake the natural body is made, and natural bodies are made, kept and work for it. The universal end is the glory of God, for whose sake the whole world was created and is kept; the particular end is the good each thing tends to by nature’s prescription, as man’s to be wise and the sun’s to shine.S 177S 178

“For the end natural bodies are produced, preserved and operate.”S 178

Propter finem corpora naturalia producuntur, conservantur, operantur.

Le Grand’s article has the refusal in its title: there is no use of the final cause in physics. We may ask by what principle natural things are made, of what matter and by what form, but not what led God to create the world, nor what ends he set himself in making the earth, the stars and men from nothing.L 201

“It is rash to try to sound so great an abyss and to make ourselves sharers in his counsels.”L 201

Temerarium est tantum abyssum velle penetrare, & consiliorum ejus velle nos reddere participes.

We are weak and of limited capacity, God immense, of infinite power and incomprehensible. In morals, where conjectures count, it may help piety to draw out an end God set himself; in physics, where nothing is admitted but what rests on the firmest arguments, it is rash to rise above the stars and pry into God’s secrets.L 201L 202

Goudin answers Le Grand’s master, René Descartes (1596–1650). Since nature acts for an end and the end is the first of the causes, the philosopher’s chief care is to contemplate the order of means to ends that God’s wisdom set in natural things.

“From this you will gather in passing that René Descartes sinned gravely against philosophy when he ordered it to abstain wholly from the search for final causes, in the first part of his book of Principles, number 28.”G 236

Ex his obitèr colliges, Renatum Descartes grauitèr contra Philosophiam peccasse, dum iubet eam prorsùs abstinere ab inuestigatione causarum finalium, in prima parte libri principiorum num 28.

Nor does Descartes’s reason press, that it is presumption to search out God’s counsels, which are past understanding: to pry into the counsels God keeps secret is the boldest rashness, but to contemplate those shown in Scripture or shining in his creatures is the work of Christian and philosophical wisdom, and the ends of natural things can be read off their disposition, as why the liver lies in the belly, the lung in the chest and the eye in the head. Galen (129–c. 216) was moved to praise God by the fitness of the body’s parts. “Those who would play the Epicurean and give nature’s works to the collisions of corpuscles do well to keep silent about final causes.”G 236G 237 The reason Goudin answers is the one Le Grand gives.

Whether the world was made for man

Sperling: besides God’s glory there is a universal end in a relative sense, that whose use the whole world with all its parts serves, “Talis est homo Mundi Epitome & Naturæ delitiæ”: such is man, the epitome of the world and nature’s darling.S 177S 178 Le Grand puts the same view in the mouths of those who say the sun was made to light us, our mind to consider it, the whole universe for man, so that God’s ends could be known like other causes. They can fix nothing about God’s end but by conjecture:

“for it is absurd to imagine that the sun was created for nothing else than to light us”L 202

Nam insulsum est imaginari, ad nihil aliud Solem quam ad nos illuminandos fuisse creatum

We are the least part of the earth and the sun many times larger than the whole earth, and many things exist and have existed that no man has seen.L 202 Goudin was not found to take the question up; he says only, after Aquinas, that we are the end of all things made by art.G 151

Whether created things truly act

Sperling divides the efficient cause: God is the first universal cause, by whose influx all natural bodies are made; the heavenly bodies are second universal causes, under God’s dominion; particular causes make one kind each, as a man begets a man, not a stone or a horse. The sun is a remote cause of a man, “Sic homo est causa proxima hominis, equus equi”, so man is the proximate cause of a man, a horse of a horse, and whoever wants to know must go from the remote cause to the proximate.S 147S 148S 167 Goudin: it is certain that the whole substance acts, since acting belongs to what has being; heat in water or iron produces heat, and the substantial form alone produces another substantial form, as all hold against a few who think substantial forms idle and let the accidents do everything.G 184G 185

Le Grand allows the name of efficient cause to God alone:

“Under the name of efficient cause comes God alone, the best and greatest, who is the principle of all things and on whom alone all the rest depend.”L 201

Causæ autem efficientis nomine, solus Deus optimus maximus venit, qui omnium rerum est principium, & à quo solo omnia cætera pendent.

His physics gives the ground. A body is nothing but extension, and motion is no effect of extension, so no body can move itself; nor can it move another, since a body gives motion only by losing as much as it gives, and no one gives what he does not have. If all creatures had lain together at the beginning with nothing but extension, none could have moved itself or the rest; so “Deum, omnis motus, qui in mundo est, esse principium”, God is the principle of all the motion there is in the world.L 208L 209

Whether God can act against nature

Sperling: no. Nature is from God, “unde si contra eam Deus ageret, contra seipsum ageret, quod impium & blasphemum”: so if God acted against it he would act against himself, which is impious and blasphemous.S 142 Goudin distinguishes. Moving creatures as the universal governor of nature, bending their inclinations from within, God does them no violence; but he can also act as a particular agent, impressing a motion against a thing’s inclination while keeping the inclination, as if he put heat into water and kept water’s enmity to heat. It seems probable that in this second way he can do violence, since Aquinas says not all God does in creatures is natural to them, and God binds the demons, as Peter says, with chains of fire.G 146G 147

What art can make

Sperling: taken apart, nature is stronger than art, “Ars enim mera facit accidentia”, for art makes mere accidents, while nature makes substances too; taken together, art excels, since nature often begins what art completes, as a born poet is perfected by art.S 140S 141 Goudin: art cannot work by its own forms, which are barren, but it can bring about natural effects by applying natural powers to fit matter, as the physician heals with natural remedies, and as the demons, who know the powers of animals, herbs, stones and metals exactly, made the magicians’ serpents and frogs in Egypt. Many natural things, though, have producing powers not subject to human art, and chief among them is gold.G 153G 154 Gold is formed by the sun’s heat in a fit place, the mine its mother and the sun its father, over almost whole ages; the fire’s heat cannot stand in for the sun’s, gold has no seed, and the experiments alleged are all false, so that the Church forbids selling such gold as true.G 154G 155G 156G 157

“no one was ever made rich by this art, but those who sought that counterfeit gold turned their true gold into smoke”G 155

nemo diues hac arte effectus est, sed qui fictitium istud aurum quæsiuere, verum aurum in fumos conuerterunt

Sperling leaves metals to his book on minerals; Le Grand does not take art up.

Whether a monster is judged against what is usual

Goudin defines a monster as an effect “à recta, & solita dispositione degenerantem”, degenerating from the right and usual disposition, and lists the poets’ Cyclopes, the six-fingered giant of Scripture, the satyrs and the centaurs as examples.G 238 Sperling quotes the same definition as the common one and rejects it: its defenders use “usual” to take out of the monsters the very ones that adorn their order, giants where they are frequent in some province, pygmies where they are small for some local cause. But a multitude of errors gives no patronage to error.S 246S 247

“what is an error at Wittenberg cannot be right and true at Rome”S 247

quòd Witebergæ erratum est, Romæ rectum verumque esse nequit

Le Grand does not take monsters up.

Why they differed

What physics may admit. Le Grand’s refusals come from one rule, which he states twice. Of ends: in physics nothing is admitted but what rests on the firmest arguments, and God’s ends can only be guessed.L 201L 202 Of qualities: it is rash in physics to say that a thing happens in a way that does not fall under our imagination, so gravity and impressed qualities, which no one can picture acting, are to be held to do nothing, and another cause of motion must be sought.L 208 With body reduced to extension, that cause can only be God.L 209 Goudin and Sperling ask less of a cause before admitting it. For Goudin the ends of natural things show in their disposition, and faith already teaches that God orders nature to its ends;G 234G 237 for Sperling nature’s constancy is proof enough that it works for an end.S 184

Descartes as Epicurus. Goudin sees the refusal of final causes as the ancients’ error come back. The order of nature refutes Leucippus, Democritus and Epicurus, who gave everything to chance and the atoms; Cicero (106–43 BC), he says, rightly laughed at Epicurus, since no clock is made by throwing iron into a fire; and those who give nature’s works to colliding corpuscles “do well to keep silent” about ends.G 234G 236G 237 Le Grand does not mention Epicurus; his reason is God’s infinity and our weakness.L 201

Aristotle’s words, and Aquinas’s. Goudin takes his definitions of nature, cause, fortune and fate from Aristotle as Aquinas read him, with Boethius and Augustine beside them.G 135G 159G 253G 257 Sperling, at Lutheran Wittenberg, treats Aristotle more freely: Aristotle gave no definitions, the schools’ definition of nature is faulty, and he writes his own; on fate he quotes Melanchthon.S 137S 138S 257 On the definition of a monster his answer to the schools is a jab at Rome.S 247

God as the universal agent. Sperling’s nature is from God, so God cannot act against it without acting against himself.S 142 Goudin’s distinction between God acting as governor of nature and God acting as a particular agent, taken from Aquinas, leaves room for a violence Sperling calls blasphemous.G 147

Questions the texts left open

  • Do frogs, mice and worms come from seed? Sperling: flies, mice, worms and frogs are determinate effects and cannot come from a universal, indeterminate cause such as the sun and stars, so those who rest in the sun and stars when such things are generated rest wrongly; “Et licet sub conspectum non semper cadant semina, non tamen propterea neganda sunt”, though their seeds do not always come into view, they are not on that account to be denied.S 167 Goudin, arguing about gold, says that a chick hatched by a fire’s heat is formed by the seminal power in the egg, not by the fire, but does not ask about frogs and worms.G 155G 156 The question belongs to the topic on generation.
  • Can a man and a beast beget a true man? Sperling: no, “Nos quæstionem negandam censemus”; the Coimbra commentators waver, first denying it, then saying experience does not wholly confirm the denial, then making such offspring beasts of some species.S 244
  • How things without knowledge reach their ends. Goudin: brutes act for an end not only as an arrow does but by apprehending the good in imagination, though they do not order their own actions; they and other natural things are directed by the author of nature.G 239 Sperling gives brutes a natural prudence, as of swallows, ants and spiders, by instinct; his questions on this were read only in the machine-read text.

Terms, for modern readers

In the textsNearest term today
The four causes: material, formal, efficient, finalAristotle’s four answers to “why?”: what a thing is made of, what makes it the kind of thing it is, what brought it about, and what it is for
Final cause; end (finis)Purpose or function; explanation by purpose is now called teleology
Cause by accident (causa per accidens)A cause of an outcome it was not aimed at, as digging is of finding a treasure
Fortune; chance (fortuna, casus)Luck in human undertakings; accident in natural events
Fate (fatum)For these authors, God’s foreordering of events, or the order of causes it sets up; not blind necessity
Natura naturansNature as the maker, meaning God, against nature as the things made
Second causesCreated things as causes, acting under God as the first cause
God alone the efficient causeClose to what historians call occasionalism, a name later than these texts
Violence (violentia)A motion forced on a thing against its natural inclination, as a stone thrown upward
MonsterAny birth with a marked malformation; also, for these authors, a cross between two kinds
