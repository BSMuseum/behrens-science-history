Snapshot c. 1675 · Natural philosophy

Water, the sea and springs

What teaching texts printed at Paris, Wittenberg, Cologne and London between 1664 and 1681 said about water, the saltness of the sea, the tides and the springs. All four bring the springs from the sea; they part on what water is, what moves the tides, and why the sea is salt.

First cut, from one text per region. Rivers and lakes in detail, the wonders of particular waters, and ice are not yet covered.

  • SWittenberg · LatinJohann Sperling (1603–1658), Institutiones physicae (Wittenberg, 1664). Book IV, chapter III, “De Aqua”, pp. 568–598.

Physics, second part, question III, on the elementary world, pp. 122–132; third part, question III, article III, § IV, on the meteors formed in water and earth and the origin of springs and rivers, pp. 273–285.

Where water sat among the sciences

Sperling gives water one chapter of his book on the elements, and puts everything in it: water as an element, the sea, its saltness, the tides, springs and rivers, and the wonders of particular waters.S 568 The other three divide the subject. Rohault ends his part on the world with the tides, as an effect of the world’s structure, and treats water, salt and springs in his part on terrestrial things, among the bodies formed in and on the earth.R 152R 169 Goudin puts water as an element, and the height of the sea, in his physics of the world, and the tides, the saltness of the sea and the origin of springs among the meteors formed in water and earth.G 122G 273 Le Grand treats springs first among the things generated in the earth, straight after the climates, and water and the tide later in the same part, after the magnet and before the air.L 340L 359

What the texts taught in common

  • The sea feeds the springs. All four argue from the same fact: most springs never fail, and the rivers they make run into the sea without swelling it. Rohault: “c’est la Mer qui fournit d’eau à toutes les fontaines”, it is the sea that supplies water to all the springs.R 275 Sperling: the sea is “origo, caput, & hospitium”, the origin, head and lodging, of rivers, streams and springs, which run through the veins of the earth like the blood of animals.S 573S 575 Le Grand: the origin of springs seems to be nothing but the sea itself.L 340 Goudin gives three causes, and the sea is “the third and more universal”.G 278
  • The tides keep time with the moon. Rohault: the sea rises and falls twice a day, about fifty minutes later each day, as the moon comes to the meridian about fifty minutes later; the tides are greatest near new and full moon.R 141R 142 Le Grand: twice in twenty-four hours and fifty minutes, the sea swelling with the moon on the meridian and falling with her on the horizon.L 361 Goudin: the tide follows the moon’s daily course and is stronger at new and full moon.G 274 Sperling: at the quarters the sea is calm and at full moon it swells.S 585
  • Waters take their tastes and powers from the ground they pass through. Rohault: salt springs, as in Burgundy and Lorraine, dissolve salt in the ground, and veins that meet metals or minerals carry off their finest parts.R 279 Sperling: however clear it looks, water “non magis tamen pura est, quàm urina aut lixivium”, is no purer than urine or lye.S 580 Goudin: the greatest part of their tastes, smells and qualities “provenit ex subterraneis locis, per quæ transeunt”, comes from the places underground they pass through.G 277 Le Grand does not take the question up: for him sea water drawn off in an alembic gives “aqua pura, fontanæ, aut fluviali omnino similis”, pure water wholly like spring or river water, and salt found in wells far from the sea is sea water that has reached them unfiltered through cracks in the earth.L 360L 343
  • Vapour is not air. Rohault: evaporating water is only water whose parts have separated, and they join again into the same water on cold bodies; a flask of air sealed and kept three years in a cask of water in his cellar showed “que l’air ne se change point en eau”, that air does not change into water.R 172R 173 Sperling: “Vapor quid nisi aqua igni cōmista est?”, what is vapour but water mixed with fire?S 577 Le Grand implies it without saying so: vapours raised by heat condense again into drops, and air is made of the subtler parts of exhalations, particles of earthy bodies, which differ from the vapours torn from water.L 342L 365L 366 Goudin does not take the question up in the sections read.

The disputes

What water is made of

For the two schoolmen water is one of the four elements. Goudin opens his question on the elementary world:

“There are four elements, or greater bodies, of which the sublunary world consists, namely earth, water, air and fire.”G 122

Quatuor sunt elementa, seu corpora majora ex quibus constat. Mundus sublunaris, scilicet Terra, Aqua, Aër, & Ignis.

Sperling’s first precept is a definition: “Aqua est Elementum crassum & grave, frigidissimum, ac humidissimum, unum cum terrà globum constituens”, water is a thick and heavy element, the coldest and moistest, making up one globe with the earth.S 568 For the Cartesians water is a heap of particles of a particular shape. Rohault puts his forward as likely, “il y a lieu de croire”, there is reason to believe: water is a heap of little bodies like little cords, long, flexible and pliant, moulded in the wavy pores of the earth; salt is long, straight parts of the matter of the first element, congealed in the straight pores of the inner earth.R 169R 175 Le Grand: water and the other terrestrial bodies are made of particles of the third element.L 359

Whether water is cold by nature

Sperling makes coldness and moisture belong to water absolutely:

“The moisture of water is likewise absolute, so that water is the first subject both of coldness and of moisture.”S 570

Humiditas aquæ itidem Absoluta est, adeò ut aqua primum & frigiditatis & humiditatis subiectum sit.

The Cartesians deny it. Rohault: liquid water is indifferent to heat and cold, and when heated water cools again on the fire, “ce n’est pas qu’elle ait aucune inclination particuliere à la froideur”, it is not that it has any particular inclination to coldness.R 171 Le Grand, against “some more recent philosophers”:

“It is not to be thought, with some more recent philosophers, that water is cold by its own nature”L 360

Non cum quibusdam recentioribus Philosophis existimandum est, Aquam natura sua frigidam esse

Frozen water feels cold only from the rest of its parts, in which, he promises to show later, the nature of cold consists.L 360 Goudin does not describe water’s own qualities in the sections read; the moon rules moist bodies, and so the sea, “the chief kingdom of moisture”.G 274

What makes water fluid

For Sperling liquidity follows moisture, of which water is the first subject; the heat of warm water belongs not to the water but to atoms of fire spread through it, which the water drives out again as it cools.S 570S 591S 594 For the Cartesians a subtle matter does the work. Rohault: if water is a heap of such little bodies, “il est certain qu’elle doit estre liquide”, it is certain it must be liquid, since the matter of the second element mixes and moves its delicate parts; where that matter lacks the force to keep them apart, the water freezes.R 170 Le Grand:

“So that it is no wonder if it makes water liquid, since it moves each of its particles separately and bends one into another.”L 359

Adeo ut mirum non sit, si aquam liquidam efficiat, cum singulas ejus particulas seorsim moveat, atque unam in alteram inflectat.

Where that force is hindered, by the sun’s distance in winter or toward the poles, the parts lie in disorder and make ice.L 359 Goudin does not take the question up.

What moves the tides

The two Cartesians make the moon press on the sea through the fluid heaven. Rohault: the fluid matter that whirls about the earth up to beyond the moon moves faster where the moon narrows its passage, so

“all the matter that moves about the earth must press it more at the place that lies under the moon”R 145

toute la matiere qui se meut autour de la terre, la doit plus presser à l'endroit qui correspond sous la Lune

and this pressure drives the waters from the equator toward the poles; as the earth turns, each part of the ocean comes under the moon, and opposite her, once a day each.R 144R 147 Le Grand draws the same figure: the moon narrows the space between itself and the earth, and “Cœlestis materia, quæ circa Terram circumvolvitur, celerius per illas angustias” interfluit, the celestial matter that whirls about the earth flows faster through those straits, here and on the opposite side, and presses the air and the sea toward the shores.L 363 He dismisses the occult quality that more recent philosophers put in its place:

“For what is occult lies hidden; and an effect is said to be unknown for as long as it is not demonstrated through its proper cause.”L 362

Quod enim occultum est, latet ; & tamdiu effectus aliquis ignorari dicitur, quamdiu per propriam causam non demonstratur.

The schoolmen keep the moon’s influx. Goudin: “Causam hujus motus communior sententia refert in influxum Lunæ”, the commoner opinion refers the cause of this motion to the influx of the moon, which rules moist bodies and so the sea.G 274 He sets aside the ancients who made the earth a great animal breathing out the tides and the “almost poetic fiction” of Plato (c. 428–348 BC), a whirlpool under the earth swallowing and vomiting the sea, and he denies that the moon makes the sea ferment and boil over: flood on one shore and ebb on the opposite shore at the same moment show a local motion.G 273G 274G 275 Sperling sides with Julius Caesar Scaliger (1484–1558), who confessed that he knew nothing of the matter and still referred it to the moon. The moon does not touch the waters, but the magnet moves iron without touching it:

“So as iron follows the magnet, water accompanies the motion of the moon.”S 587

Ut ergò ferrùm magnetem sequitur, sic aqua motum Lunæ comitatur.

Then, with Sebastian Basso (fl. 1621) and against Scaliger, he adds a second conclusion: “Causis externis addendæ veniunt internæ”, to the external causes internal ones must be added. Spirits in the sea kindle and spend themselves at fixed times, as matter does in an intermittent fever or wine working in a cask, and so the tide keeps its hours.S 585S 586S 587

How the sea water reaches the springs

The springs of the Seine stand far above the sea at Le Havre, and water does not climb above its own level; so something must lift it. Rohault rejects the sucking power some give the earth, since sucking needs motion in the sucker and a sponge lifts water only a little, and answers with distillation:R 275R 276

“it is reduced to vapours by the heat that is found in the bowels of the earth”R 277

elle est reduite en vapeurs par la chaleur qui se rencontre dans les entrailles de la Terre

The heat grows the deeper one goes; the vapours, hemmed in on every side, rise into the hollows of the mountains and condense on the cold parts near the surface.R 277R 278 Le Grand gives the same account in the same order, with the burning mountains Hecla, Vesuvius and Etna as proof of the fires and the chemist’s alembic as the model:

“It must therefore be held that the water, in the hollows and caverns into which it has been driven by its weight and fluidity, is resolved into vapours by the force of subterranean heat, and rises through the passages it finds everywhere.”L 341L 342

Censendum est igitur, aquam in specubus, & cavernis, ad quas gravitate, & fluiditate sua, adacta est, in Vapores vi caloris subterranei resolvi, & per meatus, quos ubique reperit, ascendere.

He too rejects suction as far from reason, since sucking supposes a motion in the body that sucks.L 341L 342 Sperling does not ask how the water climbs: from the sea the rivers rush through the veins of the earth like the blood of animals, now here, now there.S 575 Goudin, following Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), lets several causes work together: rivers swallowed by chasms and given back after long courses underground, a motion the heavens give the elements, spirits shut in the earth that drive the water up, as Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379) says, and the earth sucking water like a sponge, as the arteries draw blood.G 280G 281

Whether rain feeds the springs

Sperling denies it, with Lucius Annaeus Seneca (c. 4 BC–AD 65), who dug vineyards: “nullam pluviam esse tam magnam, quæ terram ultra decem pedes in altum madefaciat”, no rain is so great that it wets the earth more than ten feet deep. Yet wells dug two or three hundred feet in the driest places find rich veins, and rivers burst from bare rocks. Nor do rivers come from vapours condensed in the earth, for a perpetual flow needs perpetual causes.S 573S 574S 575 Goudin, to reconcile the opinions, makes rain one of three causes, with air that distils into drops in cold caverns, and the sea.G 278 Le Grand gives rain and melted snow the springs that run after long rains and fail in summer.L 342L 343 Rohault gives rain no part: the sea supplies them all.R 275

Why the springs are fresh

If the sea feeds the springs, the springs should be salt. Rohault answers from his distillation:

“salt does not rise in vapours with the parts of fresh water, it is easy to judge that the waters of springs and wells must be fresh”R 278

le sel ne s'éleve point en vapeurs avec les parties de l'eau douce, il est aisé à juger que les eaux des fontaines & des puys doivent estre douces

Le Grand gives the Cartesian reason for it: “Salsæ enim rigidæ sunt, & inflexiles, quæ in vapores mutari nequeunt”, the salt particles are rigid and inflexible and cannot be turned into vapour. He adds a filter as well: sea water percolated through much sand or earth puts off its rigid particles.L 343 The masses of salt found in some mountains are what the rising sea water left behind.L 344 Goudin has only the filter: springs that rise from the sea through long winding passages are cleansed of the burnt earthy matter that makes saltness, and where it is not separated enough the spring is salt.G 277 Sperling does not take the question up in his chapter, though he reports that hollow wax balls let down into the sea fill with fresh water, the finer water passing the narrow pores and the salt staying out.S 583

Why the sea is salt

Each text gives a different answer. Rohault: salt forms in quantity in the bowels of the earth, even far from the sea, and veins of water that communicate with the sea dissolve it and carry it in: “c’est dans la Mer que le Sel se doit principalement rencontrer”, it is in the sea that salt must chiefly be found.R 189R 190 He rejects the doctrine of Aristotle (384–322 BC):

“it is an error to assert with Aristotle that the saltness of the sea depends on its waters being burnt by the rays of the sun”R 190

c'est une erreur d'assurer avec Aristote, que la salure de la Mer dépend de ce que ses eaux sont brûlées par les rayons du Soleil

no one having found that the heat of the sun, or of a flame, turns fresh water into salt.R 190 Sperling rejects the same doctrine, “Solis ardor salsedinem mari non infert”, the sun’s heat does not bring saltness to the sea: salt-works would not be so rare and dear, and water boiled again and again over a kitchen fire would grow salt.S 583 His own answer is the creation:

“The efficient cause of the saltness of the sea is God. He made the sea salt in the first creation.”S 582

Efficiens causa salsedinis maris DEVS est. Hic in prima creatione mare creavit salsum.

for a twofold end, that it should be a fit home for fish and serve swimming and sailing.S 582 Goudin joins the creation to Aristotle: “Duplex hujus saporis causa assignari potest, una remotior, altera proxima”, a twofold cause of this taste can be given, one remote, the other nearest. The remote cause is the condition God gave the element at its creation, so that the greatest fish might be nourished and ships the better borne; the nearer is a dry, burnt earthy exhalation mixed with the water, and the dregs left when the subtle moisture evaporates turn to salt.G 276G 277 Le Grand rejects veins of salt, which the water would long ago have dissolved, and makes the sea its own salt-works:

“It must therefore be said that salt is generated in the sea by the force of heat.”L 360

Dicendum est igitur, salem in mari, vi caloris generari.

The sea grows no saltier over the ages because fresh water keeps flowing in and salt water out through the cavities of the earth.L 360

Why they differed

One mechanism, or many causes

The Cartesians explain everything in the chapter with the same few things, particles of given shapes and a subtle matter that moves them. The shape of water’s particles makes it fluid and tasteless, the shape of salt’s keeps it from rising in vapour and so makes the springs fresh, and the pressure of the fluid heaven makes the tides.R 170R 173R 278R 145 Rohault states the rule he works by: once the true cause of the tide is found, the same foundation must of necessity satisfy all the other circumstances.R 152 For Le Grand an occult quality is no cause at all, only an admission of ignorance.L 362 The schoolmen reason from what a thing is of itself. Sperling traces each property to what has it of itself: the heat of hot water to fire, saltness to the substance of salt, since no accident generates the substance of salt.S 583S 591 Goudin accepts that the moon acts by influx, and says that what disposition, place or aspect such an impression requires seems to pass human skill.G 276

Purposes and Scripture

The two schoolmen give God’s purposes as causes: the sea was made salt for the fish and for ships.S 582G 276 Sperling opens his questions with the waters gathered into one place on the third day, and both he and Le Grand cite the Preacher’s saying that all rivers run into the sea and the sea does not overflow; Le Grand uses it as a fact to explain, where Rohault makes the same argument from the rivers without Scripture.S 572S 573L 340R 275 Goudin brings in the Psalmist and the Preacher to confirm his conclusions.G 124G 279

A turning earth, or a resting one

The Cartesian tide needs the earth to turn: in Rohault the earth’s turning brings each part of the ocean under the moon once a day, and in Le Grand the earth turns on its centre in twenty-four hours while the moon’s place over the sea changes every six.R 147L 363 Goudin’s earth rests unmoved in the centre of the world, so the tide must follow the moon’s own daily course.G 125G 274

Questions the texts left open

  • Whether the sea is higher than the land. Goudin: it stands higher than the low parts of the earth, as Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379) says of the Red Sea and Egypt, but not above its own shores, for it is innate to water to level itself and flow down, and a sea heaped above the land would be in a state of violence; rivers run down to the sea, and ships leaving the shore do not climb.G 126G 127 The others do not take the question up.
  • Springs that ebb and flow. Le Grand: springs at Cadiz and Bordeaux rise and fall with the sea, and one in Spain the other way about, and “Causa talis æstus in Fontibus, non in aliud, quam in ipsum maris fluxum refundi potest”, the cause can be nothing but the sea’s own flood, forcing itself into channels under the earth.L 344 Goudin leaves three causes open: secret channels to the sea, periodic boilings of their own, or a disposition to receive the stars’ impression.G 275G 276 Sperling reports Seneca’s question why some springs are full for six hours and dry for six.S 588
  • A current in the sea. Le Grand: “Aquæ enim semper ab Oriente in Occidentem fluunt”, the waters always flow from east to west, since the voyage from Spain to the New World takes thirty days and the return three months.L 364L 365 Rohault explains a current that runs now south, now north by the rivers a sea receives and the south winds that hold it back.R 152
  • The sparks of the sea. Rohault: when the sea is much stirred in hot weather, “ses vagues produisent la nuit une infinité d’étincelles dans l’air”, its waves throw countless sparks into the air at night, from particles of salt that break free of the drops and dart out point first; old sea water and brine, whose parts are fouled, give none.R 181R 182
  • Whether the elements change into one another. Rohault’s flask of air, sealed and kept three years under water, showed no change, as it would if the elements changed into one another as certain philosophers claim.R 172R 173
