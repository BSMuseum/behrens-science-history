Natural philosophy

What was taught about nature in the courses of natural philosophy most reprinted in Latin Europe around 1575, topic by topic, from the teaching texts themselves.

Planned. The texts are chosen and the topic list is proposed; no entry is written yet.

The four texts

  • TFrance · LatinFrançois Titelmans OFM (1502–1537), Compendium philosophiae naturalis, seu de consideratione rerum naturalium (Lyon, 1574).
  • MProtestant German lands · LatinPhilipp Melanchthon (1497–1560), Initia doctrinae physicae, dictata in Academia Witebergensi (Wittenberg, 1575).
  • ToCatholic German lands · LatinFrancisco de Toledo SJ (1532–1596), Commentaria una cum quaestionibus in octo libros Aristotelis de physica auscultatione (Cologne, 1579).
  • SoIn place of the British Isles · LatinDomingo de Soto OP (1494–1560), Super octo libros Physicorum Aristotelis commentaria, third edition (Salamanca, 1582).

Each is the most-reprinted teaching course printed in its region between 1560 and 1590. No course of natural philosophy was printed twice in the British Isles in those years, so the slot passes to a region outside the four. Soto’s Salamanca course ties with a course from Louvain and is taken because it adds a religious order not yet represented. Two are whole courses (Titelmans, a Franciscan compendium; Melanchthon, the Lutheran course of Wittenberg); two are commentaries on Aristotle’s Physics (Toledo, a Jesuit; Soto, a Dominican).

The topics, as the textbooks divided them

The list is proposed from the divisions of the two whole courses: Titelmans’s twelve books and Melanchthon’s three. Roman numerals give Titelmans’s book and Melanchthon’s book, with his headings and pages; Toledo and Soto are cited by the book of the Physics. Places were read from running heads and chapter heads and are to be confirmed on the page images as each entry is written. “No section” means the course has none on the topic; “separate commentary” means the commentator treats it in a commentary on another book of Aristotle, not in this course.

Open points: whether Toledo’s and Soto’s commentaries on De caelo, De generatione and De anima count as parts of one course with their Physics; and, since Melanchthon has no meteors and no soul, whether the next Protestant German course in the count, the Epitome philosophiae naturalis of Georg Liebler (1524–1600), professor of physics at Tübingen, treats them; and which edition of Titelmans is read, since the Lyon 1574 scan holds only Books I–VII.

How the entries are made

Each entry reports what the teaching texts of one period said, in their own terms, and the evidence they gave. Where texts from different places disagree, the entry sets out each position and the reasons the texts themselves offer for the difference. Modern concepts appear only in a short table of terms at the end of each entry, so that a reader can connect an old name to a present one.

Every statement carries a reference to the printed page, linked to a public scan. Quotations are given in translation with the original beneath, and each has been checked against the page image. People are given their full names and years of birth and death on first mention.
