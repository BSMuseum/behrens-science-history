Snapshot c. 1775 · Natural philosophy

Earth and the bodies drawn from it

What teaching texts from Graz, Göttingen, Paris and London said between 1768 and 1778 about earths and stones, salts, inflammables and metals. They sort these bodies in much the same way. Three of them explain metals and their calces by phlogiston and teach that quicksilver can be frozen; the London text has no phlogiston, makes metals of mercury and sulphur, and says quicksilver cannot freeze. They differ too on how many kinds of earth there are, on whether quicksilver and platina are metals, on whether stones and metals grow in the earth, and on whether anyone can turn lead into gold.

First cut, from one text per region. Two of the four texts differ from the other entries: Nollet has no lesson on these bodies, so the Paris text is Para du Phanjas, and Erxleben treats them in his natural history, not his physics. Petrifactions, strata, mountains, earthquakes, volcanoes and the history of the earth are left to the entry on the terraqueous globe, and the magnet has its own entry. The British text is Martin’s Philosophical Grammar (London, 1778); the Britannica, the British text until 21 November 2026, is kept as a supplementary text.

Where the subject sat among the sciences

On this topic the four texts draw the edge of natural philosophy in different places. In Graz it is inside physics. Biwald gives it Part IV of his particular physics, between electricity and meteors, under the heading “Of earth in general, and the bodies arising from its various combination”. The magnet is the third article of that part, as an ore of iron.B 343B 353

In Göttingen it is outside. Erxleben’s Naturlehre has no place for earths, salts or metals, and his natural history treats the mineral kingdom as its last part. He arranged it, he says, on a quite different plan from the animal and vegetable kingdoms, because the mixtures of the minerals forced him to:

“I wish I could have mixed in still more chemistry, for mineralogy without chemistry is something I at least cannot conceive.”E pref.

Ich wünschte daß ich noch mehr Chemie hätte einmischen dürfen, denn Mineralogie ohne Chemie kann ich wenigstens mir nicht gedenken.

In Paris the course read for the other entries says nothing on the subject. Nollet’s six volumes have no lesson on mineral bodies. Para du Phanjas keeps them in physics: the third treatise of his course, the “Theory of the earth”, takes the globe first and then its animal, vegetable and mineral kingdoms.P ii 190 The metals also appear in his first volume, under chemical affinities.P i 143

In London they are inside natural philosophy. The fourth and last part of Martin’s Grammar, which he calls Geology, gives its second chapter to the inside of the globe, its strata, and the earthy bodies under seven heads; the loadstone closes the chapter as “this most wonderful of all Fossils”, and the shells found in the earth are the last head.M 236M 239M 255

The Britannica, a supplementary text here, puts these bodies in its treatise “Chemistry”. Earth is treated there among the principles of bodies, and then come the saline and the metallic substances.EB ii 67EB ii 77

What the texts taught in common

  • The bodies dug from the earth fall into a few classes, told apart by what fire, water and acids do to them. Biwald defines earth as a hard, friable body that resists fire and dissolves in neither air, water, spirit of wine nor oil. Stones are made of earth, and the other bodies found in the earth fall into four classes: salts, sulphurs, metals and semimetals.B 343B 347 Erxleben’s four classes are earths, salts, inflammable minerals and metals, and he adds that most mineral bodies contain particles of several classes at once.E 541–542 Para lists metals, stones, salts, sulphurs and bitumens as the fossils proper to the earth.P ii 190 Martin’s master reduces earthy bodies to seven heads: earths, ores, fossils (by which he means salts and sulphurs), minerals, metals, stones, and extraneous bodies such as shells. His minerals are fossils that neither burn, stretch nor melt and that fire turns to a calx; a metal melts in the fire and spreads under the hammer.M 239M 240M 245M 246 The Britannica’s chemistry, a supplementary text here, takes earth as a principle fixed in the fire, then treats the saline and the metallic substances.EB ii 67EB ii 77
  • A stone is an earth whose parts hold together more firmly. Stones, Biwald says, “are distinguished from earth itself only by density and a firmer binding of the parts”.B 345 Erxleben: no exact boundary can be drawn between earths and stones, which differ only in a very accidental circumstance.E 561 Para: “In general, stones differ from earths only in the hardness and the binding of their parts.”P ii 221 Martin does not compare the two. Of his stones, marble arises from “an earthy Juice” and flint from particles of sand compacted and hardened, but crystal, after Girolamo Cardano (1501–1576), from an aqueous substance.M 252M 253 The Britannica was not read on this point.
  • Gold and silver are unharmed by fire; the other metals are destroyed by it; the semimetals cannot be hammered. Biwald: the four other metals, “since they do not resist the action of fire with the same force as gold and silver, are called imperfect metals by some”.B 351 Erxleben calls the two noble metals and the rest base; the base metals are turned in the melting fire to a calx.E 552 Para’s perfect metals stay fixed in the longest and most violent fire without losing weight, while the imperfect ones, pushed too far, turn to calx or glass.P i 143P i 144 Martin agrees on the order but draws no line between two classes. Gold “is the most fixed and pure, or loses the least in the Fire, of any Bodies”, silver comes next, copper is more fixed than lead or tin, and tin is the least fixed of all, so that it “loses the most of its Weight”.M 247M 249M 251 The Britannica, a supplementary text: “The perfect metals are those which suffer no damage or change whatever by the most violent and most lasting action of fire.”EB ii 77
  • A metal that fire has turned to a calx becomes metal again when its phlogiston is given back. Biwald explains why the powder left from a metal is not counted as pure earth: “when phlogiston or an inflammable body is mixed with it, it is easily brought back again into metallic form”.B 345 Erxleben: malleability comes from the phlogiston in a metal, and a calx given its phlogiston back can be hammered again.E 548 Para: chemical fires take the metallic properties away by removing the phlogiston and give them back by restoring it.P ii 232 The Britannica, a supplementary text, calls this reducing or revivifying the metal. Martin’s Grammar has no phlogiston anywhere, and does not speak of bringing a calx back to metal.M 245EB ii 77

The disputes

How many kinds of earth are there?

Biwald follows the chemist Johann Heinrich Pott (1692–1777):

“The most celebrated Pott sets four kinds of earth”B 343

Terrarum quatuor genera statuit celeberrimus Pott

These are calcareous earth, which effervesces with acids and burns to lime; gypseous earth, which effervesces with no acid and does not burn to true lime; argillaceous earth, which resists acids, swells with water and hardens in the fire; and vitrescible earth, used for glass.B 343–344 Biwald then reports a shorter list. Christlieb Ehregott Gellert (1713–1795) of Freiberg allows only two kinds, argillaceous and alkaline or calcareous.B 344

Erxleben also has four, but not the same four. They are siliceous earth, clay, calcareous earth and alum earth, and he offers them as a probable list: “presumably the following are simple earths”.E 542 Gypsum is not one of them: vitriolic acid turns calcareous earth into gypsum.E 545

Para counts as many kinds of stone as there are kinds of earth, in five classes: clayey, calcareous, gypseous, fiery (they strike sparks, and include most of the vitrifiable stones) and fusible. The vitrifiable earth is “the purest, the most elementary”.P ii 221–223

Martin names no simple earths. His first head takes in “Clay, Lome, Marl, Sand”, and medicinal earths such as Terra Japonica, Lemnia and Armenia. Asked why they differ, the master says their first particles have the universal and specific properties of matter in different degrees, “and this is the most that can be known of this Matter.”M 240 The only elements he admits are those first particles.M 35

The Britannica, a supplementary text here, does not decide whether there is one earth or several. Earth is the principle that stays in the fire, but it is very hard, if not impossible, to obtain pure: the earths drawn from different bodies differ, and so either they are impure, “or else, if those earths be pure, we must allow them to be essentially different, seeing they have different properties.” For use it divides them into vitrifiable and unvitrifiable or absorbent earths, and allows that one kind may be beyond the reach of any fire.EB ii 67

Is quicksilver a metal?

Biwald counts six metals, gold, silver, copper, lead, tin and iron, and adds quicksilver after them with a note on its standing:B 350

“Hydrargyrum, or mercury, is commonly reckoned among the metals too by most, although it is a fluid body. It has a specific weight on account of which it seems it should be counted among the metals.”B 351

Hydrargirum, sive mercurius metallis quoque accenseri a plerisque solet, quamvis corpus fluidum sit. Gravitate specifica ea gaudet, ob quam metallis adnumerandum videtur.

Erxleben puts it among the base metals without comment: “To these belong the following five: quicksilver, lead, copper, iron and tin.”E 552 Para gives it a class of its own:

“Mercury, which has the two general and fundamental properties of metallic substances, seems to make a class apart by itself.”P i 144

Le mercure, qui a les deux propriétés générales & fondamentales des substances métalliques, semble faire seul une classe à part.

It is like the perfect metals in its purity and weight, he explains, like the imperfect ones in its volatility, and unlike both in how easily it melts.P i 144 Martin agrees:

“The Bodies to which this Definition belongs in every Part, are but six; viz. Gold, Lead, Silver, Copper, Iron and Tin; to which the Chymists add Mercury or Quicksilver, tho’ it seems to be of itself a peculiar Species of Simple Fossils.”M 246–247

He then gives its characters second, after gold’s.M 248 The Britannica, a supplementary text, agrees with Para and gives the reason:

“Some chemists admit a seventh metal, viz. quick-silver; but, as it is not malleable, it has been generally considered as a metallic body of a particular kind.”EB ii 78

Can quicksilver be frozen?

Three texts say it can, by the greatest cold. Biwald: only by the greatest degree of cold, which art can increase by pouring acids on crushed ice.B 351 Erxleben: it takes on its hardness “only in a very great cold”.E 552 Para gives the occasion: the experiments made at Petersburg in the cold of 25 December 1759, which left mercury solid, ductile and malleable.P i 144–145 Martin says it cannot. The seventh of his characters of mercury reads:

“It is yet, by Reason of its great Fluidity, incapable of congealing or freezing.”M 248

The list of characters stands unchanged in the 1762 printing of the Grammar (machine-read text). Not found in the parts of the Britannica read.

What is platina?

Platina was new to the textbooks, and three of them place it three different ways. Biwald mentions it once, among the bodies the magnet acts on: it is “a new kind of metal, not yet sufficiently known”.B 353 Erxleben makes it the first of his seven semimetals, white, brittle and very hard to melt, and much like gold, but he adds a doubt:

“It is, however, not yet quite certainly settled whether it is really a semimetal of its own.”E 555–556

Es ist indessen noch nicht ganz gewiß ausgemacht, ob sie wirklich ein eignes Halbmetall ist.

The works he cites call it the white gold, the seventh metal and the eighth.E 556 Para has no doubt: “Gold, silver and platina are the three perfect metals.”P i 143 Martin does not mention platina, nor do the parts of the Britannica read.

What are metals made of?

Martin gives the old answer of the chemists:

“The Elementary or Component Principles of Metals are reckoned two, viz. Mercury and Sulphur; Mercury as the Basis or Matter of the Metal; and Sulphur as the Binder or Cement, which renders it fixed and malleable.”M 251M 252

The mercury is common quicksilver at its purest. The sulphur is not common sulphur but “the Sulphur of Metal, which is supposed to be the Matter of Light or Fire”, and the different degrees of its union with the mercury make the different metals. He adds that a burning glass will separate “a vitrefiable Earth” from the most perfect of metals, without saying what that earth is to a metal.M 252 A note gives Boerhaave’s principles of all fossils: mercury, a subtle sulphur that fixes it, salt, and earth.M 246

The Britannica, a supplementary text here, answers in two steps. Metallic substances “consist chiefly of a vitrifiable earth united with the phlogiston.” Several chemists, it goes on, add a third principle, a mercurial earth, which according to Johann Joachim Becher (1635–1682) and Georg Ernst Stahl (1659–1734) is the same principle that, joined with vitriolic acid, makes the acid of sea salt:

“The existence of this principle hath not yet been demonstrated by any decisive experiment; but we shall shew that there are pretty strong reasons for admitting it.”EB ii 77

Erxleben teaches the same three parts, with the same hedge on the third:

“Chemistry teaches us further about them, and shows that they consist of an earth, the inflammable essence, and besides that, in all probability, of a so-called mercurial essence (principium mercuriale, arsenicale), which last may be what properly first makes them metals.”E 547

Die Chemie belehrt uns in Ansehung ihrer weiter, und zeigt, daß sie aus einer Erde, brennbarem Wesen, und ausserdem aller Wahrscheinlichkeit nach noch aus einem sogenannten mercurialischen Wesen (principium mercuriale, arsenicale) bestehen, welches letztere sie eigentlich wohl erst zu Metallen machen möchte.

Biwald reports the doctrine as the chemists’ and gives the last word to an objection. The chemists set three further kinds of earth, mercurial, inflammable and vitrescent:

“They think these the principles of all metals. But the most learned Boerhaave contends at length that these kinds of earth are not found in metals, however divided or dissolved.”B 345

Has omnium metallorum principia esse existimant. Verum multis contendit Doctissimus Boerhaave, in metallis qualibet ratione divisis, aut solutis has terrarum species haud reperiri.

The objector is Herman Boerhaave (1668–1738). Biwald says no more. Para names only two parts. The imperfect metals and the semimetals are mixtures that fire takes apart, and “these constituent parts are chiefly both phlogiston and elementary earth”.P i 145–146 The perfect metals may be the same, though no furnace can show it:

“It is not improbable that the perfect metals too have chiefly for constituent parts phlogiston and elementary earth, which the action of the hottest furnaces cannot disunite and decompose.”P i 146

Il n’est pas improbable que les métaux parfaits aient aussi principalement pour parties constituantes, le phlogistique & la terre élémentaire, que l’action des fourneaux les plus ardens ne peut désunir & décomposer.

Are stones and metals still being made in the earth?

Martin says yes, and that they grow:

“A. Do Minerals, Metals, Stones, &c. grow in the Earth? B. Undoubtedly: Yea, it is well known that divers Mines, when emptied of Stone, Metal, &c. have, after a while, recruited again.”M 257

Stony icicles, he goes on, may be seen daily engendered from petrifying juices in great caves, as he has seen himself at Okey Hole in Somerset, and mountains seem designed “as Matrices for the Generation and Maturation of Minerals and Metals”.M 257 The earth was enriched with “the Seeds” of the metals “No doubt by the all-wise Creator, for the manifold Uses of Mankind.”M 240 Marble arises from an earthy juice “concocted, and digested in the great Laboratory of the Earth”.M 252

Erxleben first rules out the picture Martin uses. Minerals are not organized, so there is no generation among them, “still less can one think of a seed or anything like it”. All the same, he says,

“what daily experience teaches cannot be doubted, that new minerals really are always arising and perishing”E 567–568

so kann doch das nicht in Zweifel gezogen werden, was die tägliche Erfahrung lehrt, daß neue Mineralien wirklich beständig entstehen und untergehen

Water dissolves minerals, carries them and lets them fall again as dripstone and crystals; air weathers them; underground fire turns them into lava and slag.E 569–572 For the metals he offers a guess: the mineral vapours of the mines, the Schwaden, “perhaps contribute most to the generation of the metals”, mixing their parts and settling them into softer stones, which then become their matrices.E 572

Para agrees that minerals do not grow. A mineral has nothing in common with the organization of animals and plants and is formed “by coagulation and by juxtaposition, without any intrinsic growth”.P ii 190 But he divides the minerals in two. The destructible ones, imperfect metals, semimetals, stones and the inflammable fossils, are being destroyed and renewed all the time by the fires and solvents under the earth, as the lavas of volcanoes show.P ii 232–233 Gold, silver and platina are not. Underground fires are no stronger than the chemist’s furnaces, and those have never decomposed these metals:

“Therefore it is likely that all the parts of these minerals that exist today have existed since the beginning of time.”P ii 231

Donc il est vraisemblable que toutes les parties de ces minéraux, qui existent aujourd’hui, ont existé dès le commencement des tems.

New masses of gold can still gather, but only from old particles carried and heaped together by fluids; the gold worn off furniture returns to the earth and stays “a molecule of gold”.P ii 230P ii 232 Para calls the question curious and hard to resolve, since only experience could decide it, and offers his answer as conjecture.P ii 230

Biwald gives a sentence to stones and none to metals. Stones “can be produced from earthy particles growing together and firmly joined by the help of some fluid, which is commonly called the petrifying juice”, or sometimes without it, when the particles change their shape and distances. A fuller account, he says, would take more room than his book allows.B 347 Stalactites form in caves as water laden with calcareous earth evaporates.B 345–346 The Britannica, a supplementary text, was not found to take up the question in the parts read.

Can one metal be turned into another?

Para takes up the old question of alchemy and says no, and so does the Britannica, a supplementary text here. Para draws the answer from his account of the elements, which no created agent can cut or wear. He calls the seekers of the philosopher’s stone men of little light or reason, who spend their time and fortune on it.P i 166 If the elements of other bodies could be given the mass and shape of the elements of gold, they would be gold; but centuries of failure show that this is not given to men, and the order of nature forbids it:

“the stability of nature, a stability decreed by the Creator, necessarily requires that such a metamorphosis be absolutely impossible to all the efforts of created agents”P i 167

la stabilité de la nature, stabilité décernée par le Créateur, exige nécessairement qu’une telle métamorphose soit absolument impossible à tous les efforts des agens créés

The Britannica disposes of the subject in its definition of alchemy:

“ALCHEMY, that branch of chemistry which had for its principal objects the transmutation of metals into gold; the panacea, or universal remedy; an alkahest, or universal menstruum; an universal ferment; and many other things equally ridiculous.”EB i 77

Neither Biwald nor Erxleben was found to take up the question in the parts read. Martin’s Grammar does not mention transmutation, alchemy or the philosopher’s stone.

Why they differed

Chemistry as the key

Erxleben and the Britannica both rest the subject on chemical analysis. Erxleben:

“Only the examination of the mixture of the minerals (§665) leads to the practical, useful knowledge of minerals that alone deserves the name, to which chemistry consequently gives the only key.”E 541

Nur die Untersuchung der Mischung der Mineralien (§. 665) führt zu der pragmatischen nützlichen Mineralkenntniß, die man eigentlich nur allein Kenntniß der Mineralien nennen darf, zu welcher folglich die Chemie den einzigen Schlüssel abgiebt.

Outward shape, the lie of the particles and colour, he adds, are often quite accidental in minerals.E 541 The Britannica’s chemistry begins by saying that analysis is finite and stops at substances it cannot change. Earth, water, air and fire are the chief of these. They are perhaps not the simplest elements, but it is more reasonable to take them as the principles than to spend the mind on conjectures about their parts.EB ii 66 That is why it leaves the number of earths open: analysis gives earths that differ, and it cannot tell whether they differ in kind.EB ii 67

The same caution decides their answer on what metals are made of. The Britannica’s reason for a third principle is a failure: phlogiston gives the form of a metal only to earths that were once part of a metal, and “a compound cannot be made with the phlogiston and sand that shall have the least resemblance of a metal”. Something besides earth and phlogiston must therefore stay with a metal’s calx.EB ii 77 Erxleben gives no reason on his page and cites a Leipzig programme asking whether arsenic is the first principle of metals.E 547 Biwald sets against the chemists Boerhaave’s report that the three earths are not found when metals are taken apart.B 345

An older chemistry in London

Martin’s chapter stands apart from the other three on every point where they rely on recent chemistry. It has no phlogiston, makes metals of mercury and sulphur, lets minerals and metals grow from seeds in their mountain matrices, and gives quicksilver a character, “incapable of congealing or freezing”, that the other three texts contradict.M 248M 251M 257 The chapter is not new in 1778. It already stands, these passages included, in the printing of 1762 (machine-read text), and the title page’s “very large Additions, by Way of Notes” did not change it. For more on metals and minerals its notes send the reader to Boerhaave’s chemistry and to the natural history of John Woodward (1665–1728).M 255 Its reasons are of an older kind too. For growth it gives the mines that fill again and the dripstone at Okey Hole; for the seeds of metals, the Creator’s provision “for the manifold Uses of Mankind”.M 240M 257 Erxleben names the seed only to rule it out: minerals are not organized, so there is no generation among them, still less a seed.E 567

A trial in the college museum

Biwald and Erxleben both reject a claim of Johann Heinrich Gottlob von Justi (1717–1771), on different matters. Biwald tested his:

“The celebrated Justi does claim that gypseous earth effervesces with acids; but various specimens of gypsum kept in the physics museum of the College of Graz, tried with acid liquors of every kind, showed no trace of effervescence.”B 344

Contendit quidem Cl. Iusti, terram gypseam cum acidis effervescere, verum specimina gypsi varia, quae in Musaeo physico Graecensis Collegii asservantur, omnis generis liquoribus acidis explorata, nullum effervescentiae vestigium praebuere.

The test matters to his list, since Pott’s gypseous earth is defined as an earth that does not effervesce. Erxleben dismisses Justi’s claim that an alkaline salt mineralizes the metals in their ores: “Herr von Justi’s new mineralizing agent, the alkaline salt, is a nonsense.”E 565

The stability of nature

Para’s answers on formation and on transmutation come from one premise: the first elements of bodies are indestructible, by the Creator’s decree, and no fire or solvent can change them.P i 166–167 The same premise rules out any mechanical account of how the globe was first made:

“That is, it seems to me, to abuse physics, which ought no more to seek a mechanism in the first forming of the terrestrial globe than in the first forming of air, water, light, or the heavenly globes”P ii 229

C’est, ce me semble, abuser de la physique, laquelle ne doit pas plus chercher de méchanisme dans la formation primitive du globe terrestre, que dans la formation primitive de l’air, de l’eau, de la lumiere, des globes célestes

From the start, he goes on, the globe had all the kinds of earths, metals, stones, plants and animals it has now, and physics asks only what changes its laws have since worked on them.P ii 229 His argument that no new gold forms rests on a comparison: the fires and solvents under the earth are no stronger than the chemist’s, which have never decomposed gold.P ii 230–231 Erxleben rests his opposite answer on observation: the daily experience of minerals forming and decaying.E 567–568

Questions the texts left open

  • Whether the common earths come from living things. Erxleben thinks calcareous earth “perhaps owes its origin to shellfish and corals alone”. Clay, after Georges-Louis Leclerc, comte de Buffon (1707–1788), comes from the destruction of siliceous earth, after others from plants dissolved by putrefaction, and alum earth perhaps also from plants.E 543E 544 Para notes that some naturalists derive all the calcareous stones from heaps of petrified shells. He thinks it more natural to say that the shells are made of the same substance that forms calcareous stones in the earth.P ii 222
  • Whether chalk comes from flint. Erxleben asks, and does not answer: “Does chalk perhaps also arise from flint by weathering? Or does chalk pass with time into flint?”E 572
  • Whether borax is natural or made. Biwald: brought chiefly from Egypt and refined in Holland, it is still uncertain to physicists whether it is a work of nature or of art; recent reports say art.B 349 Martin has no doubt: borax is “a green-coloured mineral Salt, found in the Veins of Brass, Silver, or Gold Ore”.M 242
  • Whether a rod can find a mine. Para: “Charlatans and the ignorant look for mines by means of the divining rod: a chimerical means, no fitter for discovering mines and springs than for making known thieves and receivers of stolen goods.” Physicists suspect a mine from spar or quartz on the ground and sickly plants, and only digging decides.P ii 192
  • Whether gold heals. Para: no medical virtue has yet been found in gold. “Potable gold may brighten a colour, but it can bring no relief to the sick man who swallows it”, since nothing in the body can dissolve it.P ii 196
