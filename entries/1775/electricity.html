Snapshot c. 1775 · Natural philosophy

Electricity

What teaching texts from Graz, Göttingen, Paris and London told their readers about electricity between 1768 and 1776: what they agreed on, where they split, and the reasons they gave. Three of them teach an electric fluid; the British course still treats electricity as a kind of attraction shown by a rubbed glass tube.

First cut, from one text per region. The views of Jean-Antoine Nollet (1700–1770) and Benjamin Franklin (1706–1790) appear here only as these four texts report them.

The four texts

  • BGraz · LatinLeopold Biwald SJ (1731–1805), professor of physics at the University of Graz, Physica particularis (Graz, 1768). Electricity is Part III, Section III, pp. 314–343.
  • EGöttingen · GermanJohann Christian Polykarp Erxleben (1744–1777), Anfangsgründe der Naturlehre (Göttingen and Gotha, 1772). Tenth section, “Von der Elektricität”; theory at §§522–533.
  • SParis · FrenchJoseph-Aignan Sigaud de la Fond (1730–1810), Traité de l’électricité (Paris, 1776). A whole volume on the subject.
  • FLondon · EnglishJames Ferguson (1710–1776), Lectures on Select Subjects in Mechanics, Hydrostatics, Pneumatics, and Optics, 5th ed. (London, 1776). The passage headed “Electricity” at the end of Lecture I, pp. 18–19, with the four kinds of attraction, p. 6, and the passage headed “Thunder and lightning” in the lecture on pneumatics, pp. 176–177.

Supplementary. EB Encyclopædia Britannica, 1st ed. (Edinburgh, 1771), vol. 2, the treatise “Electricity”, from p. 471. Unsigned. Until 21 November 2026 this entry’s British text was the Britannica; under the project’s rule for choosing texts an encyclopedia does not fill a region’s place, and Ferguson’s lectures, printed eight times between 1760 and 1790, are the most-reprinted British course and give electricity a passage under its own heading. Unlike most of the Britannica’s physics, its “Electricity” is not Ferguson’s: it shares no wording with his lectures or with his separate Introduction to Electricity (1770). Its treatise “Mechanics”, which reprints Ferguson’s first lecture, prints the first sentence of his passage on electricity and then sends the reader to the treatise.

References give the text and its printed page, for example B 318. Linked references open the scan of that page. Quotations are given in translation, with the original beneath.

Where electricity sat among the sciences

Biwald divides particular physics into six parts: water, air, light and fire, earth, meteors, and the terraqueous globe. Electricity is the third section of the part on light and fire, after fire and after light. The magnet is treated separately, under earth, and lightning under meteors.B Conspectus Erxleben gives electricity a section of its own, the tenth, following his section on fire. Sigaud gives it a volume. The Britannica, which digests each science into a separate treatise, makes electricity one of them.EB 471 Ferguson has no lecture on electricity. A short passage headed “Electricity” closes his first lecture, on the properties of matter: electrical attraction is the last of four kinds of attraction, after cohesion, gravitation and magnetism, and attraction “seems rather to be infused than inherent” in matter.F 6F 18–19 Thunder and lightning come in his lecture on pneumatics.F 176–177 His separate Introduction to Electricity (1770), advertised at the front of the lectures with sections on the electrical machine, on preserving buildings from damage by lightning, and on medical electricity, is a treatise on one subject and does not fill a place under the project’s rule.F adv.

What the texts taught in common

Electricity is the action of a very subtle fluid

The Britannica opens: the word electricity “signifies, in general, the effects of a very subtile fluid matter, different in its properties from every other fluid we are acquainted with.”EB 471 Biwald states it as his first proposition and proves it from the senses. The fluid is felt, like the finest wool laid round an electrified body. It is smelled, with an odour like spirit of vitriol or phosphorus. It is tasted as sour and somewhat astringent. It is seen as a bright flame leaving the corners and points of metals. It is heard when a spark is drawn.B 318–319 Erxleben places the cause in a much finer, fluid and elastic matter that pervades the interstices of all bodies. A body is electrified when this matter gathers in and around it and forms an electric atmosphere.E 422–423 Ferguson speaks of no fluid. Amber, glass, jet, sealing-wax, agate and almost all precious stones “have a peculiar property of attracting and repelling light bodies when heated by rubbing. This is called electrical attraction.” Effluvia appear only in the last of his seven observations, as what snaps against a finger brought near the rubbed tube, with “a little flash of light” in the dark.F 18–19

The texts that raise the question say that its nature is not yet known; Ferguson does not raise it.S xvii

“To account for electrical phenomena, natural philosophers have almost unanimously had recourse to the action of some most subtle fluid; how that fluid acts, and what its nature is, all agree is not so easy to say.”B 318

Ut phaenomenorum electricorum rationem redderent Physici, omnes fere unanimi sensu ad fluidi cuiuspiam subtilissimi actionem recurrerunt, qua porro ratione id fluidum agat, & quae sit eiusdem indoles, id quidem non adeo pronum dictu esse, unanimi omnes sensu profitentur.

The Britannica agrees: “As we are entirely ignorant of the nature of the electrical fluid, it is impossible to define it but by its principal properties.”EB 471

Bodies fall into two classes

Some bodies can be made electric by rubbing, striking or heating: glass, gems, hard resins, sulphur, wax, silk, dry wood, hair and feathers. Biwald calls them idioelectrica, electric of themselves; the Britannica calls them “electrics, or non conductors”. Others show electricity only when it is communicated to them: metals, water, everything moist, the bodies of animals. Biwald calls these symperielectrica.B 315–316EB 471 Erxleben explains the difference: electric bodies combine with the electric matter only with difficulty, but once they have done so they hold it fast; unelectric bodies take it up easily and do not hold it.E 423 Ferguson names only the first class. Two of his observations point to the second without naming it: a tube rubbed by a moist hand loses its electricity, and any body but air, put between, stops it.F 19

The machine

By now-established practice a globe or cylinder of glass is turned quickly on its axis against a leather cushion. A metal rod or chain close to the globe, hung on silk threads or set on glass or pitch so that it touches no other body, collects the electricity and then shows every sign of it: it draws and repels light bodies, glows bluish in the dark, and gives a stinging spark to a finger.B 314–317EB 477 Ferguson’s lectures still use a glass tube “about an inch and a half diameter, and two or three feet long”, rubbed by hand, and leave the machine to his separate treatise.F 19

The shock from coated glass

A glass vessel coated inside and out with metal or filled with water can be charged so that a person touching both coatings feels a violent blow through the arms and chest. The texts disagree about whose discovery it was. Biwald credits Pieter van Musschenbroek (1692–1761) at Leiden and calls it the Leiden experiment.B 330–331 Sigaud also credits Musschenbroek, on the evidence of his letter to René-Antoine Ferchault de Réaumur (1683–1757), while noting that some gave the honour to “M. Cuneus, Bourgeois de Leyde”, that is, Andreas Cunaeus (1712–1788).S 197–198 The Britannica says it “was first discovered by Mr Cunæus, a native of Leyden”.EB 477 Erxleben calls it “the Kleistian experiment”, after Ewald Georg von Kleist (1700–1748).E 427

Electricity acts on living bodies

The discharge shakes the limbs, injures the small nerves, and often kills small birds. Biwald adds that it can sometimes help the sick, where the fluid of the nerves needs to be moved more strongly, stagnant humours dispersed, or the nerve fibres roused.B 334–335 The Britannica ends its treatise by proposing a room in which a patient could sit, read or sleep while gently electrified, from which “good effects are at least possible, if not highly probable.”EB, final paragraph One medical claim is reported as tested and failed. It had been said that the virtues of drugs sealed inside the glass travel out with the electric matter and purge the patient. Nollet went to Turin and Venice to see it and found nothing that answered his expectation.B 335 Sigaud remarks that Nollet had every interest in the claim being true, since it would have lent weight to his own system.S 388–389

Ferguson’s lectures say nothing of the charged bottle or of electricity and living bodies. Medical electricity is the last of the six sections of his separate treatise.F adv.

The disputes

Erxleben tells his students that he has set out “two theories for explaining the phenomena of electricity that have attracted particular attention.”E 429 They are Franklin’s and Nollet’s. Ferguson takes no part in the dispute. He names neither system, and says nothing of positive and negative electricity; Nollet appears in his preface only among those who have lectured on experimental philosophy.F 18–19

Franklin’s system

Every body has a certain quantity of the electric fluid proper to it, and with that quantity it is in its natural state. If more is gathered in or around it, the body is electric positively, or by excess. If part is taken away, it is electric negatively, or by defect. The phenomena come from the disturbed equilibrium: when bodies in different states are brought near, the fluid rushes from one to the other to restore it.B 320, 322EB 477 Glass does not let the fluid through. In the charged bottle the inner surface has gained exactly what the outer surface has lost, and the shock is the fluid passing by the shortest road from the one to the other.B 328–334

Nollet’s system

“Nollet supposes that electric matter goes out of the electrified body on all sides and spreads into the air and the neighbouring bodies; and that electric matter in turn presses from the air and the neighbouring bodies on all sides into the electrified body; the one stream causes the repulsion, the other the attraction.”E 425

Nollet nimmt an, es gehe aus dem elektrisirten Körper nach allen Seiten elektrische Materie heraus und verbreite sich in der Luft und den benachbarten Körpern; und wiederum dringe aus der Luft und den benachbarten Körpern elektrische Materie nach allen Seiten in den elektrisirten Körper hinein; der eine Strom verursache das Zurückstossen, der andere das Anziehen.

On this view the shock of the bottle is two opposed streams meeting in the person’s body.E 427 The electricity of resin is the same as that of glass, only weaker.E 429 The electric matter is the same thing as elementary fire and the matter of light.E 423 Sigaud calls it the “system of affluences”.S 389

Is lightning electricity?

Biwald says it is. An insulated, pointed iron rod set up in a high place gives all the signs of electricity when storms are near, and sometimes at other times. Biwald concludes that the atmosphere itself is electric, sometimes positively and sometimes negatively, and that this natural electricity far exceeds what a machine can raise.B 336–338 The Britannica lists the likenesses point by point: lightning takes a crooked path, strikes the highest and most pointed objects, follows the best conductors, burns, and sometimes dissolves metals, and so does electricity.EB, “Of the Similarity between Lightning and Electricity”

Ferguson says it is not. Under the heading “Thunder and lightning” in his lecture on pneumatics, the effluvia of sulphureous and nitrous bodies risen into the air “will ferment with each other, and take fire very often of themselves”, as spirit of nitre and oil of cloves give a sudden flame when mixed. Electricity is not mentioned.F 176–177

Erxleben and Sigaud were not read on lightning for this entry. Erxleben’s section on meteors makes it electricity; see the entry on meteors, where the question is taken up for all four regions.

The Britannica, now a supplementary text, teaches Franklin’s system only: he is named thirteen times in the machine-read text of its treatise, and Nollet not at all. It accepts positive and negative electricity, crediting “Dr Watson and Dr Franklin”, the first being William Watson (1715–1787); it calls the fluid “different in its properties from every other” and yet “the electrical fire”; it gives the charged bottle to “Mr Cunæus, a native of Leyden”; and it sets lightning and the electric spark side by side, likeness by likeness.EB 471, 477

Why they differed

The deciding experiments were disputed as facts

Nollet reported that a small electrified leaf of metal, brought near the outside of a charged bottle, is steadily repelled. If so, the outside cannot be in the negative state Franklin gives it. Sigaud, who already accepted negative electricity, says he was astonished and at first did not think to try it himself:

“I did not dare suspect his understanding or his good faith, and persuaded on his word of the truth of the fact he announces, I racked my mind to reconcile it with the preceding phenomena, which I regarded as equally certain.”S 248

Je n’osois soupçonner son intelligence ni sa bonne foi, & persuadé sur sa parole, de la vérité du fait qu’il annonce, je me mis l’esprit à la torture, pour le concilier avec les phénomènes précédents, que je regardois comme aussi certains.

After several days he repeated the experiment. The leaf first flies to the belly of the bottle, touches it, and only then is repelled, and the repulsion lasts longer than the attraction. He supposes that Nollet attended only to the repulsion, and argues that a leaf repelled after contact is one more proof for Franklin, since it has then taken the state of the surface it touched.S 248–249

The direction of the fluid could not be seen

Franklin’s system needs the fluid to move from one named body to another. Beccaria read the direction from the shape of the light: a diverging brush where the fluid leaves a point, a small star where it enters. Biwald, who teaches Beccaria’s system, reports the argument and then concedes that the inference is not safe:

“It must be frankly admitted, however, that the direction of the rays of light observed in those brushes or little stars is not so certain and distinct that the direction of the electric fluid can safely be inferred from it.”B 325

Verum fatendum omnino est, directionem radiorum lucis, quae in illis sive floccis, sive stellulis observatur, haud adeo certam, ac distinctam esse, ut directio fluidi electrici tuto inde erui queat.

Erxleben weighs the same kind of evidence both ways. For Franklin: an insulated man becomes electrified when he rubs the globe while the conductor is not insulated. Against him: the brushes of fire seem to come out of the unelectrified point held toward the electrified conductor.E 426 He adds that Franklin himself does not venture to decide whether the conductor is electrified positively or negatively.E 427

Each system explained something the other could not

Erxleben’s test case against Nollet is a conductor lying insulated between a glass globe and a sulphur globe, both rubbed at once. It is never electrified.

“On Nollet’s theory this is incomprehensible, but on the Franklinian it can be explained very well. One of the two electricities is positive, the other negative; which is positive and which negative is not known; but the one cancels the other.”E 429

Nach Nollets Theorie ist das unbegreiflich, aber nach der franklinischen kann man es gar wohl erklären. Die eine von beyden Elektricitäten ist positiv, die andere negativ; welche von beyden positiv und welche negativ ist, weiß man nicht; aber die eine hebt die andere auf.

Of Nollet’s two streams he exclaims “how incomprehensible, how forced!” How does the air take up the outgoing matter so easily and give back the incoming? Can the two streams coexist at all?E 425 But he also finds a hole in Franklin: “why does the electric matter of the positively electrified body drive back the electric matter in the body approaching it?”E 427 Sigaud, from the other side, defends Franklin on the bottle and still judges Nollet’s the better account of electrical phenomena in general:

“I admit in good faith that I know of none that is simpler, more satisfying, or that accounts more conveniently for electrical phenomena than the one he proposed. If it were time to take a side in this matter, I believe I should embrace that of the Abbé Nollet.”S xxi

je conviens de bonne foi, que je n’en connois aucun qui soit plus simple, plus satisfaisant, & qui rende plus commodément raison des phénomènes électriques, que celui qu’il a proposé. S’il étoit tems de prendre un parti à cet égard, je croirois devoir embrasser celui de l’Abbé Nollet.

The systems rested on different ideas of how matter acts

In Nollet’s system, as Erxleben reports it, streams of electric matter cause the attraction and the repulsion, and their meeting is the blow felt in the shock. Biwald rejects that kind of cause. In the preface to his general physics he says that in theory he follows Isaac Newton (1642–1727) and, after him, Father Roger Joseph Boscovich (1711–1787). He gives the electric fluid attractive and repulsive forces: its larger molecules repel one another, its smaller portions attract, and different bodies attract or repel it in different degrees.B gen. pref.B 340 Light bodies approach an electrified body whether it is positive or negative, as the fluid passes one way or the other and action equals reaction. From this he concludes:

“Hence it is clear that the electric fluid does not act by impulse, or in the manner of a wind pressing on bodies; for then light bodies would have to be repelled by positively electric bodies and attracted by negatively electric ones.”B 341

Hinc porro elucet, fluidum electricum haud agere impulsu, aut more venti corpora urgentis; tum enim fieri sane deberet, ut corpora levia a positive electricis repellantur, a negative vero electricis attrahantur.

Standing and first commitments

Sigaud’s own explanation of Nollet’s resistance is about the man. He calls him one of the most celebrated physicists France has had, and continues:

“Prejudiced against the singularity of Franklin’s opinion, and seduced by a few experiments that seemed to demonstrate the contrary, the Abbé Nollet was the greatest antagonist of Franklinism, and he never ceased to refute it, despite the multitude of experiments, each more luminous than the last, that several celebrated physicists set against him at various times.”S xx

Prévenu contre la singularité de l’opinion de Franklin, & séduit par quelques expériences qui sembloient démontrer le contraire, l’Abbé Nollet fut le plus grand antagoniste du Franklinisme, & il ne cessa de le réfuter, malgré la multitude d’expériences plus lumineuses les unes que les autres, que plusieurs célèbres Physiciens lui opposèrent en différents temps.

The lesson he draws for his readers is to examine for oneself the experiments that seem most decisive for one’s own opinion, “since the greatest men often fall into error when they attach themselves with too much confidence to their first ideas.”S xx–xxi

Questions the texts left open

  • What the fluid is. “Here we ought to confess openly that very much concerning the nature of this fluid is still unknown to us,” writes Biwald, and he expects later experiments to bring it to light.B 339
  • Whether electric and magnetic matter are related. Sigaud reports that they are almost generally held to be wholly different, following Musschenbroek, but that the experiments of Thomas-François Dalibard (1709–1778) point to a marked analogy. He repeated both sets of experiments, both succeeded, and he declines to choose. He also reports that lightning has reversed the needles of ships’ compasses and magnetized knives.S 323–326 Ferguson reports the same effects of lightning, but since lightning for him is burning effluvia, they say nothing to him about electricity.F 177
  • Where the atmosphere gets its electricity. Some say from the rubbing of masses of air driven by winds. Biwald doubts it, because that rubbing happens in every season and yet no atmospheric electricity is observed in hard winter cold. He thinks it more likely that the atmosphere draws the fluid from the earth, carried up with vapours in summer heat.B 338–339
  • Bodies that do not fit the two classes. The stone called tourmaline becomes electric merely by being heated, and its two sides seem to have opposite electricities.E 430–431 Biwald adds a fish, the gymnotus, which recent writers on natural history say is electric at all times while living in water.B 316
