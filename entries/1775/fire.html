Snapshot c. 1775 · Natural philosophy

Fire, warmth and cold

What teaching texts from Graz, Göttingen, Paris and London said fire and heat are, between 1765 and 1778. Paris teaches a matter of fire and Graz a motion. London makes heat a motion and fire a substance made of the same particles as light. Göttingen leaves the question open.

First cut, from one text per region, on the nature of fire and heat. Cold, flame, and the measurement of heat are not yet covered.

The four texts

  • BGraz · LatinLeopold Biwald SJ (1731–1805), Physica particularis (Graz, 1768). Part III, section I, “De igne”; the nature of fire in article IV, pp. 138–151.
  • EGöttingen · GermanJohann Christian Polykarp Erxleben (1744–1777), Anfangsgründe der Naturlehre (Göttingen and Gotha, 1772). Ninth section, “Von der Wärme und Kälte”; the nature of fire at §§449–451.
  • NParis · FrenchJean-Antoine Nollet (1700–1770), Leçons de physique expérimentale, vol. 4 (Paris, 1765). Lesson XIII.
  • MLondon · EnglishBenjamin Martin (1705–1782), The Philosophical Grammar; being a view of the present state of experimented physiology, or natural philosophy, “eighteenth edition” (London, 1778). A dialogue between a pupil and his master. The passage headed “Of Heat, Cold, &c.” in the chapter on consistence and fluidity, heat and cold, pp. 114–116, with rarefaction, p. 111, fluidity, p. 113, and the elements, p. 35.

Supplementary. EB Encyclopædia Britannica, 1st ed. (Edinburgh, 1771), vol. 2: the entries “Fire” and “Heat”, and “Of Fire” in the treatise “Chemistry”, pp. 67–68. Until 21 November 2026 this entry’s British text was the Britannica. Under the project’s rule for choosing texts, an encyclopedia does not fill a region’s place. Ferguson’s Lectures on Select Subjects, the most-reprinted British course, give fire and heat no passage of their own; their margin headings touch heat only at “The expansion of metals” (p. 15) and “The thermometer” (p. 161). The place passes to Martin’s Grammar, the next British course in the count that treats the topic under its own heading. The Britannica’s passages on fire are its own and do not reprint Martin’s.

Where fire sat among the sciences

Biwald treats fire in the same part of physics as light, and before it. Erxleben’s section is headed “Of warmth and cold” and comes between light and electricity. Martin counts heat and cold among the qualities of bodies, with light and colours, density, hardness and fluidity, and treats them in one chapter with fluidity, moisture and elasticity.M 35M 113 He keeps no element of fire: the ancients counted seven elements, fire among them, but in reality there are none other than the first particles of matter.M 35 The Britannica, a supplementary text here, divides the subject three ways. Its entry “Fire” distinguishes celestial, subterraneous and culinary fire. Its entry “Heat” makes heat a quality of bodies produced by fire, and in us a sensation. And its treatise on chemistry counts fire among the elements of which bodies are composed.EB, “Fire”, “Heat”; 67

What the texts taught in common

  • Heat can be raised anywhere. By rubbing and striking, by fermentation and putrefaction, by mixing certain liquors, by the access of air, and by gathering the sun’s rays into a small space.B 144E 358
  • Heat expands bodies, though by no settled rule: not in proportion to their weight, firmness or hardness. Martin: heat rarefies bodies “by dividing, extending, and expanding the Particles”, and cold condenses them.B 127M 111
  • A burning body is carried off. Smoke and flame are the parts of the burning body itself in very rapid motion; chimney soot yields water, oils, spirits, salts and earth on analysis.B 145

The dispute: a matter or a motion

Nollet puts the question as it stood: whether fire “is a simple, unalterable matter, destined to produce by its presence or its action heat, burning and the dissolution of bodies; or whether its essence consists in motion alone, or in the fermentation of the parts called inflammable”.N 155

A matter of fire

Nollet takes the first side, with most natural philosophers as he says: there is in nature a fluid made for this purpose from the beginning, which needs only to be excited in order to act, and it is the same matter that burns and that gives light.N 156 The Britannica’s chemistry, a supplementary text here, teaches the same. Free fire is a substance of infinitely small particles in very rapid motion, flowing without cease from the sun through all bodies. It is the one body fluid of itself, and it makes other bodies fluid.EB 68

A motion of the body’s own parts

Biwald builds up to the other answer through eight propositions. All bodies have a perpetual internal motion. It breaks their larger particles into smaller ones, and some are thrown out. Every hot or burning body shows such motion and gives off such effluvia. He concludes:

“Fire, then, considered in its origin, consists in a most vehement fermentation of the internal parts, by which the larger molecules are made finer and, carried into the ranges of repulsion, are thrown out from the whole mass. Heat is a more moderate motion of the internal parts, by which a smaller quantity of effluvia is driven out of bodies.”B 146

Ignis itaque in sua origine consideratus consistit in vehementissima quadam fermentatione partium internarum, qua maiores moleculae attenuantur, atque ad areas repulsivas delatae a massa totali eiiciuntur. Calor vero est moderatior partium intestinarum motus, quo minor effluviorum copia ex corporibus expellitur.

Heat a motion, fire a substance

Martin divides the question. Heat is motion. The pupil asks what heat and cold are, and the master answers:

“Heat is a Sensation excited in the Mind by a great Agitation of the Particles of the hot Body, which exerteth its Action or Influence on us; so that Heat in us is only the Idea thereof; and in the hot Body, Activity or Motion, and nothing else”M 114

Cold is the sensation when the parts of a body move less than those of the hand that feels it. When the pupil sums this up, the master calls it “the true Nature of the Case, according to modern Philosophy and manifold Experiments”.M 115 Fire, though, is not a motion for Martin. It is one of the fluids, “as Water, Fire, &c.”, made of particles small, round, smooth and alike.M 113 Its particles are those of light. They agree in their smallness, their brightness, their activity and their power to burn, and the sun’s rays gathered by a burning glass melt silver and gold in half a minute, “from whence we must infer, the Particles of Light and Fire are the same”.M 116 He does not say how a motion of a body’s own parts and a substance made of the particles of light are related. A note on vapours, a hundred pages on, puts it differently. There he asks whether “the received Philosophy” does not teach “That Heat is Fire, and that the Particles of Fire are in a constant and violent Agitation and State of Motion among themselves”.M 197 There heat is the motion of particles of fire, not of the body’s own parts. The Grammar does not reconcile the two.

Two hypotheses, left as questions

Erxleben asks first whether heat is perhaps a very rapid trembling of the finest parts of which a body is built. Then he asks the other question:

“Or is there perhaps a matter of fire of its own, an elementary fire, a most fine fluid being that is spread evenly through the interstices of all bodies, and in whose trembling warmth consists?”E 359

Giebt es also etwa vielmehr eine eigne Materie des Feuers, ein Elementarfeuer, ein höchst feines flüßiges Wesen, das durch die Zwischenräume aller Körper gleichförmig ausgebreitet ist, und in dessen Zittern die Wärme besteht?

He does not choose. If there is such a fire it must be present everywhere, and warmth then requires its motion and not its mere presence. How warmth expands bodies, he adds, can be explained on the one hypothesis as well as on the other.E 359

Is the nature of fire settled?

Nollet says it is not: after two or three thousand years of study the most enlightened physics can still offer only probabilities on the question.N 155 Erxleben, as above, puts both accounts as questions and does not choose.E 359 Biwald draws his account as the conclusion of eight propositions and teaches it as demonstrated.B 146 Martin’s master calls the motion of the hot body’s parts “the true Nature of the Case, according to modern Philosophy and manifold Experiments”; he says this of heat and cold, and of fire only what its particles are.M 115 The Britannica, a supplementary text, states its doctrine without raising the question.

Why they differed

The spark that becomes a conflagration

Nollet’s chief argument against motion is that fire behaves unlike any motion known:

“Fire considered in its principle must be something other than the internal motion of the heated parts, or the actual dissipation of burning bodies: for in the natural state every motion once impressed slows, and at last ceases to be sensible, as it is distributed to a greater quantity of matter […]; fire on the contrary is communicated with increase: we see every day that a spark becomes a conflagration.”N 159

Le Feu considéré dans son principe doit être autre chose que le mouvement intestin des parties échauffées, ou la dissipation actuelle des corps embrasés : car dans l’état naturel, tout mouvement une fois imprimé se ralentit, & cesse enfin d’être sensible, en se distribuant à une plus grande quantité de matière […]; le feu au contraire se communique avec accroissement : nous voyons tous les jours qu’une étincelle devient un incendie.

Biwald raises the same objection against himself and answers it from the forces he supposes between the particles of bodies:

“You will ask why the motion excited in a kindled body is not extinguished so long as another body fit to take up a like motion is at hand, when it is agreed that all other motions grow faint as they are communicated to a greater mass. I answer that the cause is that these internal motions are carried on by the internal forces of bodies, for which nothing more is required than that the equilibrium of the internal forces be disturbed.”B 155

Quaeres, cur motus in corpore accenso excitatus, haud extinguatur, quamdiu corpus alterum ad similem motum concipiendum idoneum praesto est; cum tamen constet, motus omnes alios languescere, cum massa maiori communicantur. Respondeo, causam esse, quod motus hi intestini, internis corporum viribus peragantur, ad quod aliud haud requiritur, quam, ut internarum virium aequilibrium turbetur

For Nollet a kindled body receives its motion from outside and must lose it; for Biwald each particle carries the forces that keep the motion going once it is set off.

How many kinds of matter nature needs

Nollet argues from economy toward a matter: nature makes few kinds of being and gives each many properties, so he is inclined to believe that one matter both burns and lights.N 156 Biwald argues from economy the other way. Once forces that depend on distance are admitted between particles, he says, the source of these internal motions lies open, and no separate matter of fire is called for.B 140

What chemistry needed

The Britannica’s account comes from its chemistry, which needs fire to be a thing that can enter into bodies and pass from one to another. Fire fixed in a body is phlogiston. It differs from free fire in that “when united to a body, it communicates to it neither heat nor light”, and it can be conveyed from the body it is joined with into another.EB 68 Erxleben keeps the two apart for the same reason, and warns his readers not to confuse the combustible essence with elementary fire.E 386

Where the majority stood

Nollet reports that the motion opinion “has hardly any partisans left”, and that those who still hold it commonly ascribe the first motion to the aether or to subtle matter, which brings the two opinions close together.N 155–156 Biwald, in Graz in 1768, teaches it as demonstrated. Martin, in London in 1778, teaches it for heat as “the true Nature of the Case”, but keeps particles of fire, the same as those of light.M 115

Questions the texts left open

  • Whether fire has weight. Biwald reports the experiments of Pieter van Musschenbroek (1692–1761): a hundred pounds of lead calcined in a fierce fire give a hundred and ten pounds of minium; tin calcined in a sealed glass retort gains four and a half grains; metals calcined by burning mirrors gain weight even while a dense smoke pours from them. Others object, he says, that the increase comes from particles of the vessels or the tools, “or from particles coming from the surrounding air and attracted by those bodies as they are reduced to calx”.B 155 Nollet sets the experiments of Herman Boerhaave (1668–1738) against those of Wilhelm Homberg (1652–1715) and offers the same list of possible sources.N 165–168 Erxleben remarks that even if elementary fire exists, a hot body need not be found heavier than a cold one.E 360
  • What the food of fire is. Boerhaave made it oil or alcohol; Johann Heinrich Pott (1692–1777) an acid spirit; others an inflammable earth. Biwald concludes from their disagreement that nothing yet known deserves the name.B 138
  • Whether everything is solid without fire. The Britannica draws the consequence of its doctrine: “air itself might become solid, if it could be entirely deprived of the fire it contains”.EB 68 Martin puts fluidity down to the particles themselves, their smallness, roundness, smoothness and likeness, and counts fire among the fluids so made.M 113
