Snapshot c. 1775 · Natural philosophy

The system of the world

What teaching texts from Graz, Göttingen, Paris and London said between 1765 and 1776 about how the sun, the earth and the planets are arranged and what keeps them moving. All four put the sun in the middle with the earth as one of its planets. They differ in how sure they say it is, in what they will say about its cause, and in whether God must keep the system in order.

First cut, from one text per region. The fixed stars, comets, the moon’s irregularities, eclipses, the tides and the figure of the earth are not covered.

Where the subject sat among the sciences

Biwald makes it the third and last part of general physics. A first section goes through the bodies of the universe one by one, the fixed stars, the primary planets, their inhabitants, the earth, the sun, the comets, the satellites and the moon, and ends with the system that arranges them. A second section sets out the phenomena of their motions and looks for the physical cause.B 384B 401 Erxleben puts the heavens in the twelfth of his thirteen sections, together with the earth as a whole. He begins from what an observer sees, describes the arrangement and the orbits, and comes late in the section to a short chapter headed “Causes of the motions of the heavenly bodies”. He says he will teach neither astronomical calculation nor the art of observing.E 530 Nollet gives the subject one lesson of his experimental course, taught with a planetary machine set on a table, and places it after his two lessons on light.N 7 Ferguson has no lecture on the system of the world. Its doctrine comes in his second lecture, on central forces, under marginal headings among the experiments on the whirling table: “The laws of the planetary motions”, Kepler’s rule, “The absurdity of the Cartesian vortexes”, and “The earth’s motion demonstrated”.F 23F 35F 45 The heavens as they appear open his lectures on the use of the globes.F 247–248 For a true figure of the moon’s path he refers the reader to “my treatise of astronomy”.F 26 The Britannica’s long treatise “Astronomy”, which opens with the scale of the universe before it describes the solar system, is that treatise reprinted.EB i 434

What the texts taught in common

  • The sun is in the middle and the earth is one of six primary planets. In order from the sun: Mercury, Venus, the Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn. The moon goes round the earth, four satellites round Jupiter and five round Saturn.B 384E 479EB i 445 Ferguson does not list the planets in his lectures, but the sun and planets move round the common centre of gravity of the whole system, and the earth turns on its axis in 24 hours and goes round the sun once a year.F 39F 254 Biwald, Erxleben and Nollet call this the system or doctrine of Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543), as does the Britannica; Ferguson, in the lectures read, names no system and no author for it.N 7
  • The fixed stars are suns at immense distances. So Biwald and the Britannica; Ferguson says only that the remotest planet is beyond comparison nearer to us than any fixed star, and that some stars are highly probably, in a manner, infinitely more distant than others.F 248 Biwald adds that they perhaps hold the centres of new systems; the Britannica says an observer near any star would take it alone for a real sun.B 384EB i 434
  • The orbits are ellipses with the sun in one focus. The planets move fastest when nearest the sun, and the squares of their periods are as the cubes of their distances. These are credited to Johannes Kepler (1571–1630).B 402E 532–533 Ferguson confirms the last of these rules on his whirling table, but shows the planets moving in circles and does not say what figure their orbits have.F 35
  • The planets’ halts and backward motions are appearances. They follow from the observer being carried round on the earth.B 387E 506N 65 Ferguson’s lectures do not mention them.
  • The system of Ptolemy is given up. The Britannica calls it a “rude scheme” soon rejected by all true philosophers; Biwald calls it an Augean stable that Copernicus undertook to clean; Nollet thinks it useless to recall hypotheses that have fallen into discredit.EB i 445B 393N 7 Ferguson names neither Ptolemy nor Tycho, but holds it evident that the sun does not go round the earth.F 45
  • No whirlpool of fluid carries the planets. Biwald, Erxleben and Ferguson each reject the vortices of René Descartes (1596–1650).B 431E 531F 36 The Britannica reprints Ferguson’s experiment.EB iii 40 Nollet does not mention them in this lesson.

The disputes

How certain the motion of the earth is

The four texts teach the same arrangement with four degrees of assurance. Ferguson has no doubt at all, and calls it demonstrated. Under the marginal heading “The earth’s motion demonstrated” he concludes:

“From the principles thus established, it is evident that the earth moves round the sun, and not the sun round the earth.”F 45

The proof is mechanical (below), and he grants that the eye cannot decide: whether the earth is at rest and the heaven turns round it, or the heaven is at rest and the earth turns round, “the appearance to us will be exactly the same”.F 251 The Britannica, here reprinting Ferguson’s separate Astronomy, is as sure. Isaac Newton (1642–1727) “has established this system on such a foundation of mathematical and physical demonstration, as can never be shaken”, and it is “the only true and rational scheme, restored by Copernicus, and demonstrated by Sir Isaac Newton”.EB i 445 Biwald says that this disposition of the heavenly bodies is the one that obtains in the very nature of things, and reports the state of opinion:

“After Newton had drawn universal gravity, or the mutual action of all bodies on one another, out of the motions of the heavenly bodies themselves, this system so pleased all astronomers and physicists that today there is now almost no one who, looking to astronomical and physical reasons, would dare to call it in doubt.”B 385

Postquam vero Newtonus gravitatem universalem, seu mutuam corporum omnium in se invicem actionem ex ipsis corporum coelestium motibus eruisset, systema hoc ita Astronomis, Physicisque omnibus arrisit, ut hodie fere iam nemo sit, qui spectatis astronomicis, physicisque rationibus in dubium illud adducere ausit.

Erxleben speaks of probability. The daily motion of the stars may really happen as it appears, or the earth may turn on its axis:

“Which of the two happens cannot well be settled by experiments; but whoever considers the matter only a little will find the latter far more probable than that the astonishing multitude of stars should move round the earth.”E 465

Welches von beyden geschieht, läßt sich nicht wohl durch Versuche ausmachen; aber wer die Sache nur etwas überlegt, der wird das letztere weit wahrscheinlicher finden, als daß sich die erstaunende Menge von Sternen um die Erde bewegen sollte.

He promises further grounds later, and counts the slower pendulum at Cayenne as one.E 478 The arrangement itself he introduces as a supposition: “Let the sun be set at ☉”, and let the planets and the earth move round it.E 479 Nollet is the most reserved. He will stop at the hypothesis that suits his purpose best “and which is generally received today”: “I shall follow the doctrine of Copernicus, perfected by Kepler and by the astronomers of our day.”N 7 When he compares it with Ptolemy’s he gives a reason of method, not a proof (below).

What moves the planets

Biwald states a single proposition. All the heavenly bodies, at least those of our system, are urged by two forces: one by which they strive toward one another in the inverse duplicate ratio of the distances, called centripetal, and one by which they tend to go off along the tangents of their orbits, called projectile. Grant these two and every phenomenon of their motions has the plainest explanation.B 401–402 Vortices of subtle matter, he then argues, cannot exist in nature, and would not explain the motions if they did.B 431–432

Erxleben agrees, in more guarded words. The planets are not fixed in hollow spheres, as the ancients thought, and cannot be carried by vortices. “The great Newton has shown instead, for the first time, that the very force which drives a stone toward the earth, gravity, also keeps the planets in their motion.” For the moon there is “the greatest probability” that the two forces are the same; for the other planets he argues from the attractive force belonging to matter in general, “as it well appears”.E 531–532 The second force he traces to the Creator:

“The universal gravity of matter toward matter thus supplies the centripetal force in the motion of the planets in their curved paths; the second force required for it, the centrifugal force, God seems to have impressed on or given to the bodies of the world at their creation.”E 533

Die allgemeine Schwere der Materie gegen einander giebt also die Centripetalkraft bey der Bewegung der Planeten in ihren krummen Bahnen ab; die zwote dazu erforderliche Kraft, die Centrifugalkraft scheint Gott den Weltkörpern bey ihrer Schöpfung eingedrückt oder gegeben zu haben.

Ferguson teaches the two forces on his whirling table. “From the uniform projectile motion of bodies in straight lines, and the universal power of gravity or attraction, arises the curvilineal motion of all the heavenly bodies.”F 23 Balls of equal weight whirled at different distances confirm “that famous observation of Kepler”, that the squares of the periods are as the cubes of the distances, and that the sun’s attraction falls off as the square of the distance “to the remotest part of the system”.F 35 The sun and planets, held together by their mutual attraction as two balls by a wire, must all move round the common centre of gravity of the whole system. Being “as unactive and dead” as the balls, the planets could not have set themselves going; they were balanced at first “by the Almighty hand that made them and put them in motion”.F 38–39 He alone of the four, in the passages read, holds that this first adjustment was not enough:

“But after all this nice adjustment, it appears evident that the Deity cannot withdraw his regulating hand from his works, and leave them to be solely governed by the laws which he has imprest upon them at first.”F 40

The planets disturb one another’s motions when several stand in one quarter of the heavens, and the balance, once lost, could never restore itself. His instance is the comet “which appeared lately”, which passed so near Jupiter that its orbit and its period were changed.F 40–41

Nollet reports the same two forces and then asks where they come from. That question, he says, has long puzzled the philosophers and has produced only hypotheses, which are admitted or rejected according to one’s prejudice for the authors or the nations that defend them.N 151–152 Newton, it seems to him, went about it “in a very wise and very reasonable manner”. He began from what was under his eyes, and supposed that bodies weigh toward one another directly as their masses and inversely as the square of the distance, “without troubling himself in the least to decide” whether this comes from a force innate in matter or from a mechanical and external cause that escapes us.N 152–154 What Newton took only as a hypothesis, many now regard as a first cause innate in matter and depending on nothing but the will of the Creator. That opinion has some likelihood, Nollet allows, and it is no wonder that it draws mathematicians occupied with the heavens. But:

“It must be granted that the physics of our day, which prides itself on being purged for ever of those occult qualities that had made it so ridiculous, cannot see without pain an abstract virtue brought back into matter, an unknown and even unintelligible being, which has nothing to do with mechanism.”N 156

Mais il faut convenir que la Physique de nos jours, qui se glorifie d’être purgée à jamais de ces qualités occultes qui l’avoient rendu si ridicule, ne doit point voir, sans peine, qu’on fasse rentrer dans la matiere une vertu abstraite, un être inconnu, & même inintelligible, & qui ne tient en rien au Méchanisme.

It is no less hard, he goes on, to admit in the heavens a matter without resistance, which “is almost to say a matter that is not matter”; and attraction properly so called “is not so fortunate on the Earth as it appears to be in the Sky”.N 156–157 Those who speak of the attraction of the heavenly bodies, he concludes, need not be taken to admit this metaphysical being. The word is a convenient expression for an astronomer and commits him to nothing about the principle.N 157–158

Whether the planets are inhabited

Ferguson’s lectures do not raise the question. The Britannica, in words taken from his separate Astronomy, treats it as all but proved. The planets are solid opaque globes with days, seasons and moons, and the likeness leaves “no room to doubt” what they are for.EB i 435 The moons of Jupiter and Saturn can be of no use to us, and to suppose them made on our account “would be charging him impiously with having done much in vain”:

“These considerations amount to little less than a positive proof, that all the planets are inhabited: For if they are not, why all this care in furnishing them with so many moons, to supply those with light which are at the greater distances from the sun?”EB i 444

Biwald gives the question an article of its own and answers with a probability:

“I judge it, then, very probable that the primary planets at least are inhabited by living creatures of some kind, and creatures endowed with reason.”B 339

Censeo igitur admodum probabile esse, planetas saltem primarios a viventibus quibusdam, ac etiam ratione praeditis creaturis incoli.

He has nothing certain, he says, and argues from the analogy of the earth. He is less ready to people the satellites, since their going round the primaries suggests that they were meant to serve those who live there.B 340 Erxleben mentions the inhabitants only to decline the subject:

“As little as I care to inquire into the size of the inhabitants of the other planets, or their form and way of thinking, so little can I enter here into how the heavens appear to the inhabitants of these planets, although this can be done with greater certainty than that.”E 520

So wenig wie ich die Größe der Einwohner der übrigen Planeten, noch ihre Bildung und Denkungsart untersuchen mag, so wenig kann ich mich hier darauf einlassen, zu untersuchen, wie der Himmel den Einwohnern dieser Planeten erscheint, ob dieses gleich mit größerer Gewißheit geschehen kann als ienes

He sends the reader to the Cosmotheoros of Christiaan Huygens (1629–1695). Nollet says only, at the opening of his lesson, that a modest man reflecting on so great a spectacle would not dare believe it was made for him and his kind alone.N 2

Why they differed

Four supports for one doctrine

Biwald rests the earth’s motion on observation. The clean explanation of the phenomena already persuades, he says, but he adds four further grounds, and calls one “the third and weightiest”: the aberration of the stars. Its phenomena are certain; if the earth moves they follow immediately from its yearly motion and the successive propagation of light; and in a system with the earth at rest nothing can be shown, nor anything plausible even imagined, that fits them so exactly.B 390 Erxleben, who says experiment cannot well decide, argues from what is more probable. Nollet argues from method. The epicycles by which Ptolemy (c. 100–c. 170) explained the planets’ irregularities are altogether ingenious, he says, but they lack the simplicity that marks all that nature does, “and which requires that we give preference to the hypotheses that depart from it least. On this ground the explanations of Ptolemy must yield to those of Copernicus.”N 65 Ferguson argues from mechanics. The centrifugal law, he says, “will never allow a great body to move round a small one in any orbit whatever”; where a small body goes round a great one, both go round their common centre of gravity. On the whirling table a ball of one ounce draws a demi-globe of two pounds off the centre, so the sun could not stay in the very centre of the planets’ orbits.F 37 The sun has 227,000 times the earth’s quantity of matter, so their common centre lies within the sun, only the 1068⅔ part of his semidiameter from his centre.F 45–46 The Britannica’s “Astronomy” appeals to Newton’s demonstration and gives none of it; the argument is in its “Mechanics”, which reprints Ferguson’s lecture.EB iii 42–43

Who had objections to answer

Only Biwald, the Jesuit, stops to answer the case against Copernicus, and he takes it from a writer of his own order. Giovanni Battista Riccioli (1598–1671) had collected seventy-seven arguments against the moving earth, not counting those from Scripture, and later writers have mostly drawn on him. Biwald reduces them to three heads. The physical ones (clouds and birds left behind, heavy bodies not falling straight) he answers with the ship: on a vessel in even motion men walk and play ball as if it were at rest, and a stone dropped from the masthead falls at its foot. The astronomical ones (no change in the size of the stars, no parallax) he answers by the enormous distance of the stars.B 391–392 The last head is Scripture, chiefly the sun standing still in the book of Joshua:

“But the defenders of the Copernican system contend that this passage of Scripture is by no means to be taken in the literal sense, since it is agreed that Scripture, in natural matters, accommodates itself to the understanding of the common people.”B 392

Verum hunc scripturae locum haudquaquam in sensu literali capiendum esse Copernicani systematis defensores contendunt, cum constet scripturam captui vulgi in rebus naturalibus se accommodare.

It seems undeniable, he adds in his own voice, that Joshua could have spoken no otherwise even had he known the earth’s motion, just as astronomers most devoted to Copernicus still say that the sun rises and sets, and print the hours in their ephemerides.B 392–393 Tycho Brahe (1546–1601), he notes, saw that the Copernican system agreed best with astronomical reasons and devised his own because he judged it contrary to Scripture.B 393 Erxleben’s one remark on Tycho is astronomical: having let the earth rest, he “had to assume unnatural screw-paths” for the planets.E 506 The Britannica, from Ferguson’s Astronomy, calls Tycho’s hypothesis “partly true, and partly false”.EB i 445 Ferguson’s lectures name neither Ptolemy nor Tycho, and in the passages read answer no objections to the earth’s motion.

Mechanism in the heavens

Nollet’s reserve about the cause is the one he shows on weight and on capillary tubes, and he refers the reader back to what he said of attraction there.N 157 His test is whether a cause belongs to mechanism. By that test attraction in matter is an occult quality returned, and an empty or unresisting heaven is hard for a physicist to accept. He adds an argument from his own field: every day experimental physics finds that what was to be ascribed to attraction is explained as well, and often better, by impulsion.N 157 Biwald, Erxleben and Ferguson apply no such test. Ferguson’s case against vortices is an experiment, quicksilver and a cork in tubes of water on the whirling table, and his verdict is that “while gravity exists, there is no occasion for such vortexes”.F 36 Biwald’s chief argument against vortices is the comets, which move through the regions of the planets in every direction and often against the order of the signs, where a solar vortex would sweep them along with the rest.B 431 He answers the Oratorian Joseph-Étienne Bertier (1702–1783), who had revived the vortices, that his whole case rests on the analogy of terrestrial fluids, which act only on the surface of a floating body.B 434

Analogy or purpose

The two texts that people the planets argue differently. The Britannica, in Ferguson’s words from his Astronomy, argues from purpose: an infinitely wise Creator would not leave the other planets “bare and destitute of rational creatures”, nor furnish them with moons to no end.EB i 444 Biwald argues from likeness. The primary planets are opaque bodies turning on their axes round the sun, with marks of heights and hollows, and three of them, the earth among them, have satellites. One who peoples the earth and denies inhabitants to the rest, he says after Jérôme Lalande (1732–1807), is like a man who sees a flock of sheep far off and asserts that some have entrails and the others nothing inside but the hardest stone.B 339 The opinion is no novelty, having been held by the Pythagoreans. The heat of Mercury and the cold of Saturn are no obstacle, since bodies may be framed to bear them, as Lapps sleep in the snow. As to what such inhabitants exist for, we are mostly ignorant of final causes; it is certain only that the divine power is the more commended if the planets, and those that perhaps circle each fixed star, are peopled.B 340–341 He notes that the logic of Antonio Genovesi (1713–1769) uses “the planets lack inhabitants” as its example of an improbable proposition.B 341

Questions the texts left open

  • The parallax of the fixed stars. The earth’s yearly motion ought to produce one, and astronomers have not yet observed it. Biwald, after Lalande: were it a single second, the star would be 6,771,770 million leagues off, and the nearest are farther than that.B 392
  • Whether the earth’s measured figure fits its rotation. A globe flattened at the poles suits a turning earth, Biwald says, though the measurements made so far do not answer well enough to the effect of that turning.B 390 Ferguson has no such doubt: that the earth is an oblate spheroid “is demonstrable from actual measurement”, the degrees being longer in the frigid zones than in the torrid, and the equatorial diameter 36 miles longer than the axis.F 46–47
  • Where the second force comes from. Erxleben refers the centrifugal force to the creation, Ferguson to “the Almighty hand that made them and put them in motion”; Nollet says the origin of both forces has puzzled philosophers for a long time.E 533F 39N 151
  • Why all the planets go round the same way. It was urged in favour of a common stream of subtle matter. Biwald replies that no prior cause appears, that it does not follow that there is none, and that we know nothing of how our system is connected with others.B 442
  • Whether the heavens are empty. Newton, as Nollet reports him, considered them if not a void then filled with a fluid unable to alter the motions by its resistance, which Nollet finds hard to admit.N 154
  • Whether the obliquity of the ecliptic is diminishing. Erxleben gives it as 23° 28′ and asks whether it falls regularly by a minute a century, as Louville maintained.E 482
  • Whether Venus has a moon. She may, the Britannica says after Ferguson’s Astronomy, although it is undiscovered.EB i 440
