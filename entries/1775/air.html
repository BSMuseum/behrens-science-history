Snapshot c. 1775 · Natural philosophy

Air

What teaching texts from Graz, Göttingen, Paris and London said about air between 1766 and 1776. They agree on its weight and its spring. They differ on what causes the spring, and on what to make of the air that comes out of bodies.

First cut, from one text per region. Winds and sound, which the texts treat under air, are not yet covered.

Where air sat among the sciences

Biwald gives air the second part of his particular physics, after water, and treats winds and sound in the same part as motions raised in air.B Conspectus Erxleben does the same in his seventh section. Ferguson gives air his sixth lecture, on “the nature, weight, pressure, and spring of the air, and the effects arising therefrom”. Winds, the vivifying spirit of the air, damps, thunder and earthquakes come under it, and then the air pump and thirty-two experiments with it.F 168F 179 What causes the spring of air he says in his first lecture, among the properties of matter.F 8 The Britannica has two accounts in two places. “Pneumatics”, Ferguson’s lecture reprinted, treats air as a fluid with weight and spring. “Chemistry” names it, with earth, water and fire, as one of the four principles of which bodies are made.EB ii 66–67

What the texts taught in common

  • Air is a heavy, elastic fluid surrounding the globe. So Biwald defines it.B 68 Ferguson: “The air is that thin transparent fluid body in which we live and breathe.” It encompasses the whole earth to a considerable height. It has every property of a fluid, but differs from all others in four things: it can be compressed, it cannot be congealed or fixed, it grows rarer upward, and it is elastic, the force of its spring being equal to its weight. Being a body, it must have weight.F 168–169 The Britannica prints the same page.EB iii 484
  • Its weight can be measured. A hollow glass globe weighs less when the air is pumped out of it.B 71 A bottle holding a wine quart is about 16 grains lighter when emptied of air, “which shews that a quart of air weighs 16 grains”, and water is 914 times as heavy as air near the ground.F 169 In the Torricellian experiment the air holds up about twenty-nine and a half inches of quicksilver, so its pressure equals the weight of that depth of quicksilver over the whole surface of the earth; when the air round the basin is pumped away, the quicksilver falls.F 170–171 The Britannica prints these pages from an earlier edition, with 17 grains for the quart of air.EB iii 484–485
  • Its volume shrinks as the weight pressing on it grows. The volumes to which air is reduced are inversely as the compressing weights, a proposition Biwald credits to the experiments of Robert Boyle (1627–1691) and Edme Mariotte (c. 1620–1684).B 75 Ferguson puts it the other way round: “the density of the air is always as the force that compresseth it”, which is why the air grows rarer the higher it is.F 169
  • Heat strengthens its spring. At the heat of boiling water the elastic force of enclosed air grows by a third.B 79 Ferguson makes the winds follow from the swelling of air by heat, and explains the explosions in mines by it: “the elasticity of the air is increased by heat”.F 173F 178
  • Animals cannot live without it, and breathing spoils it. Ferguson: “All common air is impregnated with a certain kind of vivifying spirit or quality, which is necessary to continue the lives of animals”; a gallon serves one man for about a minute, and the spirit “is destroyed by passing through the lungs of animals”. Fire destroys it too, above all charcoal fire and the flame of sulphur.F 175 A candle under a receiver holding a gallon burns a minute, and a bird kept under a close receiver soon dies, though no air is pumped out.F 197 The Britannica prints the same passage.EB iii 490
  • Bodies contain air. It bubbles out of water, beer and wood under the air pump, and is driven out by heat, by freezing, and above all when bodies are broken down by fermentation, putrefaction or distillation.B 86E 202F 195–196F 199

The disputes

What makes air springy

Nollet offers a picture: the parts of air are small filaments coiled into flexible, elastic spirals, and a mass of air is like a bundle of cotton or carded wool that can be squeezed small and always tries to recover.N 182–183 Biwald gives no shape at all. From the compression experiments he concludes:

“There will therefore be between the molecules of air a perpetual effort to recede from one another, notwithstanding which smaller portions of the same air may attract one another with a force that falls under the senses.”B 75

Erit igitur inter aereas moleculas perpetuus recedendi nisus, quo tamen haud obstante minores eiusdem aeris portiones sese vi sub sensum cadente attrahere poterunt.

Ferguson’s lecture on pneumatics only lists the spring among the properties of air.F 169 His lecture on matter gives the cause, in the same terms as Biwald, though without his attraction between portions of air:

“The repulsive force of the particles of air is so great, that they can never be brought so near together by condensation as to make them stick or cohere.”F 8

The Britannica prints that sentence in its “Mechanics”.EB iii 34 Its chemistry, which is its own, gives a third answer: the elasticity of air depends chiefly on its being condensed and dilated by cold and heat.EB ii 67

Whether the air drawn from bodies is air

Stephen Hales (1677–1761) had measured the great quantities of elastic fluid that bodies give off, and after Boyle had called it factitious air. All four texts report it, and they read it differently. Erxleben states the fact and the name: by dissolving bodies into their smaller parts “an enormous quantity of air has been discovered in them, which has been called artificial air”, and he refers the reader to the papers on factitious air by Henry Cavendish (1731–1810).E 202 Nollet holds that it is true air which had entered into the composition of the body, divided down to its elementary particles, and for that reason “very different from that which only fills the voids or pores”; inside bodies it is, as Hales says, in a state of fixity.N 298N 321 Biwald follows Pieter van Musschenbroek (1692–1761) in drawing a line. Natural air does lodge in bodies and can be drawn out again unchanged. But the elastic fluid produced by burning, putrefaction and fermentation is a compound of air with saline, oily, spirituous and watery particles, unwholesome and foul, and no one skilled will call that vapour air, though true air lies hidden in it.B 82–83 Ferguson, closing his air-pump experiments, makes two kinds of air in bodies: elastic air, kept in by the weight of the atmosphere, which boiling or the air pump will draw out, and “the fixed air, which is by much the greater quantity”, which “cannot be got out but by distillation, fermentation, or putrefaction”.F 199 The Britannica reprints his experiments with beer and wood, in which the air bubbles out under the pump, but stops before this paragraph.EB iii 495 Its list of the marks of air keeps Ferguson’s words that air “cannot be congealed or fixed, as other fluids may”. In Ferguson, who goes on to speak of fixed air, the words mean that air cannot be made solid as water is frozen.EB iii 484F 168

Why they differed

Shapes or forces

Nollet explains a property of the whole by the shape of the parts, and is candid about how little supports the shape he picks:

“This idea is only a very rough sketch of the nature of air; and I admit that the odds are perhaps a hundred to one that the parts of this element do not have the figure I give them; because I have no other reason for supposing them so than their flexibility and their spring, and they could be elastic with a hundred figures other than a spiral thread.”N 183

Cette idée n’est qu’une esquisse bien grossière de la nature de l’air ; & j’avoue qu’il y a peut-être cent contre un à parier, que les parties de cet élément n’ont point la figure que je leur attribue ; parce que pour les supposer telles, je n’ai d’autre raison que leur flexibilité & leur ressort, & qu’elles peuvent être élastiques avec cent figures différentes d’un filet spiral

He adopts it, he says, with most natural philosophers, to make the spring of air easier to grasp. Biwald, who explains fire, electricity and the magnet by forces between particles, does the same for air and needs no figure.

What the name “factitious” was taken to mean

Biwald and Nollet both stop to say that Boyle and Hales did not mean that solid matter is turned into air.N 298 Biwald gives the reason the name was needed:

“Not because they thought that the solid parts of bodies are converted into air, but because, once released from the bond of bodies, it shows itself by its own marks and properties, of which it had seemed to be stripped.”B 87

Non, quod censerent, partes corporum solidas in aerem converti, sed quod a vinculo corporum solutus suis se notis, ac proprietatibus dignoscendum praebeat, quibus exutus veluti videbatur

The volume of air released, he adds from Hales, often exceeds many times over the volume of the body that held it, which shows how different its condition was while it was bound.B 87 Ferguson draws a warning from the same measures. Fixed air must come out of bodies slowly, or it would tear them to pieces: “Trees would be rent by the change of air from a fixt, to an elastic state”. Hales found the air in apples so condensed that, let out, it would fill a space 48 times their bulk.F 199–200

What counts as air

The texts apply different tests. For Musschenbroek, as Biwald reports him, natural air is known by being wholesome and unmixed, so a fluid that stinks and kills is something else that happens to be elastic. For Nollet, Erxleben and Ferguson whatever is released and behaves as an elastic fluid is air; for Ferguson fixed and elastic are two states of one air. Ferguson separates the air from a quality carried in it, the vivifying spirit, and says that air which has lost that spirit is called damp, because it puts out fire and destroys life as well as because it is moist.F 175–176 The Britannica prints the passage.EB iii 490

Questions the texts left open

  • Whether vapours strengthen or weaken the spring of air. Biwald says it is not easy to pronounce, the philosophers disagreeing widely. Hales showed that sulphureous vapours greatly lessen it and make air less fit for breathing.B 79
  • How far heat can rarefy air. The figures Biwald collects run from four thousand times to more than five hundred thousand.B 80
  • Whether the weight of air is its own. Herman Boerhaave (1668–1738) suspected that the weight ascribed to air might be due to the watery vapours in it alone.B 81
  • Whether air is made from anything else. Biwald denies that it is generated from water turned to vapour, as some ancients held, on the ground that after rain, thunder and wind the air remains and weighs as before.B 82
