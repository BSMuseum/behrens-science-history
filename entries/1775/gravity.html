Snapshot c. 1775 · Natural philosophy

Gravity

What teaching texts from Graz, Göttingen, Paris and London said about weight and gravity between 1759 and 1776. They agree on how heavy bodies fall and on what weight is proportional to. They part over what gravity is: an attraction belonging to all matter, the push of an unseen fluid, or a cause no one has yet found.

First cut, from one text per region. The pendulum, the inclined plane, projectiles and the centre of gravity are not covered here; the planets belong to the entry on the system of the world.

Where gravity sat among the sciences

Biwald builds the second part of his general physics on a single curve of forces. Its first arm and its many bends, he says, have served to explain the other properties of bodies; gravity is what remains, “what belongs to the last asymptotic arm of the same curve, which shows an attraction acting at the greatest distances very nearly in the inverse duplicate ratio of those distances”.B 286 Six articles follow, on universal gravity, on the gravity of bodies on the earth, on its inequality from place to place, on how far from the earth it reaches, on the opinions of others about its cause, and on objections. Erxleben gives gravity the fourth of his thirteen sections, between motion and cohesion. He starts from the hand that feels a body press on it, goes on to weight, fall and the path of thrown bodies, stops for six paragraphs on the cause of gravity, and then passes to the pendulum and the machines.E 81 Nollet reaches gravity in the sixth lesson of his course, straight after the lesson on central forces in which he whirls glass globes to try the vortices of René Descartes (1596–1650). The lesson has two parts: the effects of weight acting alone, and those in which it is one force among several.N 102–103 Ferguson treats gravity inside his first lecture, on the properties of matter. Attraction is the fifth property, and gravitation the second of its four kinds; weight, the fall of bodies and the centre of gravity follow.F 6F 8 Its reach to the moon and the planets comes back in the second lecture, on central forces, with experiments on a whirling table.F 35 The Britannica has no article on the subject. “Gravity” and “Gravitation” are one-line entries sending the reader to the treatise “Mechanics”, which reprints these pages of Ferguson.EB iii 33

What the texts taught in common

  • Every body within reach is heavy, and none is light of itself. Biwald says the gravity of every kind of body, solid or fluid, is beyond controversy among recent physicists, though some of the ancients gave air and fire a positive lightness.B 293 Nollet: “a long and continual experience does not allow us to believe that, of all the bodies in our power, there is any exempt from this affection.” Under the receiver of an air pump the smoke of a snuffed candle falls to the plate.N 105–107 Ferguson makes the same trial: the smoke of a candle that rises to the top of a tall receiver full of air falls to the bottom when the air is drawn out, which shows “that there is no such thing as positive levity, even in smoke, vapours, and fumes”.F 9 The Britannica prints the same page.EB iii 34
  • Weight is as the quantity of matter, whatever the bulk or figure. Ferguson proves it from pendulums: if they are of equal length they vibrate in equal times whatever their weights, so a double weight must be moved by a double power of gravity.F 10B 294E 83 The Britannica prints Ferguson’s proof.EB iii 34
  • Bodies fall with a uniformly accelerated motion. Biwald and Erxleben give the same measure for the first second of fall, 15 Paris feet and 1 inch.B 297E 77 Ferguson: the spaces fallen through in successive equal times are as the odd numbers 1, 3, 5, 7, and the whole spaces as the squares of the times.F 10–12EB iii 34 Nollet and Erxleben name Galileo Galilei (1564–1642) as the discoverer of these laws.N 102E 81
  • Where there is no air, all bodies fall equally fast. Nollet releases lead and a feather, copper and paper, wood and iron in pairs inside an exhausted glass tube, and no difference in the time of fall can be seen; let the air back in and the heavier body of each pair arrives first.N 128–129 Ferguson uses a guinea and a feather, which “descend with equal velocities” in the exhausted receiver and strike the plate at the same instant.F 183B 297E 77
  • Gravity reaches beyond the surface of the earth and weakens with distance. Biwald argues in two propositions that the force urging bodies toward the earth is the one that keeps the moon in its orbit.B 306–307 Ferguson has gravity, “like all other virtues or emanations which proceed or issue from a center”, decrease as the square of the distance increases, and confirms it by comparing the moon’s fall from the tangent of her orbit in a minute with the fall of bodies near the earth, and by the moons of Jupiter.F 10 In his second lecture a whirling table confirms Kepler’s rule, and with it an attraction of the sun inversely as the square of the distance, “to the remotest part of the system”.F 35 The Britannica prints the first passage.EB iii 34 Nollet gives the decrease on Newton’s word, adding that Newton deserves a hearing.N 142
  • Weight is not quite the same everywhere on the earth. A pendulum regulated at Paris goes too slowly near the equator. The fact is common ground among the three continental texts, and its explanation is not (below); Ferguson does not mention it.

The disputes

What gravity is

Nollet opens his lesson by saying that the question is unsettled: “The philosophers are not agreed among themselves on the cause of this force.” Their opinions fall into two classes. One makes weight a principle of nature, an inherent and primordial quality of bodies with no cause but the free will of the Creator, which “cuts short all difficulties”. The other makes it the effect of some invisible matter. The proofs offered for both have met great objections that do not seem to have been fully answered.N 99–100 He then dismisses the candidates in turn. To say with Aristotle (384–322 BC) that falling bodies obey a principle that makes them fall is to say nothing. To regard weight with Isaac Newton (1642–1727) as the consequence of a general gravitation whose laws he calculated so well “is to abandon the cause and attach oneself to the effect”. To hold with most Newtonians of the day that it is one case of a reciprocal attraction which all material beings have by the will of God alone is to bring into physics a novelty that Newton himself did not wish to have imputed to him. The magnet-like effluvia of Pierre Gassendi (1592–1655) are an obscure and vague cause resting on nothing certain. The vortex of Descartes is defective.N 100–101 His conclusion:

“All things considered, it seems that those who wish to hear, on the physical cause of weight, only explanations that are at once satisfying and intelligible should not look for them in any work known up to the present. Let us keep, then, to the phenomena.”N 101

tout bien considéré, il semble que ceux qui voudront n’entendre, sur la cause physique de la pesanteur, que des explications qui soient en même tems satisfaisantes & intelligibles, ne doivent point les chercher dans aucun ouvrage, qui soit connu jusqu’à présent. Tenons-nous-en donc aux phénoménes

Erxleben tells the same history and comes out elsewhere. Descartes supposed a very fine fluid whirling round the earth at great speed and carrying slower bodies toward the centre; Christiaan Huygens (1629–1695) made the fluid move in great circles in every direction; Georg Bernhard Bilfinger (1693–1750) gave the vortex two axes at right angles. None of these, he says, would bring a body to the centre of the earth.E 81–82 Then, in the form of a question, he proposes that all matter may have a power of attracting other matter even at a distance, and that this attractive force of the earth may be the cause of gravity.E 84 And he commits himself:

“I am not afraid to grant some probability to this hypothesis, that matter possesses an attractive force toward other matter, although there are people who declare flatly unreasonable those who do so.”E 84–85

Ich scheue mich nicht, dieser Hypothese, daß die Materie eine anziehende Kraft gegen einander besitzt, einige Wahrscheinlichkeit beyzulegen, ob es gleich Leute giebt, welche dieienigen gerade zu für unvernünftig erklären, welche so thun.

For Biwald the matter is decided. Universal gravity is “that affection of all the bodies making up this universe, or at least our planetary system, by which, placed at greater distances from one another, they perpetually strive to approach one another”, and the weight of bodies on the earth is a case of it.B 286 He calls this “our opinion on the cause of the gravity of terrestrial bodies” and treats every other account as history.B 319 Of the followers of Descartes:

“The Cartesians, to give the cause of the gravity of terrestrial bodies, take refuge in subtle matter as in a sacred anchor. To it they grant whatever directions, and the most diverse kinds of motion, they judge fittest to explain the phenomena of gravity.”B 313

Cartesiani, ut causam gravitatis corporum terrestrium redderent, ad materiam subtilem, velut ad sacram anchoram confugiunt. Huic directiones quaslibet, motuum item species diversissimas largiuntur, quales nempe phaenomenis gravitatis explicandis prae aliis inservire existimant.

Their systems have multiplied without end, he goes on, and it would not be worth listing them, “since they are now almost antiquated”. For the sake of the history of philosophy he sorts them into three classes: a subtle matter streaming or pressing in straight lines toward the centre, a highly elastic matter driving bodies down by its oscillations, and a vortex or several.B 313–314 He refutes each, and then the spirits of Johannes Kepler (1571–1630), the hooked particles of Gassendi, the inborn striving of the Peripatetics, the elastic medium of Newton’s own twenty-first query in the Opticks, and the vortices lately revived by the Oratorian Joseph-Étienne Bertier (1702–1783).B 318–319

Ferguson states one view and argues with no one. “Attraction of gravitation is that power by which distant bodies tend towards one another”; by it bodies on every side of the earth fall toward its centre and are kept to its surface, and since it acts on all bodies in proportion to their quantities of matter, “it accordingly constitutes their weight”. Two bodies alone in free space would fall toward each other and meet at a point nearer the place of the heavier.F 8–9 Of attraction in general he says only that it “seems rather to be infused than inherent”,F 6 and that a body alone in free space “could have no weight or gravity of itself”.F 38 The Cartesian vortices come up once, in his second lecture, where quicksilver and a cork in tubes of water on the whirling table demonstrate “the absurdity of the Cartesian doctrine of the planets moving round the sun in vortexes”, and “while gravity exists, there is no occasion for such vortexes”.F 35–36 What gravitation is, or what causes it, he does not ask. The Britannica’s “Mechanics” reprints these pages.EB iii 33–34EB iii 40 Its own entry “Æther”, in the first volume, goes further and puts the cause of gravity outside natural philosophy. Æther is “the name of an imaginary fluid” supposed to be the cause of gravity and of much else, and to look for the cause of gravity at all is to give way to a temptation:

“Even Sir Isaac Newton himself was not proof against this temptation. It was not enough that he had discovered the nature of light and colours, the application of gravity to the motions of the heavenly bodies, &c. he must go further, and attempt to assign the cause of gravity itself.”EB i 31

Why bodies weigh less near the equator

All three continental texts report that Jean Richer (1630–1696), at Cayenne in 1672, found that a pendulum beating seconds at Paris went too slowly there and had to be shortened.B 299N 149–150E 478 Nollet explains it by the daily rotation of the earth, which gives the parts of the globe centrifugal forces that are greatest at the equator and there oppose weight most; he ties it to the doubts that were then raised about the spherical figure of the earth.N 148–151 Erxleben takes Richer’s observation as “a new proof that the earth does in fact turn on its axis”.E 478 Biwald will not have it settled. His proposition is that the cause of the increase of gravity toward the poles is not yet sufficiently known. The flattened figure of the earth is the likeliest cause, but not beyond doubt until that figure is better determined. The centrifugal force has been computed for each latitude by Pierre Bouguer (1698–1758), and the shortening of pendulums that it would require “does not at all agree” with the shortening found by the most exact observations. A third opinion, that there is less attracting matter under the equator, he finds hardly probable.B 303–305 Ferguson mentions neither the pendulum nor any difference in weight. In his second lecture the earth is an oblate spheroid because its equatorial parts, moving quickest, have the greatest centrifugal force, as measured degrees confirm; he draws no consequence for weight.F 46–47

Why they differed

What an explanation has to be

Nollet asks for a cause that is “satisfying and intelligible”, and counts attraction by the will of God as abandoning the search. Biwald meets that demand with a long passage from the preface that Roger Cotes (1682–1716) wrote for Newton. Causes run in a chain from the compound to the simple, and the chain has an end:

“Of the simplest causes, therefore, no mechanical explanation can be given; for if one were given, the cause would not yet be the simplest. Will you then call these simplest causes occult, and order them into exile?”B 313

Causae igitur simplicissimae nulla dari potest mechanica explicatio: si daretur enim, causa nondum esset simplicissima. Has tu proinde causas simplicissimas appellabis occultas, & exulare iubebis?

Erxleben answers the same complaint in his own voice. It is objected that to derive gravity from an attractive force explains nothing at bottom:

“I have nothing to answer to this, except that it also explains nothing when one derives the motion produced by an impact from the impact. Why, then, it should not be permitted to use the words attractive force I do not see, so long as it is still permitted to use the words force of impact and inertia. All these words express only phenomena; but does the word body express more than a phenomenon?”E 85–86

Ich habe nichts darauf zu antworten, als daß es dann auch nichts erklärt, wenn man die durch einen Stoß hervorgebrachte Bewegung von dem Stosse herleitet. Warum es also nicht erlaubt seyn sollte, das Wort: anziehende Kraft zu gebrauchen, das sehe ich nicht ein, so lange es noch erlaubt ist, sich der Wörter: Kraft des Stosses und: Trägheit zu bedienen. Alle diese Wörter drücken nur Phänomena aus, aber drückt denn das Wort: Körper mehr, als ein Phänomenon aus?

Metaphysical objections to such a force weigh little with him, since our notion of moving force is still very dark, and we risk carrying over to bodies a notion drawn from the force of our own soul.E 85

Whirled vessels

Three of the texts argue from the same kind of apparatus. Nollet spins a glass globe of water with oil or a ball of wax in it, and the lighter body is driven to the axis, not to the centre. So Descartes’s thought on the physical cause of weight is “less just than ingenious”: on it, bodies would tend to different points of the earth’s axis.N 75–76 He tried Bilfinger’s two-axis arrangement as well, and reports from repeated trials before witnesses that the water does not take the two motions.N 77–78 Erxleben and Biwald use the same argument, Biwald citing Nollet’s own admission about Bilfinger’s machine.E 81–82B 317–318 On Ferguson’s whirling table the heavier body flies farthest from the centre, from which he concludes that a planet denser than its vortex would fly off from the sun.F 35–36 From such trials Erxleben, Biwald and Ferguson pass on to attraction. Nollet, who made the experiments, stops at the phenomena.

Mass, not surface

Biwald and Erxleben press one fact against every fluid. A fluid acts on the bodies that swim in it according to their volumes or surfaces; weight goes by mass.B 316–317E 83 Erxleben adds, as a second objection to all such fluids, that gravity acts on bodies at rest exactly as on bodies in motion. Christian Wolff (1679–1754) had replied that the gravific matter passes through bodies; Erxleben asks how, if it passes freely through them, it can act on them and move them.E 83 Biwald, quoting Father Roger Joseph Boscovich (1711–1787), puts the same dilemma. A vortex swift enough to cause weight would tear up and carry off everything in an instant; one that slips through bodies as through a sieve could not press them down so hard.B 317

Whether attraction shows itself on the earth

Both defenders of attraction look for it between bodies at hand, and they report different states of the evidence. Biwald, in 1769, gives it as the first objection to his view that if all bodies drew one another, light bodies falling near towers or the vastest mountains ought to approach them, and two spheres on a polished surface ought to seek each other, and that experience shows nothing of the kind. He answers that the mass of the globe so far exceeds that of any body on it that the effects can hardly fall under the senses. He has one experiment to offer, made by Bertier and reported by Pieter van Musschenbroek (1692–1761): thin strips hung by a hair inside tall glass bells move toward bodies brought up outside.B 319–320 Erxleben, in 1772, writes that experience has in fact taught that in very mountainous regions the mountains somewhat alter the direction of gravity, and that two flat glass plates seem to attract each other even with a silk hair lying between them.E 84

The rule of induction in Britain

Ferguson states the rule in his preface, in praise of Newton, who “insisted even in his early years, that it was high time to banish vague conjectures and hypotheses from natural philosophy, and to bring that science under an entire subjection to experiments and geometry”.F ix He assigns gravitation no cause. The Britannica’s entry “Æther” turns the same rule against Newton himself. Since Francis Bacon, Lord Verulam (1561–1626), it has been understood “that all knowledge must be derived from experiment and observation”. Newton, when he tried to assign the cause of gravity, went “in the way of conjecture, which will never lead any man to truth”, and philosophers have generally looked on the attempt as “the foible of a great man”.EB i 31–32

Questions the texts left open

  • The cause of gravity. Open for Nollet without qualification, a probable hypothesis for Erxleben, not asked by Ferguson, and for the Britannica’s “Æther” among the things that “must, and ever will, elude our researches”.EB i 31
  • Whether gravity belongs to matter as such. Nollet: one cannot say that gravity is essential to matter, since matter can be conceived without it.N 105 Ferguson: attraction seems infused, not inherent, and a body alone in free space “could have no weight or gravity of itself”.F 6F 38 Cotes, as Biwald quotes him: gravity must have a place among the primary qualities of all bodies, or extension, mobility and impenetrability have none.B 312
  • Why gravity grows toward the poles. Biwald concludes that nothing certain can be affirmed.B 305
  • Whether the approach of small bodies to one another can be made visible. Biwald calls Bertier’s experiment most worthy of being repeated more often by physicists.B 320
  • Bertier’s new vortices. Biwald puts off examining them until he treats the motions of the heavens.B 319
  • A book Erxleben cannot place. “No explanation of gravity is more mysterious”, he notes, than the one by Cadwallader Colden (1688–1776) that Abraham Gotthelf Kästner (1719–1800) translated with notes in 1748.E 84
