Snapshot c. 1775 · Natural philosophy

The terraqueous globe

What teaching texts from Graz, Göttingen, Paris and London said between 1768 and 1776 about the globe of land and water as a whole: its figure, the tides, springs, mountains, the sea, the inside of the earth, volcanoes and earthquakes, the Flood, and the age of the earth. They agree that the earth is flattened at the poles, that the moon raises the tides, and that volcanoes and earthquakes come from matter kindled underground; the three that take it up agree that the shells in the rocks were once sea creatures. They differ on whether the sea feeds springs from below, on where mountains and the shells on them came from, on a fire at the centre of the earth, on why the sea is salt and whether it is shrinking, on the Flood, on the theories of the earth, and on how old the earth is.

First cut, from one text per region. As in the entry on earth, the Paris text is Para du Phanjas, since Nollet has no lesson on the globe. The London text is Ferguson’s Lectures, which have no lecture on the globe either but treat the tides and earthquakes under headings of their own. The weather and climates, rivers and lakes in detail, and the measuring of mountains are not covered; the bodies dug from the earth are in the entry on earth, and the winds in the entry on meteors.

So, as in the entry on earth, the Paris text is François Para du Phanjas, whose course gives the globe a section of its own. Where the subject sat among the sciences

In Graz the globe is the sixth and last part of particular physics. Biwald opens by noting what is already done: the size and figure of the earth in the general physics, the bodies hidden in its bowels in the fourth part of this one. Now he will survey its outer and its inner structure, briefly.B 390 The tides he had put at the end of the general physics. They belong to particular physics, he says there, but follow from the moon’s motions almost as a corollary and confirm universal gravity, so that is where he set them out.B gen. 444 The part ends with the animals and plants on the globe’s surface, which he leaves untouched to the writers of natural history, so great is their extent and so narrow the bounds set for his book.B 402–403

In Göttingen the globe fills the second half of the thirteenth section, on the earth in particular, after the atmosphere and its meteors: the unevenness of the surface and the mountains, the smaller waters, the sea, the inside of the earth, the weather, and last “something more” on the origin of the world and of the earth and the changes they have undergone.E 580E 629 The figure of the earth is in the twelfth section, with the system of the world.E 476–477 The section ends with William Whiston (1667–1752), who has the earth melted by fire and then inhabited by the elect as a ball of crystal until the Last Judgement. Erxleben will not stay longer on a matter that is, he says, properly not even a subject of natural philosophy.E 646–647

In Paris, Para opens his third treatise by setting the earth apart from the system of the universe. Here it is an isolated globe; as a planet turning about its centre and about the sun it belongs to the treatise on astronomy. The first section asks what the globe shows, and since when it has existed.P ii 3–4 He reviews the theories of the earth first and then turns to “Nature and structure of the earth”.P ii 8 The tides, he says, depend on attraction and will be explained in his physical astronomy.P ii 33

Ferguson has no lecture on the globe. Its matter comes where his physics calls for it. The tides, under the margin heading “Of the tides”, and after them the figure of the earth close his second lecture, on central forces, as consequences of the centrifugal force shown on the whirling table.F 41F 46–47 Intermitting springs come in the hydraulic lecture, as a natural siphon.F 117 Earthquakes, under the heading “Earthquakes”, come in the lecture on pneumatics, after the damps and fire-damps of mines, and a sentence on volcanoes closes that passage.F 178–179 His two lectures on the globes teach the use of the artificial globe, and open with the earth’s roundness.F 247 On the disputes below (springs from the sea, the mountains, the shells, the central fire, the sea, the Flood and the theories of the earth) the Lectures say nothing.

The Britannica has no article on the globe either. “Terraqueous” is a term of geography, given to our globe because it consists of land and water,EB iii 892 and “Earth” sends the reader to Astronomy and Geography.EB ii 464 What it says on the subject is spread over short entries, such as Mountain, Sea, Earthquake, Vulcano, Deluge and Petrifaction, and passages of the treatises Mechanics, Astronomy, Pneumatics and Hydrostatics, which are Ferguson’s, reprinted. The short entries are its own, and a British reader of 1771 found the disputes there.

What the texts taught in common

  • The earth is flattened at the poles. Biwald recounts the measurements: Jean Picard (1620–1682) fixed the length of a degree in France, and when the pendulum of Jean Richer (1630–1696) cast doubt on a spherical earth, the Paris Academy sent expeditions to the equator and to the polar circle, whose degrees grow toward the poles. The earth therefore stands out at the equator and is pressed in at the poles, as the pendulums confirm and as Isaac Newton (1643–1727) had derived from attraction and the daily rotation.B gen. 343–346 Erxleben: the earth is a compressed spheroid, its equatorial diameter larger than its axis, by the measures of Pierre-Louis Moreau de Maupertuis (1698–1759) and Pierre Bouguer (1698–1758), though the difference is small enough that for many purposes it may be taken as a perfect sphere.E 476–477 Para opens his account of the earth’s structure with it:

    “Earth and sea together form a spheroid, or a kind of globe, swollen at its equator and flattened at its poles.”P ii 8

    La Terre & la Mer forment ensemble un sphéroïde, ou une espece de globe, renflé vers son équateur & applati vers ses poles.

    He takes the polar radius to be six or seven leagues shorter, a mean between Maupertuis’s measure in the north and those taken at the equator, at the Cape, in Italy and in France.P ii 10–11 Ferguson: all globes whose parts can yield and which turn on their axes are oblate spheroids, since the parts at the equator move quickest and so have the greatest centrifugal force; two thin hoops whirled on an axis swell out at the middle.F 46

    “That our earth is of this figure, is demonstrable from actual measurement of some degrees on its surface, which are found to be longer in the frigid zones than in the torrid”F 46–47

    The difference makes the earth’s diameter at the equator 36 miles longer than its axis, and the water’s centrifugal force keeps the sea from running off to the poles.F 47 The Britannica’s “Mechanics” reprints the passage,EB iii 43 and its “Astronomy”, from Ferguson’s Astronomy, says only that the earth is round like a globe, from its shadow in eclipses of the moon, from the masts of ships, and from voyages round it.EB i 440
  • The moon, helped by the sun, raises the tides. Biwald notes that the ancients already tied the tides to the moon without knowing how, and that their laws were fixed only after 1701, when the French ports kept records on the Academy’s method; the action of sun and moon explains them, and confirms universal gravity.B gen. 444B gen. 447–449 Erxleben seeks the true cause of ebb and flood in the attracting force of the moon, with the sun adding its share at new and full moon, while the earth’s turning carries the swelling westward.E 605–606E 607 Para calls the tides “a consequence of the attraction between the earth, the moon and the sun”.P ii 33 Ferguson gives the tides a heading of their own in his lecture on central forces. That they rise on the side next the moon, he says, “every one understands to be owing to the moon’s attraction”; what is less understood is why they rise as high at the same moment on the far side.F 41F 42 He answers with an experiment on the whirling table. The moon draws the near side of the earth more than its centre, and the centre more than the far side; and since the earth goes round the common centre of gravity of earth and moon every month, the centrifugal force of the far side raises the water there as high as the moon raises it on the near side. The experiment, he writes, is to the best of his knowledge entirely new.F 44F 45 He does not mention the sun here. The Britannica’s “Mechanics” reprints the experiment,EB iii 43 and its “Astronomy”, from Ferguson’s Astronomy, credits the discovery to Johannes Kepler (1571–1630), who made the moon’s attracting power draw the waters, and says Newton made the theory his own by explaining the tide on the side away from the moon.EB i 473
  • The shells and fishes found in the rocks were sea creatures. Biwald: shells are dug up in great quantity on the highest mountains and on plains, and whole mountains of compacted shells occur in Styria.B 395 Erxleben: sea animals turned to stone are found everywhere in the earth, lying in orderly layers as they lay in the sea when they lived.E 640–641 Para finds heaps and layers of sea shells and whole petrified fishes on every continent, far from the sea and on the highest mountains.P ii 42 An anonymous author who held that such shells grew in the earth by the laws of vegetation and crystallization, and were never sea creatures, might as well say, Para replies, that the urns and furniture of Herculaneum grew in the earth too.P ii 71–72 Ferguson does not mention them. The Britannica’s own “Petrifaction” takes petrified bodies to be mostly of vegetable or animal origin: sea shells, the teeth and bones of fish, the bones of land animals.EB iii 474
  • The layers of the earth do not lie in the order of their weight. Biwald, against John Woodward (1665–1728): the strata are often inclined to the horizon, and lighter bodies, such as coal, lie beneath the hardest rocks and metals.B 394 Erxleben finds the layers roughly horizontal in pits, but not ordered by weight, and draws a conclusion:

    “the different layers of the earth, which do not always lie according to the proper weight of the matters they are made of, seem to prove that they arose not in one but in several floods.”E 641

    die verschiedenen Schichten der Erde, die nicht immer nach dem eigentlichen Gewichte derer Materien liegen, woraus sie bestehen, zu beweisen, daß sie nicht bey einer, sondern mehrern Ueberschwemmungen entstanden sind.

    Para describes layers parallel, upright, inclined, curved and wavy, with heavy matters often lying on light ones, an image of chaos and of a world in ruin.P ii 40–41 Ferguson does not take this up, nor do the Britannica entries read.
  • Volcanoes burn with matter kindled underground by fermentation. Biwald ascribes eruptions to a fermentation from the mixture of acid and sulphurous matters, which art imitates; naphtha, petroleum and resins make the fires last. He tells of the night ascent of Vesuvius by Athanasius Kircher SJ (1602–1680), and of a recent eruption whose lava, cooled, proved a mixture of metals and inflammable matter.B 398–400 Erxleben:

    “the central fire is therefore probably innocent of their fury, which seems to come from an underground weathering and similar causes”E 615

    das Centralfeuer ist also wohl unschuldig an ihrer Wuth, die von einer unterirdischen Verwitterung und ähnlichen Ursachen herzurühren scheint

    The matter thrown out, he says, hardly comes from a great depth, and the regions of volcanoes are full of sulphur, as the Grotta del Cane and the Solfatara near Naples show.E 614–615 Para: fires hidden in the mountains, lit when great masses of combustible matter ferment, strengthened by the spring of the air and the vapour of water; volcanoes are vents of the earth, a provision of Providence against worse disasters.P ii 24–26 Ferguson gives volcanoes one sentence, at the end of his passage on earthquakes (below): from the artificial earthquake “we have a very natural account of the fires of mount Ætna, Vesuvius, and other volcanos”, which were probably set on fire at first by such a mixture of metalline and sulphureous particles.F 179 The Britannica’s own “Vulcano” gives the same experiment as Nicolas Lemery’s (1645–1715): fifty pounds of sulphur and iron filings, tempered with water and buried, threw up flames after eight or nine hours, and from this the entry takes the true cause of the fires of Etna and Vesuvius.EB iii 931
  • Earthquakes come from the same underground fires. Biwald: their causes are much the same as those of volcanoes, which is why they are commonest where there are volcanoes, metal mines and sulphurous ground.B 400B 402 Erxleben: they can hardly come from the central fire, but rather from other fires arisen under the earth, whose heat expands the air shut in caverns and may turn water into vapour.E 637 Para makes underground fires the only or the principal cause, and for shocks felt far away without any eruption, as at Lisbon, he quotes Georges-Louis Leclerc, comte de Buffon (1707–1788): air made by burning pyrites rushes through underground passages as a furious wind.P ii 25–31 Ferguson puts them under a heading of their own in his lecture on pneumatics, after the fire-damps of mines:

    “When such combustible matter, as is above-mentioned, kindles in the bowels of the earth, where there is little or no vent, it produces earthquakes, and violent storms or hurricanes of wind when it breaks forth into the air.”F 178

    An artificial earthquake is made by kneading ten or fifteen pounds of sulphur and as much iron filings with water into a paste and burying it; in eight or ten hours it bursts out in flames and makes the earth tremble all round to a considerable distance.F 178F 179 The Britannica’s “Earthquake” refers to its “Pneumatics”, which reprints the passage.EB ii 464EB iii 491
  • Springs are fed, at least in great part, by rain and snow. Biwald gives this opinion its strongest ground: springs rise only in mountainous country, where snow lies all winter and rain, fog and vapours are most frequent.B 392–393 Erxleben: rain and snow beyond doubt supply a great part, which is why springs are rare in deserts where it never rains, and Edme Mariotte (c. 1620–1684) reckoned the Seine needs only a sixth of the rain that falls on its basin.E 592 Para makes the rain the only source (below).P ii 356 Ferguson does not take up the origin of springs in general, but his intermitting springs are fed by rain: it falls on the side of a hill, sinks through its pores and crannies into a cavity, and runs out by a channel as through a siphon until the cavity is empty.F 117 His Supplement explains reciprocating springs and ebbing and flowing wells in the same way, from a cavern “filled or fed by rains and melted snow”.F Suppl. 20 The Britannica’s “Hydrostatics” reprints the first passage, and its own “Sea” makes the vapours raised from the sea and falling on the land a circulation of water.EB ii 808EB iii 573

The disputes

Does the sea feed springs from below?

Biwald rejects the old answer that the sea, drawn under the land and distilled by underground fires, condenses in the vaults of caverns and runs out as springs:

“But this opinion invents many things in the bosom of the earth whose existence is proved by no argument: chemical laboratories, alembics, underground ducts and channels, which it places at will wherever the sources of springs occur.”B 392

Verum haec sententia plurima in sinu terrae effingit, quorum existentia nullo argumento evincitur: Chemica nempe laboratoria, alembicos, subterraneos ductus, & canales ad libitum iis in locis collocat, ubi fontium scaturigines occurrunt.

He reports that Edmond Halley (1656–1742) brings vapours from the sea to condense on the mountain ridges, and adds that the electric fluid may carry vapours there too.B 392–393 Para argues the same way at length. René Descartes (1596–1650) made the globe a chemist’s still, with a central fire under caverns of sea water, but the fire is a chimera and the vapours would condense before they rose. Filtration through the earth would leave behind, every thirty years, a mass of salt equal to all the water the rivers carry to the sea in a year, and capillary tubes raise water no more than thirty-two feet and let none flow out.P ii 349–354 Erxleben does not exclude the sea. Rain cannot be the only source: Britain gets from rain and snow only half the water its rivers carry off, and there are springs high on mountains and springs that run as much in drought. Caverns joined to the sea may send up vapours, and near coasts sea water may seep through the earth, though not by rising as in capillary tubes.E 593–594 Ferguson does not take the question up. The Britannica’s “Sea”, its own entry, gives as the first of two reasons why the sea does not grow from the rivers that run into it that “waters return from the sea by subterranean cavities and aqueducts, through various parts of the earth”.EB iii 573

Where do the mountains come from?

Biwald reports that some think the mountains as old as the earth, from their usefulness:

“These mountains seem to some to be coeval with the earth itself, founded with it”B 393

Montes hi quibusdam telluri ipsi coaevi videntur, cum eaque conditi

He rejects the theory of Thomas Burnet (c. 1635–1715), that the earth was flat until the Flood broke its crust, and that of Woodward, that the Flood dissolved stones and metals and let them settle by weight.B 393–394 His own leaning is to fire. Some physicists hold that many mountains were raised by underground fires, some while the earth was still under water near the time of creation, others later from the shell-filled floor of the sea, all once volcanic, and he finds the arguments “probable enough”: eruptions have made new islands, fossil coal shows old underground fires everywhere, shells lie on summits and plains, and mountains are built of jumbled strata and loose boulders.B 395–396

“All this, moreover, seems more rightly ascribed to the eruption of underground fires than to deluges or floods.”B 396

Haec porro omnia subterraneorum ignium eruptioni rectius tribui videntur, quam diluviis, aut inundationibus.

Erxleben holds that a great many mountains are original, perhaps as old as the earth, whatever Burnet may dream to the contrary, since without mountains from the beginning there could have been no rivers. Others are plainly later: their layers, with petrified animals and plants between them, show that water washed them together in great changes of the surface. Had they all come from earthquakes they would not be layered so regularly inside.E 642–643 Para divides the mountains in two. The great chains, much higher in the New World and running north to south there but west to east in the Old, defeat every physical system:P ii 20–21

“We must therefore necessarily acknowledge that these antediluvian mountains owe their origin purely and simply to the creative action of the Almighty”P ii 22

Il faut donc nécessairement reconnoître que ces montagnes anté-diluviennes doivent purement & simplement leur origine à l’action créatrice du Tout-puissant

Lower mountains with fossil shells, trees and bones are another matter. The Flood, earthquakes, eruptions, hurricanes and the overflowing of rivers and seas heaped them up, and some sand hills of Africa raised by hurricanes may become mountains in which posterity will find petrified travellers.P ii 22–23 Ferguson does not ask how the mountains came to be; they appear in his lectures only as too small to spoil the earth’s roundness, no known hill being three miles high, and as turning the winds.F 247F 174 The Britannica’s entry “Mountain” gives only a definition.EB iii 313

How did the shells come to lie on the mountains?

For Biwald, as for the oysters found on the island that rose from the sea at Santorini in 1707, eruptions carried them up from the sea floor. Had they settled from subsiding waters, they would lie in strata matching their weight.B 395–396 Erxleben concludes from their orderly layers that the land was once the floor of the sea:

“makes it particularly probable that the land we now inhabit was formerly the bottom of the sea”E 641

macht es insbesondere wahrscheinlich, daß das Land, das wir ietzt bewohnen, vor diesem der Boden des Meeres gewesen sey

Their orderly position, he goes on, makes it incredible that floods brought them from the sea, still less earthquakes, as Anton Lazzaro Moro (1687–1764) believes. What event changed the surface so, we have no record, and perhaps can have none.E 641 For Para the heaps of shells and fishes on every continent, which a celebrated author calls the medals of the Flood, prove the universal Flood of Scripture and nothing else.P ii 42 To the objection that one year of Flood could hardly lay down so many layers he answers that its currents, turned by the primitive mountains, could lay down any number, and that shifts of the sea after changes in the earth’s centre of gravity may also have left shell-covered shores dry.P ii 68–69 Neither Ferguson nor the Britannica entries read say how the remains came where they lie.

Is there a fire at the centre of the earth?

Biwald rejects it:

“I judge, then, that the existence of a central fire is established by no argument.”B 397

Censeo itaque existentiam ignis centralis nullo argumento effici.

If there were one, heat would grow the deeper one went, which experience denies, and flame cannot last without a steady supply of fresh air, which is not to be had near the centre.B 397–398 Para calls it the philosophers’ version of the people’s hell under the earth, “a chimerical central fire, whose anti-philosophical idea is diametrically opposed to all that experience and reason teach us about the theory of fire”.P ii 31–33 Erxleben inclines the other way:

“There are also perhaps not unimportant grounds for assuming an underground fire or a central fire.”E 613

Man hat auch vielleicht nicht unerhebliche Gründe, ein unterirdisches Feuer oder ein Centralfeuer anzunehmen.

He follows Jean-Jacques Dortous de Mairan (1678–1771), who ascribes much of the earth’s warmth to it.E 613–614 He does not, however, make it the cause of volcanoes or earthquakes (above). Ferguson and the Britannica entries read do not mention it.

Has electricity a part in earthquakes?

Biwald takes the fermentation of sulphurous matter to be the cause, and adds electricity:

“And this opinion seems truest if, as we said above, account is also taken of electricity.”B 402

Haecque opinio verissima videtur, si, quod superius diximus, electricitatis quoque ratio habeatur.

Sulphur, bitumen and other fatty bodies are electrics, and electric vapour made in quantity by their rubbing, dissolving or melting can rush into negatively electric bodies underground, shake them and burst them. The fires seen on the Schneeberg in the latest earthquake, the bellowing of the ground, and the alternate rocking and jolting all match the discharge of coated glass. The falling in of caverns, after Lucretius (c. 99–c. 55 BC), is an effect, not a cause, and air shut in the earth cannot explain shocks that return for months.B 402 Erxleben reports that some natural philosophers take earthquakes to be, at least in part, effects of the electric force, and that Mayer, presumably Tobias Mayer (1723–1762) of Göttingen, explained them by a sudden change in the direction of gravity.E 637–638 Para, Ferguson and the Britannica do not mention electricity among the causes; in the same lecture Ferguson makes thunder and lightning, too, a fermentation of sulphureous and nitrous effluvia.F 176–177

Why is the sea salt?

Biwald gives the received cause: salt, nitre, sulphur and bitumen mixed with the seas from the very beginning of the world, the saltness kept steady because evaporation takes off as much fresh water as the rivers bring. Those who put mountains of salt at the bottom of the sea cannot say why these are not dissolved at last, and the sea made far saltier.B 391 Para agrees, with the same objection, and adds two purposes: Providence gave the sea its saltness to keep it from corrupting and to provide salt for plants and animals.P ii 255–256 Ferguson does not ask; his table of specific gravities gives sea water as 1.030, pump water as 1.000.F 159 The Britannica’s own “Sea” takes the side Biwald and Para reject:

“it is very rationally judged to arise from great multitudes both of mines and mountains of salt, dispersed here and there in the depths of the sea.”EB iii 573

It then reports Halley’s view that most of the salt comes from the rivers, so that the saltness must grow with time.EB iii 573 Erxleben asks the questions without answering them: if the salt comes from dissolving beds, why is the sea not saturated (perhaps it is, at depth)? Does it get all its salt from the rivers? Was it created salt? Is salt perhaps made in the sea?E 602–603

Is the sea growing less?

Biwald reports that Carl Linnaeus (1707–1778) seems to hold that the sea is always decreasing, and that many report it leaving the shores. Against this he sets difficulties of great weight, above all signs that elsewhere the sea has advanced over land as much as it has retreated.B 391–392 Erxleben will not decide:

“The question seems not yet able to be decided”E 644

Die Frage scheint wohl noch nicht entschieden werden zu können

It cannot be settled, he goes on, until longer experience has been gathered from nearly all the coasts. Even a lower sea would not prove less water, he adds, since its bed may change, and the turning of water into earth that some claim to have shown remains doubtful.E 644–645 Para holds that the sea is displaced, not diminished. It has fallen at the old port of Marseilles, Aigues-Mortes, Fréjus, Ravenna, Rosetta and Damietta, and risen on the coasts of Holland and parts of the Indies. When an earthquake opens a vast cavern under the sea, the earth’s centre of gravity shifts, and the sea rises in one region and falls in another.P ii 36–38P ii 52–55 Ferguson does not take it up, and the Britannica’s “Sea” explains why the sea does not grow from the rivers and does not ask whether it is growing less.EB iii 573

Did the Flood cover the whole earth?

Para says all naturalists agree that the globe was once buried under the waters, and that the monuments prove the universal Flood of Scripture. Some hold that it struck only the lands of the Tigris and Euphrates, where all mankind then lived.P ii 42–44

“A claim diametrically opposed both to sacred history and to natural history!”P ii 44

Prétention diamétralement opposée & à l’histoire sainte & à l’histoire naturelle!

Sacred history raises the waters fifteen cubits above the highest mountains, and natural history finds its monuments on every continent.P ii 44 Ferguson’s Lectures do not mention the Flood. The Britannica’s own “Deluge” calls Noah’s flood the most memorable of the deluges recorded, one which “overflowed and destroyed the whole earth”.EB ii 414 Erxleben reports both views: many believe the whole earth was covered and then struggle to say where so much water came from, while others hold that only a part of the earth’s surface was flooded.E 639–640 Biwald explains the shells and the mountains without the Flood and does not take up the question.

Can the Flood be explained by natural causes?

Para says no:

“This universal flood is plainly impossible in the natural order of things and according to the general laws that animate nature”P ii 43

Ce déluge universel est évidemment impossible dans l’ordre naturel des choses, & selon les loix générales qui animent la nature

No physical cause could give the earth such a volume of water, or force the waters against gravity above the mountains. The Almighty, who keeps and interrupts the laws of nature at will, worked it by miracle.P ii 43 An English author who explained it by Cartesian vortices understood neither Scripture nor Descartes, and Whiston’s comet would have had to come back for its tail.P ii 45 The Britannica is as short:

“Many attempts have been made to account for the deluge by means of natural causes: but these attempts have only tended to discredit philosophy, and to render their authors ridiculous.”EB ii 414

Erxleben reports three natural accounts without adopting any. Burnet has water shut in the earth break out when the sun dried the crust. Whiston has the comet of 1680 brush the earth with its tail, though it is hard to see how the comet got away again. Woodward has God suspend the laws of cohesion and gravity in part.E 639–640 Biwald does not take up the question.

Are the theories of the earth to be believed?

Para reviews the theories of Whiston, Burnet, Woodward, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) and Buffon, whose comet striking the sun he calls a sublime jest with nothing in common with that author’s true principles, and dismisses them all:P ii 4–8

“But let us leave the romances, and seek the truth.”P ii 8

Mais laissons les romans, & cherchons la vérité.

Biwald takes up two of them, Burnet’s and Woodward’s, and refutes both.B 393–394 Erxleben sets out those of Descartes, Burnet, Whiston, Leibniz with Benoît de Maillet (1656–1738), and Buffon in turn, and then leaves the choice to the reader:E 630–636

“Here, then, are theories enough: whoever cares to choose none of them would perhaps do best not to ask the natural philosophers at all how the earth and the world were created.”E 636

Hier sind also Theorien genug: wer noch keine darunter wählen mag, der thut vielleicht am besten, wenn er gar nicht von den Naturforschern zu lernen verlangt, wie die Erde und die Welt geschaffen sind.

Ferguson does not take the theories up. The Britannica judges only the natural accounts of the Flood (above).EB ii 414

How old is the earth?

Para answers from the sacred books, “the most ancient, the most authentic, the most irrefutable historical monuments”:P ii 72

“the existence of the earth goes back beyond the present year 1771 only about 5808, or 7572, years”P ii 72

l’existence de la terre ne remonte au-delà de l’année présente 1771, qu’à environ 5808, ou 7572 ans

The two figures are those of the Hebrew text and of the Septuagint, whose difference he discusses later. Against the far greater ages of Egyptian, Chaldean, Indian and Chinese fables he states a proposition:P ii 72–73

“The opinion that gives the earth more antiquity than Holy Scripture gives it is founded on no solid proof drawn from physics, history or astronomy.”P ii 73

L’opinion qui donne à la terre plus d’antiquité que ne lui en donne l’Ecriture sainte, n’est fondée sur aucune preuve solide, tirée de la physique, de l’histoire, de l’astronomie.

Everything seen on and in the earth comes from its first constitution, the Flood and natural causes over a few thousand years; nations, sciences and arts are all recent.P ii 73–75 The eternal earth of Aristotle (384–322 BC) and the endless changes of de Maillet’s Telliamed deserve no attention from a physicist.P ii 62–63 Erxleben declines the question:

“That it had a beginning I cannot and may not prove here; and that it was brought forth by a wise, mighty, good Creator, the contemplation of it teaches every reasonable person.”E 629

Daß sie einen Anfang gehabt habe, kann und darf ich hier nicht erweisen; und daß sie von einem weisen, mächtigen, gütigen Schöpfer hervorgebracht worden sey, lehrt die Betrachtung derselben einen ieden Vernünftigen.

He reports Moses’s account that the Flood came about 1600 years after the creation, without a reckoning of his own.E 639 Ferguson gives no argument, but his eleventh lecture shows how to calculate new and full moons and eclipses from the creation of the world to the year of Christ 5800, and its table of the moon’s mean motion counts the “Years of the World” from 0 in 4008 before Christ.F 377F 393 He takes the date for granted and does not say where it comes from. The Britannica gives no age, but reports Halley’s proposal for finding one (below, under the open questions).EB iii 573 Biwald does not take it up.

Why they differed

Where physics stops

The texts draw the line between what natural philosophy can explain and what it cannot in different places. Para draws it at the creation. The great chains of mountains admit no physical explanation, because they were made by a free Creator who gave the globe its shape “before subjecting both earth and heaven to physical laws”; only what came after, such as the lesser mountains, the fossils and the changes of the sea, calls for physical causes.P ii 22 The same line runs through his account of the minerals, where he writes that physics should seek no mechanism in the first forming of the globe.P ii 229 Erxleben draws it at what can be known. The theories he set out show only, in his view, that natural philosophers do not know how the earth was made, and he closes on the end of the world as a matter that is properly not even a subject of natural philosophy.E 636E 646–647 Biwald draws no such line. He lets underground fires raise mountains while the earth was still under water, near the time of creation, and argues for it from eruptions, coal and strata as he would for any other effect.B 396 Ferguson does not raise the question. The Britannica draws the line at the Flood, where natural accounts only make philosophy ridiculous.EB ii 414

Moses as a historian

Two texts use Scripture as a record of the earth’s past. Against Burnet the first argument Biwald gives is the authority of Moses, the most ancient of historians, whose account the theory does not fit.B 394 In his general physics he had read Joshua’s command to the sun as language accommodated to common understanding (see the entry on the system of the world); here, on the history of the earth, Moses is evidence. Para builds his chronology on the sacred books as the oldest and most authentic historical monuments, and opposes a partial Flood with the fifteen cubits of Genesis.P ii 72P ii 44 Erxleben reports Moses on the date of the Flood and argues from the rocks alone.E 639E 640–641 Ferguson does not argue from Scripture, though the tables of his eleventh lecture date the creation (above).

The same warm cellars

The texts that differ on the central fire cite the same facts. Erxleben’s grounds for it are that higher places are always colder, that frost never goes deep, that the sea never freezes at the bottom, and that a little below the surface a nearly constant warmth is felt; with Mairan, he reckons this underlying warmth at Paris to be 393 times what the sun alone gives there on the shortest day.E 613–614 Biwald answers the same phenomena with heat continually made near the surface by friction, fermentation and effervescence.B 398 Para explains the constant ten degrees above freezing in deep cellars by the free elementary fire lodged evenly in all bodies, which the seasons do not reach.P ii 32–33 Biwald and Para both rest their denial on the theory of fire treated earlier in their courses: fire cannot last without fresh air and new food, and there is neither at the centre.B 398P ii 33

Purposes

Para gives purposes freely, with one reservation: if it is permitted to sound the Creator’s designs and seek the final cause of things, the mountains were made chiefly to keep up the springs.P ii 19 The sea was given its saltness against corruption, and volcanoes are vents provided against worse disasters.P ii 256P ii 26 The Britannica’s “Sea” argues the same way: the sea needed great cavities so as not to overspread the land, and its salt keeps it sweet and makes it bear larger ships.EB iii 572–573 Ferguson gives none. Biwald reports the argument from the uses of mountains as the reason others give for thinking them coeval with the earth, and does not adopt it.B 393 Erxleben’s argument for original mountains, that rivers need them, is an argument from necessity, not from purpose.E 642–643

One cause or several

On springs Erxleben concludes that “several causes must nevertheless beyond doubt contribute to the making of springs”.E 593 Para has a name for that position:

“to the influence of a certain and sufficient cause they join the influence of a fabulous and chimerical cause, which serves for nothing and is a great encumbrance”P ii 369

à l’influence d’une cause certaine & suffisante, ils associent l’influence d’une cause fabuleuse & chimérique, qui ne sert à rien, & qui embarrasse beaucoup

He compares the conciliators to a mechanic who, bored with a cart rolling easily on four wheels, added a fifth crosswise.P ii 369 Rain and snow, he says, are the one simple cause that satisfies all the phenomena; Erxleben’s doubt comes from figures, such as the rain of Britain against its rivers and the springs on mountain tops, which Para answers with Mariotte’s figures for the Seine.P ii 356–359E 593

Questions the texts left open

  • Whether earthquakes can be foretold. Biwald:

    “It is commonly asked whether there are any signs foretelling earthquakes. I answer that there are none certain and stable.”B 401

    Quaeri solet, an quaedam terrae motuum prognostica habeantur. Respondeo, nulla certa, ac stabilia esse.

    The earthquake that had frightened Graz a few weeks before came with violent winds and dense clouds at Vienna but with the greatest calm at Graz, the barometer standing above 27 inches and not moving during the shock. Nor is it certain that earthquakes come more often in spring and autumn, or at night: Graz was last shaken in the third hour of the night, and a few months earlier at half past noon.B 401
  • Whether the earth’s axis has ever moved. Erxleben asks it in passing: “Has the earth also perhaps once changed its axis?”E 641 Para thinks it very probable, with Noël-Antoine Pluche (1688–1761), that before the Flood the axis stood upright to the ecliptic, giving an eternal spring. The Egyptians and Chaldeans remembered that time, and it would explain the long lives of the patriarchs. His own explanation is that the masses of earth heaped up by the Flood in one hemisphere tipped the axis.P ii 45–51
  • Why the sea shines at night. Biwald: from the smallest phosphorescent insects living in sea water, as Linnaeus fully proved in his dissertation on the Noctiluca marina.B 392 Erxleben: from the worms in it, and perhaps from other causes not yet well enough known.E 603–604
  • How regular the figure is. Biwald notes that the measured degrees do not grow by any fixed law, as they would in an earth of uniform make, and that the attraction of great mountains turned the plumb lines aside: Bouguer and Charles-Marie de La Condamine (1701–1774) at Chimborazo, Father Roger Joseph Boscovich (1711–1787) and Father Christopher Maire (1697–1767) in the Apennines.B gen. 345 Erxleben: it is not yet settled whether all meridians are alike, or whether the southern half is shaped exactly like the northern.E 477
  • What is inside the earth. Erxleben: the deepest mines come nowhere near the six-thousandth part of the radius, so nothing can be said with certainty, but the earth cannot be wholly hollow, or the attraction of dense mountains would be greater than it is found.E 613 Biwald lists underground lakes and rivers, such as the Rhône, which disappears five leagues from Geneva and comes out again.B 397
  • Whether the sea’s salt could date the world. The Britannica reports Halley’s proposal that if experiments in different ages found the saltness of the same water growing, “it would be easy from thence, by rules of proportion, to find the age of the world very nearly”.EB iii 573
