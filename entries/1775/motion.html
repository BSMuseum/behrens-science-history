Snapshot c. 1775 · Natural philosophy

Motion and the balance of forces

What teaching texts from Graz, Göttingen, Paris and London said about motion, force and collision between 1759 and 1776. They agree on how to reckon a quantity of motion and on the rules of collision. They differ on whether inertia is a force, on how one body passes motion to another, and on the old quarrel over the measure of force.

First cut, from one text per region, on the principles of motion. The pendulum, the inclined plane, projectiles, central forces and the machines are not yet covered.

Where motion sat among the sciences

Biwald treats motion twice. In the first part of his general physics, after body in general, he gives the plain rules: uniform and compound motion, fall, inclined planes, the balance of weights, collision and the simple machines.B 95–162 In the second part, where he builds everything on forces between points of matter, he returns to what those rules rest on, and there argues about contact and inertia.B 273 Erxleben gives motion his third section, after bodies in general and before gravity; collision and the composition of forces fall within it.E 35 Nollet spends his third and fourth lessons on motion, with experiments on hanging balls of ivory and lead.N 179 Ferguson spreads it over his first three lectures. Inactivity is a property of matter in the first;F 2 the laws of motion open the second, on central forces;F 19–21 and the quantity of motion opens the third, on the mechanical powers, under the marginal heading “The foundation of all mechanics”.F 47 The Britannica, which reprints the three lectures as one treatise, “Mechanics”, gives the reason for joining them: the first two are needed for a thorough knowledge of the third.EB iii 32

What the texts taught in common

  • A quantity of motion is the mass multiplied by the velocity. Erxleben: the sizes of motions are as the products of masses into velocities, and forces can be compared the same way, since they are proportional to their effects.E 42 Biwald reports that from the beginning of the world the quantity of motion was reckoned so, and reckons the momentum of balanced forces in the same way, as the absolute force multiplied by the initial velocity.B 112–113 Ferguson: “the whole momentum or quantity of force of a moving body is the result of its quantity of matter multiplied by the velocity with which it is moved.” A body of 40 pounds moving two miles a minute and one of 4 pounds moving twenty would strike an obstacle with equal forces.F 47–48 The Britannica prints the same sentence.EB iii 43 Nollet says it had always been thought so.N 198–199
  • Forces balance when the quantities of motion on each side are equal. Two bodies joined by a rigid rod on a fulcrum are in equilibrium when, if they moved, their quantities of motion would be equal.B 113 On this principle, says Ferguson, “depends the whole of mechanics”: in any machine, the excess of weight in one body is made up by the excess of velocity in the other.F 48 The Britannica repeats it.EB iii 43–44 Erxleben and Nollet were not read on this point; their treatments of the machines are still to come.
  • Action and reaction are equal. Erxleben: “Whoever can doubt that this reaction is always equal to the action that occasioned it must necessarily form a most incorrect idea of it.”E 45 Biwald deduces it from the mutual action of the points of matter, and notes that Father Roger Joseph Boscovich (1711–1787) derives the laws of collision from it.B 272 In Nollet’s hanging balls, the ball at rest takes part of the motion of the one that strikes it, and the striker loses what the other gains.N 180 Ferguson does not list it among the laws of motion in the parts read, but uses it as a known principle in hydrostatics: “action and reaction are equal and contrary to each other”.F 106 The Britannica’s entry “Inertia” has bodies resist as much as they are resisted.EB ii 840
  • Colliding bodies follow the same rules in the three continental texts. Hard or soft bodies move on together after the blow, and elastic ones spring apart.B 120–138E 58–60N 289ff Erxleben and Nollet both warn that nature has no perfectly hard bodies, so experiment can only approach the rules.E 66–67N 289 Ferguson’s course has no section on collision; the Britannica’s entry “Collision” sends the reader to “Mechanics”, which is Ferguson’s text and does not treat it either.

The disputes

Is inertia a force?

Nollet calls it one and proves it by experiment. A lead ball hanging at rest is struck by another; if the ball at rest made no resistance, both would move on with the whole of the first ball’s motion, and they do not. A heavier ball, struck the same way, is carried less far.

“A body at rest therefore makes a real resistance to the effort that tends to move it. […] There is therefore something more to overcome than a mere privation of motion.”N 180–181

Un corps en repos fait donc une résistance réelle à l’effort qui tend à le mouvoir. […] Il y a donc quelque chose de plus à vaincre, qu’une seule privation de mouvement.

He names this resistance the force of inertia, proportional to the quantity of matter like weight, and argues that it is neither the air nor weight itself, since it acts in a vacuum and in every direction.N 179–183 Ferguson makes “inactivity, or passiveness” the second property of matter, “by which it always endeavours to continue in the state that it is in, whether of rest or motion”. A body with twice or thrice the matter has twice or thrice the inactivity, and needs twice or thrice the force to set it moving or to stop it.F 2 Force must be applied “to overcome its inactivity or resistance”, and the moon goes round the earth “although she be as unactive and dead as any stone upon it”;F 3 but he names no force of inertia, and does not ask what the resistance is. The Britannica’s “Mechanics” prints the same words.EB iii 32 Its own entry “Inertia” gives two definitions by Isaac Newton (1642–1727) side by side without remark: “a passive principle by which bodies persist in their motion or rest”, and “a power implanted in all matter, whereby it resists any change endeavoured to be made in its state.”EB ii 840

Erxleben reports that the property has been regarded as a force peculiar to bodies, and asks:

“But does a thing then need a force of its own to remain what it once is? Can one well conceive a force that never acts of itself but only resists? That has no size of its own, but is great or small according to what it resists?”E 45

Aber braucht denn ein Ding eine eigne Kraft um das zu bleiben, was es ein Mahl ist? Läßt sich wohl eine Kraft gedenken, die niemahls von selbst wirkt sondern nur widersteht? die gar keine Grösse für sich hat, sondern nur groß oder klein ist, nachdem das ist, dem sie sich widersetzt?

For him the proposition that bodies have inertia says only that something must set a body moving and something must stop it: “So inertia is in fact nothing other than the principle of sufficient reason applied to the changes of state of bodies.”E 46

Biwald goes furthest. Bodies, he holds, do not positively resist a change of state at all:

“For there is in bodies a motive force, which, as the metaphysicians teach, consists in a continual striving or endeavour to change place. But if bodies positively resisted a change of state, there would also be in them a striving or endeavour to keep their place; and these endeavours, being wholly opposed to each other, cannot be in the same substance.”B 279

Datur enim in corporibus vis motrix, quae, veluti metaphysici docent, consistit in continuo nisu, sive conatu mutandi locum. Quod si vero corpora mutationi status positive resisterent, daretur in iis quoque nisus, sive conatus conservandi loci; qui conatus tanquam sibi omnino oppositi eidem substantiae inesse non possunt.

He goes on to deny that the first law, a body’s persistence in rest or uniform straight motion, can be proved either from the nature of bodies or from the phenomena, since nothing in nature is ever absolutely at rest or moves uniformly in a straight line.B 275–278 He keeps the ordinary language of mechanics, that bodies resist motion and destroy part of the motion of a body striking them, provided physics says plainly that this resistance or reaction “is more rightly to be called the mutual action of bodies”.B pref.

How one body passes motion to another

For Nollet, collision is a matter of contact. There is no perfectly hard matter, so when one body strikes another, even one too large to move, “a dent is always made at the place of contact”.N 289 Erxleben declines to say what happens:

“But how this communication of motion properly comes about remains at bottom, for us who do not know bodies in their inner constitution, an unsearchable mystery; for what passes, in the communication of motion, out of the moving body into the one set in motion by it?”E 44

Aber wie eigentlich diese Mittheilung der Bewegung (communicatio motus) geschieht, das bleibt wohl im Grunde für uns, die wir die Körper ihrer innern Beschaffenheit nach nicht kennen, ein unerforschliches Geheimniß; denn was geht bey der Mittheilung der Bewegung aus dem bewegten Körper in den durch ihn in Bewegung gesetzten über?

The expression can always be justified, he adds, in the sense that the one body acts on the other with a force depending on the size of its own motion.E 44 Biwald answers that bodies never touch:

“There is therefore no mathematical contact of bodies, or contact strictly so called, which there would be when two bodies are so adjacent that nothing not only is placed between them, but nothing could be. There will, however, be physical or apparent contact, consisting in two bodies being so near that it seems nothing could be placed between them, though in fact something may come between.”B 196

Nullus igitur est corporum contactus mathematicus, seu stricte talis, qui tum habetur, cum duo corpora ita contigua sunt, ut inter ea non modo nihil interponatur, sed ne quidem interponi possit. Erit tamen contactus physicus, sive apparens, consistens in eo, quod duo corpora ita sint propinqua, ut inter ea nihil interponi posse videatur, quamvis reipsa intercedere quidpiam possit.

A repulsive force that grows without limit as the distance shrinks stops the bodies short of contact, and it is this force that takes away the speed of the striking body and gives speed to the struck one, degree by degree.B 196–197 Ferguson does not take the question up.

The measure of force

Biwald sets out the quarrel. From the beginning of the world down to Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716), the quantity of motion was reckoned as mass times velocity, whether the velocity was actual or only initial, as in a body pressing on a table. Leibniz and those who followed him, some of the Dutch, Italians and Germans, kept that measure for pressures, which they called dead forces, but for actual motion multiplied the mass by the square of the velocity and called these living forces. The English and French fought hard for the old measure.B 112 He ends:

“Several tried to reduce the matter to a question of a name; and what makes us agree with them is the very great number of demonstrations that each side produces for the truth of its own opinion.”B 112–113

complures rem ad quaestionem de nomine reducere conabantur, quibus ut assentiamur, facit demonstrationum numerus bene magnus, quas utraque pars pro sententiae suae veritate asserenda producit.

Nollet reports that the greater number, revolted by the new doctrine, regarded it as a paradox, and that most thought it better to reconcile the phenomena Leibniz cited with known principles than to admit a novelty not tied to clear ideas of motion. He names the two best statements of the case, the last chapter of the Institutions de physique (1740) of Émilie du Châtelet (1706–1749) for living forces, and the dissertation of Jean-Jacques Dortous de Mairan (1678–1771) against them, “by very strong reasons”.N 201–202 Erxleben passes over “the Leibnizian distinction between living and dead forces” and “the famous dispute over the measure of forces” as too long for an elementary course.E 67–68 Ferguson gives the old measure as the foundation of the science, “Upon this easy principle depends the whole of mechanics”, and measures by it the very thing the quarrel was about, the force with which a moving body strikes an obstacle; he does not mention the dispute.F 47–48

Why they differed

What a body is

Biwald says outright that the question of inertia is not about a word. In the preface to his second edition he writes that he has kept his view unchanged, that friends well practised in these matters have written to agree with him, and that the objections of others have not moved him:

“For I noticed at once that these authors, having undertaken to expound Boscovich’s theory, had so sworn by the words of this author that they thought they should not depart from him by a nail’s breadth. […] Nor is this a quarrel about a word; it goes straight to the heart of the opinion we hold about the essence of body.”B pref.

Illud enim illico adverti, Authores hos, cum Boscovichii theoriam exponendam sibi sumpsissent, ita in Authoris huius verba iurasse, ut ne latum quidem unguem ab eo recedendum esse existimarent. […] Neque de verbo ea lis versatur, sed eam, quam de corporis essentia tenemus, sententiam plane ingreditur.

His bodies are made of points endowed with a motive force, a perpetual striving to change place; a second, opposite striving to keep place cannot sit in the same substance.B 279B 172–173 He parts here from Boscovich himself, who, after showing that inertia could be proved neither from first principles nor from the phenomena, still gave it to his points.B 279 Nollet does not reason from the essence of body at all. His evidence is the behaviour of hanging balls: the ball at rest takes motion from the one that strikes it, and a heavier ball is carried less far, which he reads as a real resistance in the body struck.N 180–181

Whether sufficient reason proves anything

Erxleben grounds inertia on the principle of sufficient reason.E 46 Biwald, following Boscovich, rejects exactly that argument when Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) uses it. Euler said that a single body in an immense void must stay at rest or move uniformly straight on, since there is no reason for it to go one way rather than another. Biwald answers that we do not know the inner natures of things; that by the same reasoning the body could be made to circle any point in space, since there is no reason for it to approach that point rather than recede; and that, reasoning purely a priori, accelerated motion along some curve is infinitely more probable than rest or uniform straight motion, because there are infinitely more such states.B 275–277

Ferguson argues from experience instead. That matter cannot set itself moving is “allowed by all men”; that it has no propensity to fall from motion into rest he shows by taking away, in thought, what stops it. A bowl runs only a short way on a bowling-green because of friction; on a green perfectly level and covered with polished glass it would go much farther; with the air taken away as well, “if the green were extended quite around the earth, the bowl would go on, round and round the earth, for ever.”F 2–3

The law of continuity

Biwald takes the impossibility of contact from Boscovich’s law of continuity, by which every change of state passes through all the intermediate states. If a ball moving with six degrees of speed struck an equal ball at rest, and they touched, its speed would jump from six to three without passing through five and four, and the other ball would gain three degrees without first having one and two.B 197 Erxleben knows the argument and doubts its use:

“In hard bodies, on the other hand, the passage of motion into rest happens suddenly: for this reason some have concluded from the law of continuity that there can be no perfectly hard bodies. Experience indeed teaches this, but it is still the question whether the law of continuity is rightly applied here.”E 60

Bey harten Körpern hingegen geschieht der Uebergang der Bewegung in Ruhe plötzlich: deswegen haben einige aus dem Gesetze der Stetigkeit gefolgert, es könne keine völlig harten Körper geben. Dies lehrt nun zwar die Erfahrung, aber es ist noch die Frage, ob das Gesetz der Stetigkeit hier recht angebracht wird?

Nollet reaches the same practical conclusion, that there is no perfectly hard matter, from the dent left by every blow.N 289

Agreement on the effects

Nollet gives the reason the measure of force mattered less than it seemed. Both sides agree that a body with two degrees of speed produces four times the effect of one with one degree; they differ only on why:

“And it must be admitted that if one brings the consideration of time into the examination of the facts offered as proofs of living forces, one finds oneself back on the ordinary road. […] It follows from this admission and its restriction that if the affair of living forces is not a question of a name, at least one can say it is not of so great consequence as it seemed it must be for mechanics.”N 203

Et il faut avouer que si l’on fait entrer la considération du tems dans l’examen des faits qu’on apporte en preuves des forces vives, on se retrouve alors dans la route ordinaire […] Il suit de cet aveu & de sa restriction, que si l’affaire des forces vives n’est point une question de nom, au moins on peut dire qu’elle n’est pas d’une aussi grande conséquence qu’elle paroissoit devoir l’être pour la méchanique

In practice, he concludes, one may without error estimate the force of bodies by their mass and simple velocity.N 203–204

Questions the texts left open

  • Whether anything is ever at rest. Nollet defines rest as a body keeping the same relations to everything near and far, then grants that if the earth turns on its axis and goes round the sun, as is very probable, no body on it is at rest except relative to other earthly things.N 204–205 Biwald holds that absolute rest exists nowhere in nature: forces depending on distance, internal motions, effluvia and heat keep every part of every body moving.B 277–278
  • Whether inertia is weight. Erxleben names Father Andreas Gordon OSB (1712–1751), professor at Erfurt, as one who made them the same thing. Nollet meets the same objection, calls it specious, and answers it: weight acts only downward, the resistance in every direction, and a long thread takes away the weight of a hanging ball but not its resistance.N 179, 182–183 Erxleben rejects Gordon’s view, and also that of Euler, who seems to him to make inertia the same as impenetrability.E 47
  • Whether inertia can itself give a body motion. Some natural philosophers, Erxleben reports, say so and offer experiments; if they were right, his account of inertia would be false. He promises to show in his lectures that the experiments prove nothing of the kind.E 47–48
