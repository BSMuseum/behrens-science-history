Snapshot c. 1775 · Natural philosophy

Meteors

What teaching texts from Graz, Göttingen, Paris and London said between 1765 and 1776 about the things that happen in the air: dew and fog, clouds, rain, snow and hail, the winds, the rainbow, lightning, will-o’-the-wisps, falling stars and the northern light. They agree that meteors are made of what rises from the earth. They differ on what lightning is: three teach that it is electricity, while the British course, unchanged on this since 1760, still makes it a fermentation of sulphureous and nitrous effluvia. They differ too on what raises the vapours, on whether dew falls, on what makes hail and waterspouts, on whether lightning strikes upward too, on whether iron rods protect a house, and on what the northern light is.

First cut, from one text per region. Nollet has no lesson of his own on meteors; he treats them as applications in his lessons on air, fire and light, and thunder again in his lesson on electricity, and those passages are the Paris text. Springs, the sea and earthquakes are left to the entry on the terraqueous globe. Lightning as electricity is also in the entry on electricity. The British text is Ferguson’s Lectures on Select Subjects (London, 1776); the Britannica, the British text until 22 November 2026, is kept as a supplementary text.

Why these texts: the project chooses, for each topic, the most-reprinted course in each region that treats the topic in a section of its own. Nollet’s Leçons have no lesson on meteors, but they treat them at length, under the period’s three classes, in passages of their own within three lessons, so Nollet stays the Paris text here. François Para du Phanjas (1724–1797), whose course was the Paris text for the entry on earth, gives meteors a treatise of their own (vol. 4, §784 onward); his is the first Paris text to add when this entry is widened. In Britain, Ferguson has no lecture on meteors either, but his margin headings on the winds and on thunder and lightning make him the British text by the same reading. The Philosophical Grammar of Benjamin Martin (1705–1782), with a chapter on the winds and a “Meteorography” of vapours, clouds, rain, hail, snow, thunder and fiery meteors, and the Compendious System of Natural Philosophy of John Rowning (c. 1701–1771), with dissertations on the winds, on rain, snow and hail, and on thunder and the northern light, are the British texts to add when it is widened.

Where the subject sat among the sciences

In Graz meteors are the fifth part of particular physics, between the part on earth and the part on the terraqueous globe. Biwald begins from the atmosphere, which holds particles of every kind, and from their mixture and action on one another come the operations which his general physics called the chemical properties of bodies: solutions, precipitations, effervescences and coagulations, and from these the bodies that float in the air or fall from it.B 363–364 He lists what rises from each of the three kingdoms: the scents, waters, oils and salts of plants, the perspiration of animals and the vapours of their bodies, the vapours of fresh water and the ocean, sulphur from volcanoes and naphtha from coal pits. Among his instances are the fires that burn without end on the Absheron peninsula near Baku, and a mine of quicksilver at Idrija where vapours, lit by a candle, blew down the miners and wrecked a hut at the entrance.B 364–365 The winds are not in this part. Biwald treats them with the air, in Part II, and there promises particulars “in the fifth part, when we deal with meteors”.B 95 In Part V he breaks off his account of rain for want of room:

“since I am already nearly reaching the limits prescribed for the work”B 374

quod praescriptos operi limites iam prope attingam

No article on winds follows. In Göttingen the atmosphere and its meteors are part of the thirteenth section, on the earth in particular, between the earth’s surface and its mountains. Erxleben opens with the height of the atmosphere and then takes the winds first among the meteors.E 545–551

In Paris meteors have no lesson. Nollet divides the matter that rises from the earth into vapours, which are watery, and exhalations, which are saline, sulphurous, fatty and spirituous, and he divides meteors accordingly: watery meteors, such as fog, clouds, rain and hail; meteors of exhalations that take fire, such as thunder, lightning and will-o’-the-wisps; and luminous meteors, such as the rainbow and mock suns. He treats the watery ones in the lesson on air, “so as not to make too long a digression”, and leaves the rest to the lessons on fire and on light.N iii 370–371 Thunder appears a fourth time, in the lesson on electricity.N vi 234

In London meteors have no lecture. Ferguson treats the winds and then thunder and lightning in his lecture on pneumatics, as consequences of two properties of the air: it swells with heat, “and of this property, wind is a necessary consequence”, and it is the receptacle of every effluvium that rises from bodies.F 173F 176 The rainbow comes at the end of the lecture on optics, after the prism.F 246 He has no classes of meteors, and nothing on dew, clouds, snow, hail, waterspouts, fiery meteors or the northern light (machine-read text of the whole book searched).

The Britannica, a supplementary text here, keeps the old definition in its dictionary entry: a meteor is an imperfect, changeable and mixed body appearing in the atmosphere, formed by the action of the heavenly bodies out of the common elements.EB iii 203 Then:

“Meteors are of three kinds; fiery, airy, and watery.”EB iii 203

What it says about them in substance is spread over short entries and three treatises: winds in “Pneumatics”, thunder-clouds in “Electricity”, the rainbow in “Optics”.EB iii 494–495EB ii 480–484EB iii 435

What the texts taught in common

  • Meteors are made of what rises from the earth. Biwald lists the particles carried up from plants, animals and minerals.B 364–365 Erxleben: the air is never pure but full of foreign particles, and vapours of many kinds gather in it, driven up by warmth, putrefaction, fermentation and the wind.E 551–553 Nollet derives each class of meteor from vapours or exhalations.N iii 370 The Britannica, a supplementary text here: fiery meteors are a fat sulphurous smoke set on fire, airy meteors flatulent and spirituous exhalations, watery meteors vapours modified by heat and cold.EB iii 203 Ferguson: the atmosphere is “the common receptacle of all the effluvia or vapours arising from different bodies”, of smoke, of fogs from damp places, and of effluvia from sulphureous, nitrous, acid and alkaline bodies; whatever is volatile rises in it “according to its specific gravity”.F 176 Erxleben and Ferguson do not sort meteors into classes.
  • A cloud is a fog high in the air. Biwald calls it beyond all doubt, and adds his own observation in the mountains of Upper Styria, where he went to study plants: those which, standing at the foot of the mountains, “I took for the densest clouds, I found, when I had climbed to the summits, to be only fog”.B 368–369 Erxleben gives the same test: climb a mountain covered by clouds and you find yourself in a fog.E 558 Nollet: travellers in the Alps and the Pyrenees pass through clouds.N iii 382 Ferguson names the clouds only as part of the atmosphere.F 168 The Britannica, a supplementary text here, makes dew, mist and rain stages of the same ascending vapour, according as it is checked lower or higher.EB iii 525–526
  • Rain is the vapour of clouds gathered into drops too heavy to float. Biwald, Erxleben, Nollet and the Britannica agree on this.B 370E 559N iii 383EB iii 525 On what gathers them they differ in emphasis: winds pressing the clouds together (Biwald, Nollet, the Britannica), cold (Nollet, the Britannica), the electric fluid passing from cloud to cloud (Biwald).B 370–371N iii 383EB iii 525–526 The Britannica reports further opinions, of William Derham (1657–1735), Jacques Rohault (1618–1672) and Samuel Clarke (1675–1729), without choosing among them.EB iii 525–526 Ferguson has no account of drops. His rain falls when the air grows too light to carry the vapours, which is why the barometer falls before rain or snow: then “the air is too thin and light to bear up the vapours”, and before fair weather it is too dense and heavy to let them fall.F 189
  • Snow is frozen vapour, and its flakes are often six-rayed stars. Biwald: the rays usually join at angles of 60 degrees, and the snows of the year he wrote were exact hexagonal stars.B 374–375 Erxleben: the little stars are built of rays of ice meeting mostly at 60, sometimes at 30 and 120 degrees, and Johannes Kepler (1571–1630) is said to have been the first to observe them.E 561–562 Nollet says snow forms when the cold seizes the vapours before they have joined into drops, and does not describe the figures.N iii 384 The Britannica has no entry on snow, and Ferguson names it only with rain, at the barometer.F 189
  • Rains of blood, sulphur and frogs are natural events. Biwald: a rain of sulphur is the pollen of plants carried by the wind, a rain of blood is small insects and their excrement mixed with the drops, and frogs and toads appear after a warm rain because it hatches their eggs; much less should anyone believe it rains animals.B 383 Erxleben: the common man takes falls of earth, sand, pollen or ash for extraordinary rains, and the so-called rain of blood comes from various insects.E 552 Nollet gives the longest account: the yellow powder taken for sulphur that Johann Jakob Scheuchzer (1672–1733) examined was pine pollen, the spots of blood are red drops left by butterflies leaving the chrysalis, and the grain that seems to fall with rain is the bulbs of the lesser celandine.N iii 385–390 Ferguson and the Britannica pages read do not take it up.
  • The rainbow is sunlight refracted and reflected in the drops of rain, its colours those of the prism. Biwald and Nollet imitate it with a hollow glass globe of water hung in a sunlit room, and both trace the explanation from Marcantonio de Dominis (1560–1624), archbishop of Spalato, through Descartes to Isaac Newton (1643–1727), who applied the different refrangibility of the rays.B 377–379N v 410 The primary bow comes from one reflection and two refractions, the fainter secondary bow, with its colours reversed, from two reflections.B 378E 564–567EB iii 435 Biwald and Erxleben give the same angles, about 42° for the red and 40° for the violet.B 377E 565 Ferguson gives the same account after his lecture on colours: the rays, refracted in passing into drops of falling rain, reflected from the far side of the drops and refracted again as they leave them, make all the colours appear “in the form of a fine arch in the heavens”; the interior bow comes from two refractions and one reflection, the exterior, less vivid and with its colours inverted, from two refractions and two reflections.F 246–247
  • Will-o’-the-wisps, falling stars and fireballs are exhalations from the earth that burn or shine in the air. Biwald: fireballs are dense inflammable vapours gathered in the air, falling stars small burning globules, and will-o’-the-wisps exhalations from rotting bodies, fat soils, wood, corpses and marshes.B 386–388 Erxleben: oily and fatty vapours, which perhaps take fire or perhaps only shine, and in which electricity perhaps sometimes plays a part.E 577–578 The Britannica: a fat sulphurous smoke set on fire.EB iii 203 Nollet agrees that the matter is probably chosen among the exhalations of the earth, but he will call the whole account only conjecture (see below).N iv 297–298 Ferguson does not take up these meteors. The nearest he comes is the inflammable matter that flashes harmlessly in the upper atmosphere, and the fire-damps of mines, “much of the same kind with lightning”.F 177
  • Wind is air flowing to restore an equilibrium, and the sun’s heat is the chief cause. Biwald: whatever disturbs the equilibrium of the air, among them the action of sun and moon, heat and cold, and effervescences of vapours in the air.B 95 Erxleben derives the constant east wind of the hot zone, the land and sea breezes and probably the monsoons from the sun’s heating of the earth as it turns.E 547–549 Nollet: winds come “immediately from a defect of equilibrium in the air”, and the east winds of the torrid zone, with fair likelihood, from the daily turning of the earth, each part of the zone being rarefied in turn by the sun.N iii 493–494 Ferguson: the air swells with heat, so that air heated by the sun rises and the air around rushes in; as the sun moves from east to west with respect to the earth, the air follows it, which gives the constant east wind at the equator, inclining a little north and south on either side. The general course is changed by exhalations from earthquakes and volcanoes, by heavy rain condensing the air, by burning sands, by mountains, and by the sun’s declination, and to these he puts down the uncertain winds of Europe, the monsoons, the sea and land breezes, storms and hurricanes.F 173F 174 The Britannica, a supplementary text here: the sun is the principal and most universal cause, and the general trade winds follow from it.EB iii 494–495 Nollet alone adds, as probable, winds from exhalations fermenting in the middle region of the air, the opinion of Wilhelm Homberg (1652–1715).N iii 494–495

The disputes

What raises the vapours?

Biwald says the opinions of physicists differ widely and he has no room to discuss them. He gives two causes. First, internal motions at the surface of the earth or within it, from fires above and below ground, heat and fermentation, break bodies into very small particles which form a fluid lighter than the air, and the air drives it up. Second, the electric fluid:B 366

“Secondly, the electric fluid, which is poured round them in greater quantity, can also increase the quantity of vapours and carry them to a greater height.”B 366

Secundo, copiam vaporum augere, eosque ad maiorem altitudinem evehere quoque potest fluidum electricum, quod maiore copia iisdem circumfunditur.

It makes the light particles electric, positively or negatively, so that they repel one another and the earth’s surface. Biwald also gives the changing shapes of clouds, and the different heights and contrary motions of their layers, chiefly to the electric fluid.B 366–369 Erxleben names warmth, putrefaction, fermentation and the wind and goes no further.E 552–553 Nollet makes the matter of fire the carrier: at night the earth is warmer than the air, and the fire passing from it carries the subtlest parts of terrestrial bodies with it.N iii 371 Clouds float at the height where they are in equilibrium with the air.N iii 382 The Britannica, a supplementary text here, in its entry “Rain” reports William Derham’s vesicles, little bubbles of water full of air, and its treatise “Electricity” reports the view of Giambattista Beccaria (1716–1781) that electric matter issuing from the earth carries the watery particles up to form the thunder-clouds.EB iii 525EB ii 482 Ferguson gives no cause for the vapours’ rising beyond their lightness: whatever is volatile rises “to greater or less heights, according to its specific gravity”, and the weight of the air holds the vapours up until the air grows too light to bear them.F 176F 189 On the other disputes below he has nothing.

Does dew fall?

Nollet says it does. In the evening a damp, the serein, settles on clothes; at sunrise the vapours that rose in the night with the fire return to the earth: “The vapours that fall back in this way are called dews.” He separates this from the drops on plants:

“One must not, however, confuse this dew that falls from the air with that which is seen in the morning on plants.”N iii 373

Il ne faut pas confondre cependant cette rosée qui tombe de l’air, avec celle qu’on remarque le matin sur les plantes.

Those drops are the plant’s transpiration, as a cabbage covered for the night shows.N iii 372–373 He adds that Christian Ludwig Gersten (1701–1762), Musschenbroek and Charles François de Cisternay du Fay (1698–1739) have dispelled false ideas about dew, and reports their most surprising finding, that polished metal stays dry where glass and porcelain beside it are wet.N iii 378–379 Erxleben has no falling dew. Dew forms in the evening from the vapours that plants and other bodies give off, which thicken on the leaves as the air cools, together with vapours already in the air brought together by the cold:

“From a part of the watery vapours which plants and other bodies give off, the dew (ros) arises in the evening.”E 555

Aus einem Theile derer wässerichten Dünste, welche die Pflanzen und andere Körper von sich geben, entsteht am Abend der Thau (ros).

His reading list begins with Gersten’s dissertation against the old and common error that dew falls.E 556 Biwald takes no side. He defines dew as a thin vapour collected into drops by the night’s cold, and reports the three kinds of Pieter van Musschenbroek (1692–1761): one flowing up from the earth, one falling back from the atmosphere, one exuded from plants.B 374 The Britannica keeps the falling dew: a dense moist vapour that falls on the earth like a fine rain while the sun is below the horizon.EB ii 420

What makes hail?

Erxleben: raindrops carried into very cold regions of the air freeze into lumps of ice. That is also why it seldom hails in winter:

“In winter it does not easily hail, because the atmosphere is too cold for the water in the air to be able to run together into drops.”E 561

Im Winter hagelt es nicht leicht, weil der Lustkreis zu kalt ist, als daß das Wasser in der Luft sollte in Tropfen zusammenfliessen können.

Nollet has the same account: when the cold seizes the vapours before they join, they fall as snow, when after, as hail; large hailstones are several grains joined in falling, or grains coated with fresh layers of ice.N iii 384 Biwald reverses the order. Hail begins as snow, which grows and partly melts as it falls and so turns to ice; a nucleus like snow is sometimes found inside. Then, after Beccaria, he asks what can gather the vapours so suddenly into such masses, and answers the electric fluid:

“That hail must be ascribed to the sudden diffusion of the electric fluid appears from this, that it hails very rarely in winter, but often at the end of spring, in summer and at the start of autumn, the seasons in which the atmosphere holds a greater quantity of electric fluid.”B 376

Grandinem vero fluidi electrici repentinae diffusioni tribuendam esse vel inde patet, quod hyeme perquam raro, vere autem exeunte, aestate, autumnoque inchoante frequenter grandinet, quibus anni tempestatibus fluidi electrici maior copia atmosphaerae inest.

Thunder and lightning, he adds, often come with hail.B 375–376 The Britannica’s entry “Hail” explains the same summer hail by a third cause. Raindrops freeze when, in their passage through the lower air, “they meet with nitrous particles, which are known to contribute greatly to freezing”, and more nitre is exhaled from the earth in summer.EB ii 769 Its treatise “Electricity”, describing thunder-clouds after Beccaria, says only that when the clouds below them are agitated very violently it commonly hails.EB ii 481

What is a waterspout?

All four describe the column of water that hangs from a cloud to the sea and can overturn a ship. Erxleben and Nollet say sailors break it with cannon shot.E 560N iii 395 On its cause they divide. Nollet will not decide:

“Few observers have had leisure to examine these accidents closely, and for this reason we are not yet well informed of the manner in which they arise.”N iii 395

Peu d’observateurs ont eu le loisir d’examiner de près ces sortes d’accidens, & par cette raison, l’on n’est pas encore bien instruit de la maniere dont ils naissent.

It is believed, he goes on, “with fair likelihood”, that the cloud is set turning by two contrary winds whose directions are parallel.N iii 395 Erxleben: a whirlwind drives the parts of a cloud so close together that it turns suddenly to water, which comes down at once and is whirled round.E 560 Biwald explains it by electricity, quoting at length his fellow Jesuit Karl Scherffer (1716–1783), who follows Beccaria and Franklin’s theory: a cloud with too much or too little electric fluid draws vapours to itself until they form a canal down which the superfluous fluid pours, and the vapours rushing in raise a wind that sets the column turning.B 371–373 The Britannica holds both views in different places. Its entry “Spout” reports Abraham de la Pryme (1671–1704): a gyration of clouds by contrary winds.EB iii 623 Its treatise “Electricity”, after Beccaria, makes waterspouts and hurricanes come from the parts of clouds drawn down toward places of the earth that lack the electric fluid.EB ii 482

What is lightning?

Three of the texts make it electricity. Since the electricity of the atmosphere became known by most certain experiments, Biwald says, physicists have found so much agreement between the phenomena

“that it is no longer permitted to doubt that lightning and the thunderbolt are effects of natural electricity”B 382

ut dubitare iam non liceat, fulgur, fulmenque electricitatis naturalis effectus esse

Erxleben, after the experiments with insulated iron rods and Benjamin Franklin’s (1706–1790) paper kites, says “it can no longer be doubted today that lightning and thunder are only effects of a strong electricity”, especially since Georg Wilhelm Richmann (1711–1753) was killed at Petersburg by a spark from such a rod.E 570–571 Nollet claims the identity as his own conjecture of 1749:

“I ventured to say that thunder and lightning, which are part of that formidable meteor, were only a great electricity, like in its essence to the one we excite in our laboratories by rubbing certain substances”N vi 234

j’osai dire que le tonnerre & les éclairs qui font partie de ce formidable météore, n’étoient qu’une grande Électricité, semblable par son essence à celle que nous excitons dans nos laboratoires en frottant certaines substances

The Britannica’s treatise “Electricity” sets out Franklin’s point-by-point comparison of lightning and the electric spark, and its entry “Thunder” sends the reader there.EB ii 480–481

Ferguson makes it a fermentation. Under the margin heading “Thunder and lightning”, after a passage on fermentations, he writes:

“Whoever considers the effects of fermentation, cannot be at a loss to account for the dreadful effects of thunder and lightning: for the effluvia of sulphureous and nitrous bodies, and others that may rise into the atmosphere, will ferment with each other, and take fire very often of themselves; sometimes by the assistance of the sun’s heat.”F 176–177

Thin inflammable matter flashes harmlessly in the upper air; dense matter near the ground explodes, kills men and cattle, splits trees and rocks, and is heard as thunder. He shows the principle with spirit of nitre poured on oil of cloves, which flames and explodes. Electricity is not mentioned. The heat of lightning “appears to be quite different from that of other fires”, since it melts a sword without hurting the scabbard, and knives and forks struck by it keep a strong magnetic virtue for years.F 176F 177 Why the British course stands apart is taken up below.

Does lightning strike upward too?

Scipione Maffei (1675–1755) had held that lightning comes not from above but from the earth. Biwald makes the electrical account allow both: since electric matter can rush from a positively electric cloud into a negatively electric earth, or from the earth into the cloud,

“it is plain that thunderbolts can not only fall from the clouds to the earth, but also be struck out of the earth itself into the clouds, which has been observed and put in writing by several, chiefly by the Marchese Maffei in Italy”B 385

patet, fulmina non modo e nubibus in tellurem delabi posse, verum etiam ex ipsa tellure in nubes excuti, id, quod a compluribus, praecipue a Marchione Maffeio in Italia observatum, literisque proditum est

Erxleben allows that flashes have been seen close to the ground without storm clouds, perhaps inflamed vapours from cellars or coal pits, “but one may not on that account believe with Maffei that all lightning arises in this way”.E 576 Lightning for him is a great spark between electrified and unelectrified clouds, or between clouds and bodies on the earth.E 573 Nollet mentions Maffei in a footnote, as a learned man so sure of his opinion that he could not see how anyone held another; he will not adopt it strictly, but is glad to point out that an able man has held that lightning is not an inflamed matter falling from the cloud.N iv 310–311 The Britannica, reporting Beccaria, has the electric matter darting from the clouds at one place while it is discharged from the earth at another, the clouds serving to carry it from places of the earth that have too much to places that have too little.EB ii 481–482

Can a house be protected from lightning?

The Britannica gives Franklin’s instructions, which it prints without naming him:

“An iron rod being placed on the outside of a building, from the highest part continued down into the moist earth, in any direction strait or crooked, following the form of the roof or other parts of the building, will receive the lightning at its upper end, attracting it so as to prevent its striking any other part”EB ii 484

The rod should rise six or eight feet above the building and end in a fine gilt point, which may draw the fluid silently from the clouds and prevent a stroke altogether. Ships and powder magazines can be protected the same way, and a person in an unprotected house should sit in the middle of the room, or in a hammock hung by silk cords.EB ii 484 Biwald reports the dispute. Franklin thought pointed iron rods would make houses and ships safe; Nollet answered sharply that only a small part of the electric fluid could be drawn off that way. Against Nollet, Biwald sets Richmann’s death and the fright of three physicists at Bologna who were thrown back by a flash from their rod, and concludes:

“Wherefore it can hardly be doubted that by such iron rods the thunderbolt can be led off into harmless places.”B 386

Quamobrem dubitandum vix est, perticis eiusmodi ferreis fulmen in loca innoxia derivari posse.

Erxleben gives his own advice first. Standing on electric bodies gives no safety, still less electrifying oneself; the safest place is a room in a low building, away from high objects and from metals. Ringing bells or firing guns may disperse the storm clouds, or may draw them nearer, as any draught of air does. Of the rods he reports only that they “have also been much recommended”.E 575 Nollet’s lessons read for this entry do not take up the rods; his objections, which Biwald reports, are in his other writings.

What is the northern light?

Erxleben accepts the explanation of Jean-Jacques Dortous de Mairan (1678–1771):

“Mairan explains the origin of the northern light uncommonly well from vapours of the sun’s atmosphere (§609) which are drawn by the earth: time, place and all the circumstances of the northern light become comprehensible from it.”E 579

Mairan erklärt die Entstehung des Nordlichtes ungemein glücklich aus Dünsten der Sonnenatmosphäre (§. 609) die von der Erde angezogen werden: Zeit, Ort und alle Umstände bey dem Nordlichte werden daraus begreiflich.

Others, he notes, have explained it from fatty vapours.E 578–579 Biwald rejects Mairan and, with “a number of physicists after Franklin”, gives the northern light to the electric fluid, condensed somewhere into a dark cloud from which it bursts into other vapours as columns of fire; there is no thunder or lightning because the matter, high up in very rare air, is little condensed.B 389 He ends by quoting Musschenbroek against the electrical view: the aurora was not seen in the Low Countries from 1629 to 1716, and is seldom seen in France or Italy, where the air is no less electric, so it is better to suspend judgement.B 389–390 Nollet’s lessons read for this entry do not mention the northern light. The Britannica calls it an extraordinary meteor seen at night in the northern part of the heavens and refers the reader to “Pneumatics, Of Meteors”; a search of the machine-read text of “Pneumatics” finds no section of that name.EB i 509

Why they differed

How far electricity reaches

Biwald and the Britannica’s treatise on electricity (a supplementary text here), both following Beccaria, make the electric fluid work throughout the air: it raises vapours, shapes clouds, bursts them in sudden downpours, makes hail and waterspouts. Biwald gives his reason in the article on rain:

“That meteors in general, and rains in particular, depend in great part on the various motions of the electric fluid can hardly be called in doubt, especially if we attend to certain particular phenomena of rains.”B 371

A fluidi electrici variis motibus cum meteora universim, tum pluvias in specie magna ex parte pendere, in dubium vocari vix potest; praecipue, si ad particularia quaedam pluviarum phaenomena attendamus.

His instance is the cloudburst near mountains, which he compares with smoke gathering round an electrified chain and scattering when a spark is drawn.B 371 The Britannica’s reason, from Beccaria’s observations, is the quantity of electric matter in a storm, far more than any cloud could hold, so that it must pass continually between earth and clouds.EB ii 481–482 Erxleben keeps electricity to thunderstorms and leaves its source open: does the air rub against the clouds by help of the winds, do electric and unelectric clouds rub together, are the clouds of the sea electric as Franklin will have it, or are clouds electrified by mere warming, like the tourmaline?E 571–572 Nollet, in the lesson on electricity, says the natural electricity of the atmosphere arises “from causes hitherto unknown”, and offers, as no more than a conjecture, the rubbing of two currents of air that slide past each other.N vi 235 Ferguson gives electricity no part in any meteor, not even in thunder; in his lectures it is a kind of attraction shown by a rubbed glass tube.F 18–19

A lecture course that was not revised

Ferguson’s passage on thunder and lightning already stands, in the same words, in the first edition of his lectures (London, 1760), eight years after the experiments at Marly-la-Ville had drawn electric fire from a thundercloud; the fifth edition of 1776 reprints it unchanged (machine-read text of both). The author himself had moved on. His separate Introduction to Electricity (London, 1770), a single-topic treatise that does not fill the British place here, describes an apparatus, contrived by James Lind of Edinburgh, for testing Franklin’s method of protecting houses with metal rods that carry the lightning into the ground, and advises a man caught near a thunder-cloud to stand his gun upright and run from it (machine-read text only). So the difference between the British course and the others is not a difference of opinion argued on the page. Ferguson does not reject the electrical account; his lectures never mention it, and the passage printed in 1760 was carried through four later editions.F 176–177

The Britannica shows the same gap from the other side. Its “Pneumatics” reprints Ferguson’s passage on fermentation and thunder nearly word for word, and an editor inserted, after “the dreadful effects of thunder and lightening”, the words “(see Electricity:)”, sending the reader to a treatise that explains lightning by Franklin’s electricity.EB iii 490EB ii 480–481 The two accounts stand in the same work, joined by a cross-reference.

Two accounts of thunder in one course

The Paris text carries both the older account and the newer. Nollet’s lesson on fire gives what it calls the most likely and most received opinion, that thunder is a mixture of exhalations that ferment or are struck together as winds drive the clouds, and explodes.N iv 304–305 He lists its difficulties: how a vapour burning far off can give so bright a light for hours, why the rain from a thundering cloud makes the air colder, how an inflamed matter can strike like a solid body.N iv 305–312 He opens the subject with a general warning:

“conjectures on the cause of their inflammation; conjectures on the manner in which they work the effects we are as it were forced to attribute to them; uncertainty everywhere”N iv 298

conjectures sur la cause de leur inflammation; conjectures sur la maniére dont ils opérent les effets qu’on est comme forcé de leur attribuer; incertitude par-tout

He ends the lesson with the thought that thunder may be in nature’s hands what electricity is in ours, an idea which, “if it were well supported, would please me greatly”.N iv 315–316 A footnote added to the later printing reports the outcome: “Since the first edition of this volume, these conjectures have become almost certainties.”N iv 316 The lesson on electricity then treats the identity as proved by the experiments of 1752.N vi 234–235

Mairan’s sun and Musschenbroek’s gap

On the northern light the texts give reasons on both sides. Erxleben’s reason for Mairan is that the theory accounts for the time, the place and all the circumstances.E 579 Biwald’s reasons against are two: he can hardly bring himself to think that the sun’s atmosphere comes so near the earth, when some aurorae have been very close to it, and the matter of that atmosphere is known by no experiment, so that whether it can ferment at all is uncertain.B 389 The objection he quotes from Musschenbroek turns on the record of observation: an electrical cause should have shown the aurora in every country, and in every period.B 389–390

The dictionary and the treatises

As elsewhere, the Britannica’s short entries and its treatises, now a supplementary text, do not always agree. The entry “Meteor” keeps a definition from the action of the heavenly bodies on the common elements, which none of its treatises uses.EB iii 203 The entry “Hail” explains hail by nitre, and the entry “Spout” explains waterspouts by contrary winds, while the treatise “Electricity” brings both into Beccaria’s electrical account.EB ii 769EB iii 623EB ii 481–482

Questions the texts left open

  • What makes halos and mock suns. Biwald: a halo is light refracted and reflected in drops or watery vapours, as a candle seen through fog shows; he reports the view of Christiaan Huygens (1629–1695) that it is made by globules of ice with a core of snow.B 380–381 Erxleben: the rays are strongly refracted in the particles of vapour.E 569 Both make mock suns from ice: Biwald from frozen vapours, since cylindrical spicules of ice have often fallen after mock suns were seen, Erxleben from needles of ice hanging upright in the air.B 381E 569–570 The Britannica reports that Newton intimates halos are formed by light passing through drops of rain with two refractions and no reflection, and adds that it is not easy to see how.EB ii 769 Ferguson does not take up halos or mock suns. Among his air-pump experiments, a candle seen through the vapours that fill a receiver as it is exhausted appears with circles of various colours, faintly like those of the rainbow, but he does not connect them with halos (machine-read text, p. 199).
  • Whether falling stars leave a slime. Biwald reports Musschenbroek: where such globules fall, a tenacious, glutinous matter has often been found.B 387 Nollet finds it hard to believe Robert Fludd (1574–1637) that a glairy matter is found where will-o’-the-wisps settle; if it were so, they would be flying phosphori, not fires.N iv 299–300 Erxleben asks: do they really consist of a slimy matter, as some say who have tried to catch them? A clump of shining insects can imitate them.E 578
  • How the clouds become electric. Erxleben’s questions and Nollet’s conjecture are given above. Biwald, in his part on electricity, had derived the fluid from the earth, carried up with vapours.E 571–572N vi 235B 338–339
  • What first moves the air. Nollet: it is known in general that winds come from a defect of equilibrium, as water in a canal moves in waves when a stone is thrown in, “but who has thrown the stone, when we see the atmosphere stir? That is what we know only very imperfectly.”N iii 493 Erxleben thinks the causes of the monsoons perhaps not yet fully worked out.E 549
