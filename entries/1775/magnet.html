Snapshot c. 1775 · Natural philosophy

The magnet

What teaching texts from Graz, Göttingen, Paris and London said about the loadstone and the compass needle between 1765 and 1776. They agree on its properties and on almost nothing about its cause.

First cut, from one text per region.

Where the magnet sat among the sciences

For Biwald the magnet belongs to the part of physics that treats of earth, among the ores: it is simply an ore of iron, since the purest iron can be smelted from it as from other iron ores.B 353 Ferguson has no lecture on the magnet. In his first lecture, on the properties of matter, magnetism is the third of four kinds of attraction, with cohesion, gravitation and electricity, and a short passage headed “Magnetism” gives the loadstone’s properties “peculiar to themselves” and the law of its force.F 6F 17–18 The Britannica, now a supplementary text, files the loadstone under natural history as a very rich iron ore.EB, “Magnet” Erxleben gives “the magnetic force” a section of its own, the eleventh, between electricity and the system of the world. Nollet gives it a lesson.

What the texts taught in common

Five properties

Biwald lists them. The magnet attracts iron and is attracted by it. It attracts or repels another magnet according to how the two are turned. It passes its own properties to iron very readily. It turns itself toward the poles of the world. And the pole that looks north also dips toward the earth.B 353 Ferguson names three as the most remarkable. The loadstone attracts iron and steel only. It turns one side to the north and another to the south when hung by a thread that does not twist. And it “communicates all its properties to a piece of steel when rubbed upon it, without losing any itself.”F 18 The Britannica prints the same sentences.EB 36 Erxleben calls poles that attract one another friendly and those that repel hostile: like-named poles are hostile, unlike-named friendly.E 439

The force passes through everything

Whatever its cause, the force of the magnet acts across every kind of body placed between, solid or fluid, so long as the distance is not made too great. Biwald says the innumerable experiments of Pieter van Musschenbroek (1692–1761) put this beyond doubt.B 355 Erxleben adds that it persists in a space emptied of air.E 438–439 Ferguson uses the fact as a proof that bodies are full of pores: “the particles of heat and magnetism find an easy passage through the pores of gold”.F 15

The needle does not point true north, and its error changes

The needle deviates from north by some degrees, differently in different places, and in the same place differently over time. Observations printed in the English Transactions for 1759 show a daily movement as well, seven or eight minutes westward in the morning and as much eastward in the evening. Edmond Halley (1656–1742) drew a chart of the declination over the seas, but because it keeps changing, Biwald says, such a chart cannot be of much use.B 359E 441–442 Ferguson shows how to find the variation at sea with the globe: compare the sun’s azimuth found on the globe with the azimuth shown by the compass, and if they differ, “the compass does vary” in that place, by the difference.F 285 The dip of the needle grows as one goes north, according to reports in the memoirs of the Paris Academy for 1754. Some infer that it vanishes at the equator and reverses in the southern hemisphere. If that were established by sure observations, Biwald remarks, it would throw some light on the unknown cause.B 359–360

Magnets can be made

Biwald counts artificial magnets among the chief inventions of the century. A steel bar stroked with a magnet becomes one; bundles of such bars make magnets stronger than natural ones. Musschenbroek and Nollet worked on them first, he says, but it was the English who first gave iron and steel magnetic force without any magnet at all: in 1746 the physician Gowin Knight (1713–1772) showed the Royal Society bars that could reverse the poles of a natural loadstone in a quarter of an hour. In Vienna, Father Maximilian Hell SJ (1720–1792), director of the imperial observatory, has artificial magnets of various shapes made by a skilled craftsman, in no way inferior to those of other nations, and has printed a dissertation on their composition and use.B 356–358 Erxleben names another Englishman, Servington Savery (c. 1670–c. 1744), as apparently the real inventor.E 452

Iron also becomes magnetic of itself. A bar held upright at once acts on a needle as a magnet with two poles; iron crosses long standing on towers have been found magnetic; lightning and strong electricity sometimes do the same.N 221–222E 451–454 Ferguson finds it remarkable that knives and forks struck with lightning keep “a very strong magnetical virtue for several years after”, and has heard that lightning striking a mariner’s compass will sometimes make it stand with the north pole toward the south.F 177

One use dismissed

A magnet ground to powder has no poles and no longer draws iron. Biwald therefore calls wholly vain the opinion of those who make from powdered magnet an ointment, the weapon-salve, and believe that it can draw fragments of iron out of wounds.B 355

The dispute: what causes it

A magnetic matter that circulates

Nollet states the common view. Of natural philosophers he says:

“There is hardly one among them who does not admit, around every natural or artificial magnet, a subtle and invisible fluid that circulates from one pole to the other, and to which the name magnetic matter has been given. This supposition is altogether probable, and one can hardly refuse it on seeing the following experiment.”N 212

il n’en est presque point parmi eux qui n’admette autour de chaque aimant naturel ou artificiel, un fluide subtil & invisible, qui circule d’un pole à l’autre, & auquel on a donné le nom de matière magnétique. Cette supposition est tout-à-fait vraisemblable, & l’on ne peut guères s’y refuser quand on voit l’expérience qui suit.

The experiment is iron filings sifted onto a card laid over a magnet: they arrange themselves in curves running from pole to pole. Erxleben reports the same system and its two best-known forms. René Descartes (1596–1650) gave the magnetic matter the shape of small screws and the magnet matching passages, so that it could pass in one direction only. Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) explains it by straight passages lined with fibres that yield to matter entering one way and close against it the other, which Erxleben finds less contrived. On either account the earth itself contains a great magnet, as William Gilbert (1544–1603) already thought, and a magnetic vortex flows round it from pole to pole and steers the needle.E 443–447

Forces between particles

Biwald sets beside this the attempt of Father Roger Joseph Boscovich (1711–1787) to bring the magnet under his general law of forces. The particles of iron and of the magnet attract and repel along particular axes. Where many particles lie with their axes aligned there is a good magnet; iron is magnetized when a magnet turns the axes of its particles into line; and the needle is steered by great masses of iron and magnets under the earth’s surface, more plentiful toward the poles.B 361–362

A law without a cause

Erxleben reports a third course. In a paper read to the Göttingen Society of Sciences in 1760 and still unprinted, Tobias Mayer (1723–1762) left aside why a magnet is a magnet and sought the law of its force. Each particle acts on each particle of another magnet in proportion to its distance from the middle of its own magnet and inversely as the square of the distance between them. From these two rules Mayer computed the force between magnets, and the direction a needle takes near one, and found his calculation in complete agreement with experiment.E 456–457 Ferguson takes a similar line without saying so. Magnetism is a kind of attraction, he offers no cause, and he reports only how the force falls off. By the experiments of Richard Helsham (1683–1738) it falls as the square of the distance: a loadstone hung from a balance over iron needs four times the counterweight when the iron is brought twice as near. But “from some later experiments, made with the greatest accuracy”, it is found to fall in a ratio between the inverse square and the inverse cube.F 6F 18 The Britannica prints the passage.EB 36

Why they differed

The filings seemed to show the fluid to the eye

Those who explain the magnet by a fluid leaving its poles hold that the pattern of the filings displays the fluid’s direction and path, Biwald reports, and think its presence is all but shown to the eye. He notes how confidently Nollet asserts that no one doubts the magnetic fluid exists.B 356, 360–361 Biwald grants that several phenomena argue for some very subtle fluid. His objection is to what follows.

No system could account for the needle

Nollet himself lays out the trouble. If a stream of magnetic matter steers the needle as a river steers a hollow tube lying along it, a needle set squarely across the stream should stay there; it never does. A stream that steers iron because it enters iron easily should also steer a needle of copper or silver, as wind turns a weathercock without passing through it. And the needle does not point to the true north, by an amount that changes with time and place. Halley’s remedy, a great magnet inside the earth with its own slow rotation, he calls an ingenious thought that lacks proof.N 216–219

Erxleben presses further:

“I pass over the fact that the whole explanation may come out more contrived than nature’s way of acting: it remains to me, besides, still somewhat incomprehensible how it happens that this magnetic matter does not act at all on so many bodies and can pass through them so wholly unhindered. […] And why then is an iron wire or a magnet on a thread not swept along by the earth’s magnetic vortex? […] Why do even the hostile poles of two magnets often cling together when they are brought close to one another?”E 456

Ich geschweige, daß die ganze Erklärung gekünstelter herauskommen möchte, als die Natur zu handeln pflegt: es bleibt mir wenigstens außerdem auch noch immer in etwas unbegreiflich, wie es zugeht, daß diese magnetische Materie auf so viele Körper gar nicht wirkt, und so ganz ungehindert durch sie durchgehen kann. […] Und warum wird denn ein Eisendrat oder ein Magnet an einem Faden nicht von dem magnetischen Erdwirbel fortgerissen […]? Warum hangen öfters auch die feindlichen Pole zweener Magnete untereinander zusammen, wenn sie dicht an einander gebracht werden?

Evidence from the iron mines of Styria

Biwald follows Boscovich in the theory of physics generally. Here he accepts his forces for attraction, repulsion and the poles, and rejects his account of the needle’s direction on two grounds, the second drawn from where he lives:

“For first, those iron mines existing in greater abundance at the poles are a hypothesis resting on no observation; and then there is something else that overturns this explanation from the ground up, namely that the magnet is attracted by very few iron ores. Nowhere is it easier to try this than in the very province where I live as I write: for here there are very extensive iron mines, and a magnetic needle carried to them keeps its former position unchanged and shows the hours by which the underground surveyors judge the direction, position and angles of the galleries and take their measurements.”B 363

nam primo illae ferri fodinae ad polos maiori copia existentes hypothesis sunt nulli observationi nixa; deinde aliud quoque est, quod hanc explicationem funditus evertit, nimirum, magnetem a paucissimis ferri mineris attrahi. Experimenta huius rei non alibi pronius est capere, quam in ea ipsa Provincia, quam haec scribens incolo: in ea enim ferri fodinae amplissimae occurrunt, ad quas delata acus magnetica situm priorem haud mutatum servat, horasque indicat, quarum ope Geometrae subterranei de cuniculorum directione, situ, angulisque iudicium ferunt, mensurasque capiunt.

He adds that it can hardly be explained how such mines would act at such immense distances, and concludes with Musschenbroek that the cause of magnetic properties is still wholly unknown to natural philosophers.B 363

Different ideas of what an explanation owes

Nollet wants a mechanism and keeps the fluid in spite of its difficulties. Biwald will have neither a fluid nor forces if they need a heap of hypotheses, and prefers to say the cause is hidden:

“It must be admitted here that it still lies hidden from all natural philosophers, although not a few systems have been forged; examined with a mind free of prejudice, these will easily be seen to be entangled in insoluble difficulties.”B 360

Verum fatendum hic est, eam Physicos omnes adhucdum latere, quamvis systemata non pauca procusa fuerint, quae si animo a praeiudiciis libero ad examen vocentur, insolubilibus difficultatibus involuta esse facile apparebit.

He cites Musschenbroek for the same stance: the mind inclines almost naturally to a magnetic fluid, learned and unlearned alike run to that opinion, and yet it is better to suspend judgment and confess the cause unknown until clear and certain signs show what it is.B 361 Mayer, as Erxleben reports him, and Ferguson ask only for the rule the force obeys.

Questions the texts left open

  • The law of the force. Ferguson sets Helsham’s inverse square against later trials that find a ratio “between the reciprocal of the square and the reciprocal of the cube of the distance; approaching to the one or the other, as the magnitudes of the attracting bodies are varied”.F 18 Mayer, in Erxleben’s report, found the inverse square in complete agreement with experiment;E 456–457 Biwald says no law has been found.B 354
  • Which pole the matter enters. Euler leaves it undecided; Charles François de Cisternay du Fay (1698–1739) made the north pole the one it flows into. Erxleben asks why this should be so hard to settle if the matter exists.E 447, 456
  • How far iron is spread through nature. Because the magnet draws particles from the ashes of plants and animals and from burnt blood, some suspect that all these bodies contain iron, though Biwald says this is by no means proved.B 353–354
  • The link with electricity. Lightning and strong electricity magnetize iron and reverse the poles of magnets. The texts report it and draw no conclusion.E 452, 454 Ferguson reports the knives and the compasses, but for him lightning is burning effluvia, not electricity.F 177
