Snapshot c. 1775 · Natural philosophy

Light

What teaching texts from Graz, Göttingen, Paris and London said light is, between 1765 and 1776. Graz and London teach one answer, Göttingen a second, Paris a third.

First cut, from one text per region, on the nature of light. Colours are touched on; vision and instruments are not yet covered.

The four texts

  • BGraz · LatinLeopold Biwald SJ (1731–1805), Physica particularis (Graz, 1768). Part III, section II, “De luce”, pp. 172–313; the nature of light at pp. 217–245.
  • EGöttingen · GermanJohann Christian Polykarp Erxleben (1744–1777), Anfangsgründe der Naturlehre (Göttingen and Gotha, 1772). Eighth section, “Vom Lichte”; the nature of light at §§303–308.
  • NParis · FrenchJean-Antoine Nollet (1700–1770), Leçons de physique expérimentale, vol. 5 (Paris, 1765). Lesson XV.
  • FLondon · EnglishJames Ferguson (1710–1776), Lectures on Select Subjects in Mechanics, Hydrostatics, Pneumatics, and Optics, 5th ed. (London, 1776). The lecture on optics (headed Lecture VIII), pp. 201–202 on the nature of light and pp. 242–244 on colours; with the velocity of light in Lecture I, p. 5.

Supplementary. EB Encyclopædia Britannica, 1st ed. (Edinburgh, 1771), vol. 3, the treatise “Optics”, from p. 416. Where light sat among the sciences

Biwald treats light together with fire, in one part of his particular physics, and gives it some 140 pages: propagation, refraction, reflection, the nature of light, colours, vision, and optical instruments.B Conspectus Erxleben gives it his eighth section, after air and before warmth and cold. Ferguson’s lecture on optics opens with what light is and goes on to refraction, the eye, glasses, telescopes, the camera obscura and mirrors, and ends with the different refrangibility of rays, colours and the rainbow.F 201F 242 The Britannica treats it in the treatise “Optics”, and its short entry “Light” only points there.EB, “Light”

What the texts taught in common

  • Light moves in straight lines. Biwald calls the rectilinear propagation of light the fact that recent natural philosophers weigh most heavily.B 218–219 Ferguson builds it into the definition of a ray, and proves it: “As no object can be seen through the bore of a bended pipe, it is evident that the rays of light move in straight lines”.F 201F 202 The Britannica prints the same pages.EB iii 417
  • Light takes time to cross space. Erxleben says a certain time, though a small one, is needed; Biwald adds that the light of the sun and of the fixed stars travels at the same speed.E 247B 220 Ferguson gives a figure in his lecture on matter: “the velocity of the particles of light is demonstrated to be at least a million times greater than the velocity of a cannon-ball”.F 5 The Britannica’s reprint of that lecture leaves the passage out.
  • Ordinary light is compound. A prism splits a ray into seven simple lights that are refracted in different degrees; white light is the mixture.E 292B 220 Ferguson shows it with a prism in a dark room and a convex glass that gathers the colours into a strong white again: “As white is a composition of all colours, so black is a privation of them all, and, therefore, properly no colour.”F 242–243F 244 The Britannica, in a section of its own, calls what it had called a particle of light “a bundle of rays, which may be separated from one another”.EB iii 427
  • Light resembles sound. Even Biwald, who rejects the conclusion drawn from it, says he gladly grants the Cartesians that the analogy between light and sound is great.B 225 Ferguson does not compare them.

The dispute: what light is

Biwald tells his students that in this age only two opinions are worth remembering, that of the Cartesians and that of Isaac Newton (1642–1727).B 217–218

Particles sent out by the luminous body

On Newton’s view light is a real outflow of the substance of the shining body, sent in every direction to any distance, which affects the eye as the effluvia of a scented body affect the nose.B 218 Ferguson states it as plain fact, in the first words of his lecture:

“Light consists of an inconceivably great number of particles flowing from a luminous body in all manner of directions; and these particles are so small, as to surpass all human comprehension.”F 201

A candle fills all the space within two miles of it with light before it has lost any sensible part of its substance, and “a ray of light is a continued stream of these particles, flowing from any visible body in a straight line”.F 201 The Britannica prints the same words.EB iii 416–417 Erxleben calls this the system of emanation.E 245

A medium set in motion

On the other view nothing travels from the sun to the eye. The space between is full of a subtle matter, and the luminous body moves it. René Descartes (1596–1650) pictured hard globules lying against one another, so that a blow at one end is felt at the other.E 246 Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) gave the view its current form:

“But these difficulties can be removed if, with Euler, one conceives a most fine, fluid and also elastic matter that is spread everywhere, and on whose parts the luminous bodies, as they tremble, strike just as sounding bodies in their coarser trembling strike the coarser air. This fine matter is also called aether.”E 247

Aber diese Schwierigkeiten lassen sich heben, wenn man sich mit Euler eine höchst feine, flüßige und dabey elastische Materie gedenkt, die allerwärts ausgebreitet ist, und auf deren Theile die leuchtenden Körper, indem sie zittern, eben so schlagen, wie die schallenden Körper bey ihrem gröbern Zittern auf die gröbere Luft schlagen. Diese feine Materie nennt man auch Aether.

Biwald counts Euler among the Cartesians, as the one “who defends the Cartesian opinion with the greatest ardour”.B 220

Why they differed

Each side’s best argument was the other’s open difficulty

For Biwald the straight path of light settles the matter. He cites Pieter van Musschenbroek (1692–1761): if light were a pressure in a fluid filling the world, pressure would spread equally every way, there would be no shadows, and night would be as bright as day.B 219 Against Euler’s appeal to the likeness of light and sound he writes:

“If then observations teach us that light is propagated in a line that is straight to the senses, while all motions excited in fluids flow off to the sides, as is plain in the circles that arise on the surface of water and spread through any narrow opening, and likewise in sound entering through any chinks or little holes, it will have to be said that light cannot be placed in the pressure of any medium, and that the analogy of nature which Euler builds upon is therefore no analogy at all.”B 225

quod si ergo observationes nos doceant, lumen propagari linea ad sensum recta, motus vero omnes, in fluidis excitatos, ad latera diffluere, uti patet in circulis, quae in superficie aquae oriuntur, & per angustam quamlibet aperturam propagantur, item in sono per rimas quaslibet, aut foramella subeunte, dicendum erit, lumen in pressu medii cuiusvis reponi non posse, nullam adeo esse eam, quam Eulerus adstruit, naturae analogiam.

Erxleben finds the difficulties on the other side heavier:

“But when one considers that the sun would then have to send out an enormous quantity of rays of light without ceasing, although one does not perceive that it loses anything of its size, nor can say anything probable about where these rays remain afterwards; that this enormous quantity of rays would have to cross one another without checking one another’s motion or altering their directions; that transparent bodies would have to be bored through in straight lines in every direction if this hypothesis were to hold, which seems quite impossible: one should almost become more inclined to assume that the matter of light is present everywhere.”E 245

Allein wenn man bedenkt, daß die Sonne alsdann unaufhörlich eine ungeheure Menge Lichtstrahlen ausschicken müßte, da man doch nicht wahrnimmt, daß sie von ihrer Größe verliert, noch etwas wahrscheinliches davon sagen kann, wo diese Lichtstrahlen hernach bleiben; daß diese ungeheure Menge von Lichtstrahlen einander durchkreutzen müßten, ohne sich doch dabey in ihrer Bewegung aufzuhalten oder ihre Richtungen abzuändern; daß die durchsichtigen Körper nach allen Richtungen in geraden Linien durchbohrt seyn müßten, wenn diese Hypothese Statt finden sollte, welches doch ganz unmöglich scheint: so sollte man fast geneigter werden anzunehmen, daß die Materie des Lichtes vielmehr allerwärts vorhanden sey

Nollet presses the same points from Paris: emission obliges one to imagine the oddest accidents to prevent or repair the exhaustion of the stars.N 11

The heavens: empty or full

The two views carry different pictures of the space between the stars. Nollet says so outright, and reads Newton’s choice as forced by the rest of his system:

“It would have been everyone’s opinion, had particular interests not stood in the way. Newton himself would perhaps have adopted it, if a resisting medium in the vast extent of the heavens had seemed to him compatible with the system of attractions.”N 9

elle eût été l’opinion de tout le monde, si des intérêts particuliers n’y eussent mis empêchement. Newton lui-même l’auroit peut-être adoptée, si un milieu résistant dans la vaste étendue des cieux lui eût paru compatible avec le systême des attractions

Erxleben meets the objection that a medium would slow the planets by turning it round: the same can be said with more right against streams of emitted light.E 246 Biwald argues from the other direction. Light arrives at the same speed from the sun and from the stars, so the aether would need the same density and elasticity through all those spaces, which could only be if some immovable barrier at the edge of the world held it in, as glass holds compressed air.B 220

Which phenomena a theory was expected to cover

Biwald teaches the experiments on colours in thin plates at length, and holds it against the medium that its globules cannot explain refraction, reflection, and above all the alternate fits of easier reflection and transmission, of which the recent Cartesians and Euler himself “make no mention”.B 220 Nollet asks less of a theory at this point. He takes the view that seems to him most probable and easiest to conceive:

“If a side must be taken between these two opinions, I admit frankly that probability decides me for the first. It has its difficulties, though, which I will not hide.”N 11

S’il faut prendre un parti entre ces deux opinions, j’avoue franchement que la vraisemblance me détermine pour la première. Elle a pourtant ses difficultés que je ne dissimulerai pas

Questions the texts left open

  • What colours are. Erxleben reports two accounts. For Newton the simple lights differ in kind, perhaps in the size of their particles. For Euler a ray is a row of beats on the aether that follow one another at unequal rates, so that colours are to the eye what tones are to the ear. Erxleben says he cannot conceive the octaves of colour Euler speaks of, and notes that a colour-music invented on this idea has not found favour.E 292–294
  • What the aether is. Euler doubts that it has weight and holds it to be the cause of the weight of other bodies. He takes it as probable that it is nearly four hundred million times rarer than air and at least a thousand times more elastic.E 247–248
  • Whether light and fire are one. Biwald argues that they are the same in nature, since light gathered by a burning mirror does what the fiercest fire does.B 226 Nollet thinks it very probable that light and heat have one principle, and notes that here he departs from Descartes, who gave them two.N 15
