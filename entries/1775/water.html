Snapshot c. 1775 · Natural philosophy

Water and other fluids

What teaching texts from Graz, Göttingen, Paris and London said about water and the behaviour of liquids between 1759 and 1776. They agree on the laws of fluid pressure and on the facts of narrow tubes. They differ on whether water is a melted solid, on why it climbs a narrow tube, on whether it can be squeezed, and on whether it can turn into earth.

First cut, from one text per region. Efflux, waves, ice and vapour are not yet covered; springs and the sea belong to the entry on the terraqueous globe.

Where water sat among the sciences

Biwald opens his particular physics with water, and makes it the occasion for everything that liquids have in common. A short first section treats water itself; a long second section, the statics of fluids, treats pressure, bodies immersed, specific weights, efflux, waves and capillary tubes. Rain, ice, vapour, springs and the sea he sends to later parts of the course.B 1, 10 Erxleben has no section on water as such. Fluids appear as a kind of body under cohesion, and his sixth section treats their equilibrium, their drops, their rise in narrow tubes, floating, specific weight and solution; freezing comes under warmth, and the question whether water turns into earth under the earth itself.E 121 Nollet treats hydrostatics in his second volume and water as a body two volumes later, in one lesson divided by its three states: liquid, vapour and ice.N iv 3 Ferguson has no lecture on water. His fifth lecture, on hydrostatics and hydraulic machines, treats “the nature, gravity, pressure, and motion of fluids in general; and of weighing solids in them”, and goes on to pumps, engines and a table of specific gravities. The rise of water in narrow tubes comes in his first lecture, as an instance of the attraction of cohesion.F 101F 6 The Britannica spreads water over four places. Its one-line entry calls it “a simple fluid, and liquid body, reputed the third of the four vulgar elements” and sends the reader to chemistry and to hydrostatics.EB iii 936 Its chemistry counts water, with earth, air and fire, among the principles or elements of bodies.EB ii 66 Its “Hydrostatics” is Ferguson’s fifth lecture reprinted.EB ii 804

What the texts taught in common

  • Water is a fluid without taste, smell or colour. It is clear and transparent, rises in vapour with little heat, does not burn, and mostly puts out fire.B 1N iv 4–5EB ii 67 When it looks cloudy, coloured, or has a taste or smell, Nollet says, it is mixed with a foreign matter.N iv 5 Biwald adds that its particles are too small for any microscope and nearly spherical: water is slippery, does not hurt the eye, has no taste, and its drops are round.B 3 Ferguson does not describe the qualities of water.
  • Natural water is hardly ever pure. It dissolves salts, soaps and many earthy bodies, which is why springs run salt, mineral, medicinal or poisonous. It is cleaned by straining through sand, by precipitation, by clarifying with egg white or isinglass, by evaporation, and by repeated distillation; Biwald gives the chemical tests of Pieter van Musschenbroek (1692–1761) for the purest water.B 7–9 Nollet adds that waters from the same province, even the same place, differ sensibly in weight, and that the lightest is commonly the best.N iv 33–34 Ferguson’s table of specific gravities, without comment, puts pump water at 1.000, spring water at 0.999, distilled water at 0.993 and sea water at 1.030.F 159
  • A fluid presses up and sideways as much as down. Ferguson calls it a wonderful property of fluids “that their pressure upwards and sidewise is equal to their pressure downwards; and this is always in proportion to their perpendicular height, without any regard to their quantity”. A small tube open at both ends, plunged into a tall tube of water, fills to the level of the water round it.F 101–102 The Britannica prints the same pages.EB ii 804 The pressure on the bottom of a vessel equals the base multiplied by the height of the fluid, whatever the shape of the vessel.B 16 A solid immersed in a fluid loses as much of its weight as the fluid of equal volume weighs.B 22 Ferguson adds that “all fluids weigh just as much in their own elements as they do in open air”: a corked phial of shot, hung in water and then opened, takes in water and needs exactly the weight of that water to balance it again.F 111
  • Water rises above its level in a narrow glass tube. The heights are inversely as the diameters of the tubes; the length of the tube makes no difference; the rise happens just the same in a vessel emptied of air; and mercury stands lower in the tube instead of higher.B 48–49, 55–56E 144–145N ii 409–410 Ferguson gives no law of heights. The water rises until the weight of the column balances the attraction of the tube, and the same in an exhausted receiver; attraction raises it only where the bore is very small, never in a tube as wide as that of a common barometer.F 6F 102 Biwald, from Musschenbroek, adds that the height depends on the kind of glass as well as the bore, which is why different natural philosophers had reported results so much at odds.B 50 Three of the texts also apply the facts beyond the laboratory: sap rises in trees, sponges and lumps of sugar draw up liquids, and a heap of sand or an upright log is wet to the top.F 6EB iii 33B 67–68N ii 435

The disputes

Is water a melted solid?

Biwald puts the question as it was commonly put:

“Some are accustomed to ask here whether the fluid state of water is wholly natural to it, or whether, considered in its natural state, it would rather be a solid body, made fluid by a certain portion of fire that is never absent from it.”B 3

Quaeri hic solet a nonnullis, an status fluiditatis aquae sit omnino connaturalis, an vero ea in statu naturali considerata corpus potius solidum foret, quod a certa ignis portione, quae eidem nunquam non admista est, fluidum efficiatur.

Nollet answers yes, and names his authorities, Edme Mariotte (c. 1620–1684), Jean-Jacques Dortous de Mairan (1678–1771) and Herman Boerhaave (1668–1738):

“Speaking exactly, the natural state of water, the one it would have if nothing were mixed with its own matter, would be to be a solid body, as Messrs Mariotte, de Mairan and Boerhaave have very well remarked. Yes, water, like fat, wax, and all the other matters that we see flow only when they are heated to a certain degree, would be ice continually, if the matter of fire, which commonly penetrates it in sufficient quantity in temperate climates, did not keep up the mobility of its parts relative to one another, to make it fluid.”N iv 3–4

À parler exactement, l’état naturel de l’eau, celui qu’elle auroit, si rien ne se mêloit à sa matière propre, seroit d’être un corps solide, comme l’ont fort bien remarqué MM. Mariotte, de Mairan, & Boerhaave. Oui, l’eau, comme la graisse, la cire, & toutes les autres matières que nous ne voyons couler, que quand on les chauffe à un certain degré, seroit continuellement glace, si la matière du feu qui la pénètre pour l’ordinaire en suffisante quantité dans les climats tempérés, n’entretenoit la mobilité respective de ses parties, pour la rendre fluide

Ferguson does not take up the question. The Britannica’s chemistry, which is its own and not Ferguson’s, agrees in one line: water becomes solid at a certain degree of cold, and “Solidity therefore seems to be its most natural state.”EB ii 67

Biwald reports that view and sides with Musschenbroek against it:

“But the celebrated Musschenbroek judges that the fluidity of water is not to be attributed to the action of fire alone. […] If we turn our minds to the principles established in the general physics, we readily understand that besides the action of fire something more is required for water to be able to be fluid.”B 4

Cl. vero Musschenbroek […] existimat fluiditatem aquae haud soli ignis actioni tribuendam esse […] Quod si ad principia in generali Physica […] stabilita animum advertamus, facile intelligimus, praeter actionem ignis alia insuper requiri, ut fluida esse aqua possit.

Musschenbroek’s reason, as Biwald gives it, is that fats and wax melted by fire grow thinner as they are heated, so water ought likewise to be resolved into finer particles; yet no chemist has managed this by repeated distillation.B 4 The principles Biwald means are those of Father Roger Joseph Boscovich (1711–1787), whose theory of forces he adopts. Fluidity belongs to bodies whose particles attract one another, as in water and mercury, and it arises from their shape and the forces between them:

“Fluidity, chiefly in the third kind of fluids, arises from their consisting of particles that come close to a spherical figure, or of such particles as have their points so arranged on concentric spherical surfaces that they are distributed equally and in equal number at equal distances from the centre.”B gen. 253

Fluiditas in tertio praecipue fluidorum genere inde enascitur, quod constent particulis ad figuram sphaericam proxime accedentibus, sive eiusmodi, quarum puncta ita in superficies sphaericas concentricas disponuntur, ut in aequalibus a centro distantiis aequaliter, numeroque pari distributa sint.

Such particles can roll round one another, their attractions and repulsions balancing, so that only a few bonds need be overcome at a time.B gen. 253–254 Erxleben holds a version of the general rule without speaking of water’s natural state. The cause of fluidity seems to him to lie “not so much in the nature of the parts themselves as in the manner of their composition”; heat expands a body until its particles no longer hang together, and when the heat is withdrawn they attract one another again and the body freezes.E 121–122E 368

Why water climbs a narrow tube

Biwald teaches attraction as the fundamental proposition of the subject:

“Fluids rise in capillary tubes when their parts are attracted less by one another than by the walls of the tube.”B 56

Fluida in tubulos capillares assurgunt, cum partes eorum minus a se invicem, quam a tubuli parietibus attrahuntur.

He credits the full theory to Josias Weitbrecht (1702–1747) in the Saint Petersburg Commentarii, as set out by Pierre Sigorgne (1719–1809) in his Institutions newtoniennes.B 66–67 Erxleben calls the fact a proof of attraction itself:

“The experience that water rises in capillary tubes is a powerful proof of the attractive force of matter; the phenomenon cannot possibly be explained from a pressure of the air or of the aether, or from a merely cohering, not attracting, force of matter.”E 145

Die Erfahrung, daß das Wasser in den Haarröhrchen aufsteigt, ist ein kräftiger Beweis der anziehenden Kraft der Materie; aus einem Drucke der Luft oder des Aethers, oder aus einer bloß zusammenhangenden nicht anziehenden Kraft der Materie läßt sich die Erscheinung durchaus nicht erklären.

Ferguson gives the rise as his first instance of the attraction of cohesion. Attraction, a property of matter that “seems rather to be infused than inherent”, is of four kinds, cohesion, gravitation, magnetism and electricity. The water rises by “the attraction of a ring of particles of the glass all round in the tube, immediately above those to which the water at any instant rises”, until the weight of the column balances it. “This can be no ways owing to the pressure of the air upon the water in the bason”: the tube is open at the top and full of air, which presses on the water in it as much as on any column in the bason, and in an exhausted receiver there is no difference.F 6 The Britannica prints the same page.EB iii 33

Nollet reviews three classes of explanation: the unequal pressure of a surrounding fluid, the adherence of the water to the glass, and the attraction of the glass. He finds fault with all three and ends without a decision:

“From all this it follows that these phenomena either are not yet well explained, or that the explanations given depend on hypotheses that are not generally received. Perhaps this comes from people having insisted on giving them one sole and single cause. […] The unequal pressure of some fluid is probably the fundamental point of the explanation; but the adherence or natural viscosity of liquids, the size and shape of their parts, and perhaps a certain motion proper to them, &c., are so many means that nature may employ for effects of this kind.”N ii 433–434

De tout ceci il résulte que ces phénomènes, ou ne sont point encore bien expliqués, ou que les explications qu’on donne, tiennent à des hypothèses qui ne sont pas généralement reçues. Peut-être cela vient-il de ce qu’on s’est obstiné à ne leur donner qu’une seule & unique cause […] La pression inégale de quelque fluide est probablement le point fondamental de l’explication; mais l’adhérence ou la viscosité naturelle des liqueurs, la grandeur & la figure de leurs parties, & peut-être un certain mouvement qui leur est propre, &c. sont autant de moyens que la nature peut employer pour ces sortes d’effets

Whether the parts of a fluid are always in motion

Biwald and Erxleben both report an opinion that the particles of a fluid are kept in perpetual internal motion, and that this motion belongs to the nature of fluidity. Both reject it. Biwald gives Musschenbroek’s reasons. Fluids shut up in metal spheres and pressed with the greatest violence stay fluid, though no such motion could go on there; powders and sand mixed into a fluid float only while it is stirred and sink when it comes to rest; and no lens or microscope shows the motion in a fluid left standing.B gen. 256–257 To the argument that fluids mix of themselves, he replies that they mix far too slowly for that.B gen. 257 Erxleben says the opinion does not follow from fluids changing their shape, mixing, or dissolving solids, nor from their needing warmth, and that “not a single observation shows such an internal motion”.E 122 Neither Nollet nor Ferguson takes up the question in the passages read, and neither does the Britannica. Nollet comes nearest when he lists “perhaps a certain motion proper to them” among the means nature may use in capillary effects.N ii 433–434

Whether water can be squeezed into less space

Biwald and Ferguson say it cannot. Ferguson lays it down, defining a fluid at the head of his lecture on hydrostatics, that “no fluid, except air or steam, can be pressed into a less space than it naturally possesses”, and takes this as the proof that the particles of fluids are hard.F 101 His lecture on pneumatics makes it the first way in which air differs from all other fluids: it can be compressed into a much less space than it naturally possesses, “which no other fluid can”.F 168 The Britannica prints the first passage.EB ii 804 Biwald rests on the Florentine experiment, in which water forced in a metal sphere “sweated through the pores of the metals like dew”, and takes it to show how strongly the particles of water repel one another at the smallest distances.B 4 Erxleben says the opposite: fluids can be compressed, and “of water this is at least now proven”.E 124 Nollet touches the point only in passing, saying that liquids do not “let themselves be compressed in the manner of solids”.N iv 383

Whether water can be turned into earth

Water distilled again and again leaves a little earth behind, and the texts disagree about where it comes from. Nollet, following Boerhaave, holds that it is a foreign matter and that water is not reducible to earth.N iv 31–33 Biwald reports Boerhaave’s denial and then sets against it five grounds for the change, which he calls not light.B 9 Erxleben says the transformation “still remains doubtful”.E 643 Ferguson does not raise it, and it was not found in the parts of the Britannica read.

Why they differed

Whether attraction counted as an explanation

For Biwald and Erxleben attraction between particles is the physics; for Nollet it is at best a name. If the glass is said to attract the water, he asks, on what is the supposition founded, and what rule does it follow?

“For if there were no other proof of it than the fact in question, and if one made of this cause an abstract quality subject to no measure, it would much resemble the sympathies and occult qualities of the Peripatetics, so justly and so generally banished from modern physics, that is to say, from reasonable physics.”N ii 415

Car si l’on n’en avoit d’autres preuves que le fait en question, & si l’on faisoit de cette cause une qualité abstraite qui ne fut asservie à aucune mesure, cela ressembleroit beaucoup aux sympathies & aux qualités occultes des Péripatéticiens, si justement & si généralement bannies de la Physique moderne, c’est-à-dire, de la Physique raisonnable.

He accepts attraction as a fact still awaiting its mechanical cause, as Isaac Newton (1642–1727) himself treated it, and rejects it as an innate force put into bodies by the Creator.N ii 415–418 The same commitment governs his account of fluidity. Solidity and fluidity, he says, depend on one cause, a subtle fluid:

“It is the subtle air that fixes the parts of a matter, when its pressure from outside exceeds the reaction it makes within; and it is this same fluid that renders and keeps the parts mobile, by introducing itself between them in sufficient quantity.”N ii 471

c’est l’air subtil qui fixe les parties d’une matière, lorsque sa pression extérieure excède la réaction qu’il fait en-dedans; & c’est ce même fluide qui rend & entretient les parties mobiles, en s’introduisant entre elles en suffisante quantité.

Nollet’s parts of water are mobile only while this fluid lies between them, so water left to itself is solid. Biwald’s particles are fluid by their own figure and forces, and need nothing added. The division does not simply follow what each text thinks of attraction. The Britannica teaches attraction in the pages it takes from Ferguson, and its own chemistry still calls solidity the most natural state of water, on the plain ground that cold makes it solid.EB ii 67

The same experiments sent them different ways

Everyone accepted that capillary rise goes on undiminished in a vessel emptied of air. Nollet and Ferguson took it as the end of explanations by the pressure of ordinary air.N ii 409F 6 Nollet then looked to a finer fluid that passes through glass, and found that James Jurin (1684–1750), though committed to attraction, had been driven to the same resort; he also reports the calculation of Alexis Clairaut (1713–1765), which agreed with experiment but placed the effective attraction in the lower part of the tube rather than the ring above the water.N ii 431–433 Biwald closes that road from his side. A subtler aether that passes freely through the most solid bodies cannot press harder outside a tube than inside it. He disposes of the other rivals in turn: air, because lighter fluids would then rise higher, and Georg Bernhard Bilfinger (1693–1750) showed that air passes freely through a capillary tube; the electric fluid, because Jean Jallabert (1712–1768), John Ellicott (1706–1772) and Musschenbroek electrified tubes strongly and the water stood no higher.B 67 And he names a French opinion of an earlier day:

“It once seemed to the French that the suspension of fluids in capillary tubes comes from adhesion to the sides of the tubes; for they held that the snake-shaped particles of fluids fix themselves in the roughnesses and projections of the glass.”B 67

Gallis quondam visum est, suspensionem fluidorum in tubulis capillaribus ab adhaesione ad latera tubulorum provenire; censebant enim particulas fluidorum anguiformes, asperitatibus, & prominentiis vitri infigi.

That, he says, might account for the water hanging in the tube but not for its rising; a drop running down the outside of a tube is drawn in at the bottom and rises to the height fitting the bore.B 67 Nollet had used the same drop against the explanations by pressure.N ii 411–412

How much weight an old experiment could bear

The Florentine academicians had filled metal spheres with water, sealed them and squeezed them in presses, and the water came through the metal. Biwald takes this as the evidence that water’s particles strongly repel one another at the smallest distances:

“Water particles repel one another most strongly at very small distances. At somewhat greater ones they attract one another, and at greater distances still they strive to draw apart again.”B 4

Particulae aqueae in distantiis admodum exiguis fortissime se repellunt. In paulo maioribus sese attrahunt, rursumque auctis distantiis a se invicem recedere nituntur.

Erxleben, citing Samuel Christian Hollmann (1696–1787) on the fallacies of the Florentine experiment and John Canton (1718–1772) in the Philosophical Transactions, judges it worthless:

“Of water this is at least now proven; the famous Florentine experiment, by which it was sought to show the contrary, is really of such a kind that it can decide nothing, because no exact measurements take place in it.”E 124

Vom Wasser ist dieses wenigstens ietzo erwiesen; das berühmte Florentiner Experiment, wodurch man das Gegentheil hat darthun wollen, ist wirklich von der Art, daß es nichts entscheiden kann, weil keine genauen Ausmessungen dabey Statt finden.

What counted as a foreign matter

Water distilled to dryness, however often, always leaves a little earth at the bottom of the still. Robert Boyle (1627–1691) and Robert Hooke (1635–1703) observed it, and Newton concluded that water changes into a solid earth by repeated distillation.N iv 31 Boerhaave held instead that the parts of water are unalterable elements and that the earth came from the dust of the laboratory air or from careless handling. Nollet follows him:

“I would rather believe, after the examination Mr Boerhaave made of it, that it is a foreign matter mixed with the water, than think, on a proof so slight and so doubtful, that water is reducible to earth.”N iv 32–33

j’aimerois mieux croire, après l’examen qu’en a fait M. Boerhave, que c’est une matière étrangère mêlée à l’eau, que de penser sur une preuve aussi légère & aussi douteuse, que l’eau soit réductible en terre.

Biwald gives Boerhaave’s answer and then the other side, which he does not call slight: “Affirmant alii, non levibus fundamentis nixi”, others affirm it, resting on grounds that are not light. Slaked lime set with sand and water weighs more when dry than the lime and sand did; burnt gypsum mixed with water gains weight; plants fed on water alone grow solid roots and stems, and a willow branch planted by Boyle weighed 169 pounds after five years while its earth lost scarcely two ounces; Johann Theodor Eller (1689–1760) found green films and an inflammable oil in distilled water sealed in glass and left in the sun; Andreas Sigismund Marggraf (1709–1782) distilled the same water forty times and saw it grow more and more turbid and lay down earth on the glass. Biwald describes that earth closely, as white, shining and very light.B 9 Erxleben meets the question in a different setting, the Swedish debate over whether the waters of the earth are diminishing. Carl Linnaeus (1707–1778) believes they are, turning into earth, and pictures the habitable land growing from a single mountain as the waters dried. Erxleben thinks the question cannot yet be settled, and adds:

“And the transformation of water into earth, which some have sought to demonstrate by experiments that seemed evident, still remains doubtful.”E 643

Und die Verwandlung des Wassers in Erde, die einige durch augenscheinliche Versuche haben darthun wollen, bleibt noch immer zweifelhaft.

Questions the texts left open

  • Whether hot water is more fluid than cold. Boerhaave said no: Newton found a pendulum swung as freely in the coldest water as in the hottest. Nollet objects that the bob swells in hot water and meets more resistance, which could hide a real difference, and asks why hot water then penetrates bodies, dissolves salts and cooks meat better than cold.N iv 6–9 Biwald, reporting the dispute, says Nollet’s objections could easily make anyone wish to see the opinion confirmed by other experiments.B 4
  • The shape of the particles of water. Biwald argues that they are nearly spherical.B 3 Ferguson says the particles of a fluid are too small for the best microscopes, hard, since no fluid but air or steam can be compressed, and “round and smooth, seeming they are so easily moved among one another”.F 101 The Britannica prints the passage with “since” where the 1776 edition has “seeming”.EB ii 804 Nollet will not decide:

“I take care not to decide whether they are little spindles, little cylinders, or globules, because I know of no observation or experiment that can warrant the decision.”N iv 5

je n’ai garde de décider si ce sont des petits fuseaux, des petits cylindres, ou des globules, parce que je ne connois aucune observation, ni aucune expérience, qui puisse garantir cette décision

  • The degrees of fluidity. No body is perfectly fluid, and the same fluid takes different degrees as heat and cold move its particles apart or together; but the degrees, Erxleben says, are hard to compare or measure.E 123
  • Why water swells some bodies. Ropes wetted shrink and lift great weights, and peas and beans swelling in water burst an iron pipe. Musschenbroek found that water, wine, vinegar and spirit of wine do this and oils do not, so it depends on something other than fluidity, present in watery fluids alone.B 6
