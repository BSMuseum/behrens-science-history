Snapshot c. 1775 · Natural philosophy

Body in general and its properties

What teaching texts from Graz, Göttingen, Paris and London said about body and matter in general between 1759 and 1776. They agree on a short list of properties: bodies are extended, impenetrable, divisible, and full of pores. They differ on whether division ever ends, on what bodies are finally made of, on whether there is empty space inside them, and on what holds their parts together.

First cut, from one text per region. Figure, hardness, elasticity and compressibility are not covered. Inertia is in the entry on motion, fluidity in the entry on water, and attraction at a distance in the entry on gravity.

Where body sat among the sciences

All four texts begin their courses here. Biwald treats body twice, as he does motion. The first section of his general physics gives what the senses show: a definition and four attributes. He leaves out of the list, on purpose, attributes such as the force of inertia and attractive force, “since they are called in doubt by some”.B 84 The second part then asks what bodies are made of and derives the same attributes from a law of forces.B 162B 224 Erxleben gives the subject nine short paragraphs straight after his introduction: extension, matter, density, pores, divisibility, hardness and cohesion.E 29–35 Nollet opens his course with it. His first lesson treats extension, divisibility, figure and solidity, and his second porosity, compressibility and elasticity.N 78N 81 Ferguson’s first lecture, “Of Matter and its Properties”, defines matter and gives it five properties, solidity, inactivity, mobility, divisibility and attraction, then passes to gravity, the centre of gravity, density, pores, expansion by heat, the loadstone and electricity.F 1F 15 The Britannica reprints the lecture at the head of its treatise “Mechanics”, and its short entries “Body” and “Matter” send the reader there.EB iii 32EB i 577

What the texts taught in common

  • A body is extended, and it keeps other bodies out of the place it fills. Biwald: “We shall therefore call body a solid, extended, divisible, movable substance.”B 84 Erxleben: besides extension, the essence of body requires something more, “which we call matter and which makes the body impenetrable”.E 29 Ferguson: “By the word matter is here meant every thing that has length, breadth, and thickness, and resists the touch”; by its solidity “each particular body hinders all others from occupying the same part of space which it possesseth”, and even water or air fixed between two bodies keeps them from touching.F 1–2 The Britannica prints the same words.EB iii 32 Nollet treats the same property as solidity, “also called impenetrability”.N 78
  • Where one body seems to penetrate another, it has only entered its pores. A sponge takes in water without growing, and equal parts of spirit of wine and water fill less room mixed than apart; one must distinguish, Nollet says, the apparent size of bodies from their real solidity.N 78 Biwald gives quicksilver entering gold and silver, and draws the same conclusion.B 85 Erxleben and Ferguson do not take up apparent penetration.
  • Every body has pores, gold included. Biwald proves it by induction: thin leaves of every body let light through, the microscope shows pores in metal foil, fire pervades the hardest bodies.B 86 Erxleben: gold is the densest body known and still holds a considerable quantity of interstices, and no perfectly dense body exists.E 31 Nollet: gold is porous, since quicksilver enters it in a moment.N 109 Ferguson: “All bodies are full of pores, or spaces void of matter: and in gold, which is the heaviest of all known bodies, there is perhaps a greater quantity of space than of matter”, since the particles of heat and magnetism pass easily through it and even water has been forced through it; and light passing through glass in every direction shows bodies to be far more porous than is generally imagined.F 15 The Britannica’s “Mechanics” prints the passage.EB iii 35
  • Bodies can be divided far beyond what the senses can follow. Biwald takes his examples from Pieter van Musschenbroek (1692–1761): gold leaf, dyes, scents, and the animalcules that Antoni van Leeuwenhoek (1632–1723) saw in pepper-water, the smallest a thousand million times less than a grain of sand.B 90–91 Erxleben names gold, colours and odorous things.E 32 Nollet promises experiments to show parts “so small that their number and their tenuity far surpass common ideas”, and begins by splitting a coin with burning sulphur.N 14–15 Ferguson’s examples from art: a grain of gold melted with a pound of silver is found in any grain of the mass, and a grain of gold beaten into leaf can be cut into parts of which a fifty-millionth of a grain is visible under a microscope. “Yet all this is nothing in comparison of the lengths that nature goes in the division of matter.” Leeuwenhoek found more animals in the milt of a single cod than there are men on the earth, a grain of sand bigger than four millions of them; and the particles of their blood exceed the particles of light as much as mountains exceed grains of sand.F 3–5 The Britannica, reprinting these pages, leaves the examples out and keeps only Ferguson’s geometrical proof.EB iii 33
  • Density is comparative. Mass is the quantity of matter under a given volume, and a body is called dense or rare only by comparison with another.B 88–89E 30–31 Ferguson: “The quantity of matter in all bodies is in exact proportion to their weights, bulk for bulk”, so heavy bodies are as much denser than light bodies of the same bulk as they exceed them in weight.F 15 The Britannica prints the same.EB iii 35 Nollet was not read on this point.

The disputes

Does division ever come to an end?

Ferguson says no: “The fourth property of matter is divisibility, of which there can be no end.” Matter can never be annihilated by cutting or breaking; however small a particle is cut, laid on a table its upper side is farther from the table than its under side; and “it would be absurd to say that the greatest mountain on earth has more halves, quarters, or tenth parts, than the smallest particle of matter has.”F 3 That matter is infinitely divisible in a mathematical sense he proves by a figure.F 5–6 The Britannica’s “Mechanics” reprints the claim and the figure, without the mountain.EB iii 33 Its own entry “Atom” says something else: an atom is “a particle of matter, so minute as to admit of no division”, and atoms “are conceived as the first principles or component parts of all physical magnitude”.EB i 502

Erxleben declines the question for physics:

“Experience can teach us nothing more here than that it goes very far; not yet that it goes on without ceasing. In general the answer to the question whether bodies are divisible to infinity belongs more before the tribunal of metaphysics than of natural philosophy.”E 32

Die Erfahrung kann uns hier weiter nichts lehren, als daß sie sehr weit geht; noch nicht, daß sie ohne Aufhören fortgeht: überhaupt aber gehört die Beantwortung der Frage: Ob die Körper bis ins Unendliche theilbar sind, mehr vor den Richterstuhl der Metaphysik, als der Naturlehre.

Nollet separates the division the mind can make, which has no bounds, from the division nature allows. Whether that second kind goes on to infinity “is only a matter of system, in which probabilities are found for and against”.N 14 He states a leaning and withdraws its proof in one sentence:

“Although I am more inclined to admit atoms, or uncuttable corpuscles, than to suppose matter physically divisible to infinity, I cannot hide that the argument I have just cited, specious as it is, has not force enough to decide the question, and that it can be validly answered.”N 12

Quoique j’aie plus de penchant pour admettre les Atomes ou Corpuscules insécables, que pour supposer la matiére physiquement divisible à l’infini; je ne puis dissimuler cependant que l’argument que je viens de citer, tout spécieux qu’il est, n’a point assez de force pour décider la question, & qu’on y peut répondre validement.

Biwald answers twice. In his first part he says experience cannot settle it, and then that the phenomena make an end probable:

“From a more accurate consideration of the phenomena it seems to be made probable that the division of bodies does not go on to infinity, but is at length bounded by its own limits.”B 92

Ex phaenomenorum accuratiore consideratione probabile effici videtur, corporum divisionem in infinitum haud procedere, verum suis tandem terminis definiri.

In his second part the answer follows from what he takes bodies to be made of:

“On this view the first elements of matter have no divisibility at all, since they are simple and unextended; but any masses, which for us are nothing other than collections of the said points, can be divided into parts, though not into more than the number of the points that make up the mass.”B 227

Primorum quidem materiae elementorum in hac sententia divisibilitas nulla est, cum ea simplicia, & inextensa sint; at vero massae quaelibet, quae nobis aliud non sunt, quam dictorum punctorum congeries, dividi in partes possunt, non tamen plures, quam sit ipse punctorum massam constituentium numerus.

What bodies are finally made of

Biwald is the only one of the four to review the answers on offer. The three elements of René Descartes (1596–1650), ground from cubes of a first matter, he dismisses with a comparison to the opening of Ovid’s Metamorphoses:

“This system deserves no praise but that of an ingenious fiction, and no more belief than is commonly given to the pleasant inventions of the poets.”B 164

Systema hoc non aliam, quam ingeniosae fictionis laudem meretur, neque alia ei fides tribuenda, quam quae solet amoenis Poetarum commentis tribui.

The atoms of the ancients and of Pierre Gassendi (1592–1655) he takes more seriously, and he notes that Isaac Newton (1642–1727) seems to have adopted them in good part, giving his solid, hard, impenetrable particles certain active powers as well.B 166–167 His one objection is that atoms are supposed to differ in size, so that there could be an atom as big as the highest mountain that still could not be cut: how can a thing with really distinct parts be wholly incapable of being divided into them?B 167 The monads of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) he rejects for giving material substances a power of perception, which removes the difference between spirit and matter.B 168 The four common elements and the five of the chemists are themselves kinds of body, whose principles have still to be sought; the matter and form of the Peripatetics may be borne as an abstract notion and are of no use for knowing nature.B 169–170

His own answer is a proposition:

“The principles of bodies are simple substances endowed with motive force.”B 176

Principia corporum sunt substantiae simplices vi motrice praeditae.

These are what Father Roger Joseph Boscovich (1711–1787) calls indivisible and unextended points. Biwald places the doctrine between two others: it agrees with Leibniz in making the elements simple substances, and the active forces it gives them “are after the mind of Newton”.B 177 A body, on this view, is an aggregate of substances and is called a substance only in the common way of speaking:

“Bodies seem to stand to those simple substances of which they are composed as an army stands to the single soldiers of which it is made up.”B 176

Corpora videntur se habere ad substantias illas simplices, ex quibus coalescunt, eo modo, quo se habet exercitus ad milites singulos, ex quibus componitur.

Nollet leans to atoms and leaves it there. In his second volume he remarks that the hardness of the parts we take for actually indivisible may be no greater than that of a larger mass of the same matter, and seems excessive to us only “because we know no agent that can cut into them”.N ii 471 Erxleben speaks of “smaller corporeal particles” and does not say what they are.E 34 Ferguson does not take the question up. The Britannica’s entry “Atom” calls atoms the first principles of all physical magnitude, and its entry on the atomical philosophy describes that as the system of Epicurus (341–270 BC).EB i 502

Is there empty space inside bodies?

Nollet uses the word void and takes away its strict sense at once:

“The porosity of bodies is nothing other than the void that is found between their solid parts; and by this word void we do not mean spaces deprived of all matter: it is beyond doubt that the greater part of these interstices lodges fluids whose presence shows itself by a thousand proofs.”N 81

La Porosité des corps n’est autre chose que le vuide qui se trouve entre leurs parties solides; & par ce mot de vuide nous ne prétendons pas faire entendre des espaces privés de toute matiére: il est indubitable que la plus grande partie de ces interstices loge des fluides dont la présence se manifeste par mille preuves.

Erxleben grants that the larger interstices certainly hold some foreign matter, and then asks whether the very finest hold anything material, or whether there is a real scattered empty space, a vacuum disseminatum. His answer favours it:

“Whatever may be urged on metaphysical grounds against the existence of such an empty space, it cannot be denied that it can be defended on strong physical grounds.”E 31

Was man auch aus metaphysischen Gründen dem Daseyn eines solchen leeren Raumes entgegen setzen möchte, so ist doch nicht zu läugnen, daß es sich durch starke physische Gründe vertheidigen lasse.

Biwald reports that Descartes, making the essence of body its three dimensions, held a space empty of all body to be impossible, and that Gassendi put a scattered vacuum between his atoms.B 163B 166 For Biwald himself the question takes another form. A body is a number of points with distances between them, near enough together that the intervals escape every sense; that is physical or sensible extension, and “mathematical extension, or extension strictly so called, we have already removed from nature”.B 226 To the objection that the senses show the parts of gold joined with no empty space between, he answers that the senses are not to be appealed to here, since the smallest things escape them as the greatest do.B 218 Ferguson takes empty space for granted. Pores are “spaces void of matter”, and gold may hold more space than matter; he argues with no one who denies a void.F 15 The Britannica’s “Mechanics” prints his sentence,EB iii 35 while its own entries define a vacuum as a space empty of all matter and a plenum as the state of things the Cartesians suppose, in which every part of space is full; neither entry says which obtains.EB iii, Vacuum; Plenum

What holds the parts of a body together

Nollet’s answer comes in the appendix to his lesson on hydrostatics. Solidity and fluidity depend on one cause: “it is the subtle air that fixes the parts of a matter, when its pressure from outside exceeds the reaction it makes within”.N ii 471 Biwald lists that opinion among the ones he rejects, and names Nollet:

“Pourchot, Nollet, and several Cartesians, among them most recently the celebrated Bertier, priest of the Oratory, derive the cohesion of bodies from the mutual contact of the parts along plane surfaces, and from the pressure of some very thin fluid.”B 237

Purchotius, Noletus, pluresque Cartesiani, quos inter recentissime Cl. Bertier Presbyter Oratorii, cohaesionem corporum a mutuo partium contactu secundum planas superficies, & tenuissimi cuiusdam fluidi pressu repetunt.

Edmond Pourchot (1651–1734) taught philosophy at Paris; Joseph-Étienne Bertier (1702–1783) is the Oratorian whose revived vortices Biwald answers elsewhere. Biwald rejects as well the rest of parts lying side by side, which was Descartes’s account; the conspiring motions of Johann Bernoulli (1667–1748) and Leibniz; the hooks of Epicurus and Gassendi; and the attraction in contact of the Newtonians.B 235–239 His own proposition places cohesion in the curve of forces:

“The cohesion of bodies arises from the placing of the points of matter at the limits of cohesion, or even outside those limits, yet at those distances at which forces equal and opposite on either side cancel one another.”B 239

Cohaesio corporum oritur ex collocatione punctorum materiae in limitibus cohaesionis, vel etiam extra eosdem limites, in iis tamen distantiis, in quibus vires utrinque aequales, & oppositae se elidunt.

Erxleben rules out a glue between the particles, little hooks on them, and the pressure of an outer matter, and concludes for a force of their own:

“One should therefore almost think it more probable that the smaller corporeal particles themselves possess a real inner force of holding together, which must consequently belong also to the larger parts composed of them, and to bodies themselves.”E 34

Man sollte es also fast für wahrscheinlicher halten, daß die kleinern körperlichen Theilchen selbst eine wirkliche innere Kraft besitzen untereinander zusammenzuhangen, die folglich auch den aus ihnen zusammengesetzten grössern Theilen, und den Körpern selbst zukommen muß.

Ferguson makes attraction the fifth property of matter, one which “seems rather to be infused than inherent”, and cohesion the first of its four kinds, “that by which the small parts of matter are made to stick and cohere together”. His instances are water rising in a narrow glass tube, sugar and a sponge drawing up fluids, drops of quicksilver running together, scraped lead pressed with a twist, polished plates and floating corks. That it is not the pressure of the air he shows by the tube, which is open to the air at the top, and by the lead and the plates, which cohere as strongly in an exhausted receiver. It “reaches but to a very small distance”, and where it ends a repulsive force begins.F 6–7 The Britannica’s “Mechanics” reprints the passage.EB iii 33–34

Why they differed

What physics may decide

Three of the texts say that the senses reach only a little way into body. Biwald says it most strongly, in his first part:

“We are wholly ignorant of the inmost essences of things; those who linger over investigating them therefore waste their labour. For which reason we dismiss as vain and useless the questions once commonly debated here, on the metaphysical essence of body, and on the first attribute from which all the others flow.”B 84

Essentias rerum intimas penitus ignoramus: qui proinde iis investigandis immorantur, operam ludunt. Quamobrem quaestiones olim hic agitari solitas, de essentia metaphysica corporis, de primo attributo, ex quo caetera omnia dimanant, tanquam vanas, inutilesque missas facimus.

Nollet draws the practical consequence. Of his three conclusions on divisibility only the last, that bodies have a multitude of very small separable parts, admits the kind of proof to which his book is limited, and so: “I appeal to experience.”N 14–15 Erxleben sends the question of infinite division to metaphysics and goes on. Biwald alone then crosses the line he drew. In his second part he argues to the elements of bodies from propositions which, he grants, belong to the court of metaphysics, and which he brings in so that the whole doctrine of the principles of bodies may be seen together.B 170

One fact, two readings

Biwald and Nollet weigh the same argument for an end to division: nature keeps its kinds. As Biwald gives it from Musschenbroek, the constant order of the universe, in which the same bodies are regenerated from dissolved ones with the same properties, shows that bodies are not resolved into infinitely small parts; if they were, endlessly various particles would form and new kinds of things would appear.B 92 Nollet states it with an oak that is always an oak and a horse that is today what it was at the beginning, and calls it specious. Even if the first particles could be cut, he replies, the Author of nature would have provided for the lasting of his works by leaving in the world only means too weak to disturb them.N 12 He takes a different lesson from the fact, against those who hope to change one matter into another: “ought we to flatter ourselves that we shall work miracles in our laboratories?”N 13

Active or passive matter

Biwald’s elements rest on a chain of propositions that begins: every substance is endowed with active force, and bodies are so endowed. A body resists being moved, keeps the motion it has, and sets another moving, and these are marks of an active force.B 170–172 Since a composite thing has no activity of its own, the activity must belong to simple things of which it is composed, and those are his points.B 176 He turns the argument on the Cartesians, who make bodies only the occasions of the effects they seem to produce.B 172 Ferguson holds the position he is arguing against. The second property of matter is “inactivity, or passiveness”; that matter can never put itself into motion “is allowed by all men”, and the moon goes round the earth “although she be as unactive and dead as any stone upon it”.F 2–3 The Britannica’s own entry “Body” puts it more strongly: body is “an extended solid substance, of itself utterly passive and inactive, indifferent either to motion or rest”.EB i 577

Why a pressing fluid will not do

Erxleben and Biwald give the same reason against holding bodies together by an outer fluid. If the particles are pressed together by a surrounding matter, Erxleben says, “one could then ask again what holds its parts together”.E 33 Biwald adds that the pressure of common air is far too small, by Musschenbroek’s trials with cylinders; that the existence of a subtler fluid is assumed without proof; and that a fluid passing freely through the pores of every body would press as much from within as from without.B 237–238 That last point is the one both press against the fluids offered as the cause of gravity. Biwald’s objection to the Newtonians is of another sort. They put cohesion in the attraction of parts in immediate contact, and he has already shown that there is no contact strictly so called.B 239 Ferguson’s case against the air is an experiment: the lead and the polished plates cohere as strongly in an exhausted receiver.F 7

Questions the texts left open

  • How much of any body is pore. Musschenbroek, as Biwald quotes him, regrets that not one wholly solid mass is known, since the solid and the porous part of every other body could then be found by weighing.B 89 Nollet says such a body “will never be more than a supposition”, and reports from Newton the conjecture that gold may hold as much void as full.N 109 Ferguson goes further: in gold “there is perhaps a greater quantity of space than of matter”.F 15
  • What mass is. Biwald, having defined it as the number of points under a given volume, calls the idea of mass “very vague and indeterminate”: it is hard to say which portion of matter belongs to the constitution of a body, and we must be content with the common estimate.B 227
  • Whether solidity is more than a matter of moderate speeds. If we could give ourselves a great enough velocity, Biwald says after Boscovich, we could pass through closed doors and walls without any true penetration; and if the bodies about us always moved so fast, we should have no idea of solidity at all.B 226
  • Whether one matter can be changed into another. Nollet thinks the constancy of nature should make us slow to promise it, and says that chemistry is giving up such claims.N 13–14
  • Whether two principles are enough. Biwald reports that Johann Theodor Eller (1689–1760) of the Berlin Academy made water and fire the principles of all things, and calls his trials worthy of being confirmed by more frequent repetition.B 169
  • Whether a composite must be made of simples. Biwald answers at length a dissertation by Johann Heinrich Gottlob von Justi (1717–1771), crowned by the Berlin Academy in 1747, which denied it.B 177
