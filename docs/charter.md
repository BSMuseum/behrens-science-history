**Source of truth since 2026-11-24: the GitHub repository BSMuseum/behrens-science-history** (site: https://history.bsmuseum.org/). The claude.ai project's docs and the two year-page artifacts are frozen as of that date and are no longer updated. Where this charter mentions `claude/claims/...` paths, the artifact as the site, or publishing with the artifact tools, read: the repository's `data/`, `claims/`, `entries/` and `site/`, and a pull request (see `CONTRIBUTING.md`). Registries are in `data/`; the checker runs from the repository root as `python3 -I tools/check_claims.py`.

Last updated 2026-11-17 (project renamed from "History of Science" to "Behrens Science History"; doc paths, such as this file's, keep their old names so links from other tabs still work. Earth and the bodies drawn from it published; meteors published the same day; the terraqueous globe published the same evening, so all thirteen 1775 entries are published). Working record for future sessions; decisions are Bruce's unless marked "proposed".

## Scope decisions

- Product: a browsable public site, navigable by period and by topic. A book with a thesis may come later, drawn from the site. For now the site artifact stays private (read: owner): Bruce does not want it shared, and wants it where his agents can update it (2026-11-16).
- Minimum viable product (2026-11-16), meant to attract support: natural philosophy at three snapshots, c. 1575, c. 1675 and c. 1775, with a machine-readable claims file for every entry and one or two cross-snapshot visuals (below). The long-term aim stays comprehensive (medicine, chemistry and alchemy, natural history and so on), because fields move between disciplines over time; the MVP is limited to natural philosophy. A public site where volunteers contribute their own Claude time may come later; "Don't do that yet."
- Span: first snapshot c. 1275, last no later than 1900. Stop earlier if the method stops working.
- Intervals: roughly fixed, anchored on 1575 (2026-11-16): 50-year steps 1275-1625 (1275, 1325, 1375, 1425, 1475, 1525, 1575, 1625), then 25-year steps 1675-1900 (18 snapshots). Each snapshot is a window of about a decade centered where good sources exist. This replaces the earlier grid of 50-year steps from 1250.
- Order: finish the pilot c. 1775 (done 2026-11-17, with the terraqueous globe); the persistent questions and the claims format were designed against the ten published 1775 entries and their claims files backfilled (done 2026-11-17); then c. 1575 and c. 1675. The order after the MVP is not yet decided (the earlier plan was to work forward from the oldest).
- Geography: Latin Europe and its vernacular successors. Other traditions only if this works.
- Voice: present the period's ideas and the evidence for them, in the period's terms. No modern verdicts. Modern concepts appear only as brief background glosses so readers can connect terms (dephlogisticated air -> oxygen; consumption -> tuberculosis).
- Controversy: where schools or countries taught different things, record each and explain why they differed. Bruce finds this the interesting part.
- Topics: taken from the period's own classifications (curricula, encyclopedias, textbook tables of contents). Each snapshot keeps its own topic list; do not fit c. 1575 into the 1775 list (in 1575 natural philosophy still includes the soul, animals, plants, meteorology and the heavens as a fifth element). Showing subjects leave natural philosophy is the point of the comprehensive scope.
- Questions: a persistent question layer runs across snapshots (2026-11-16). Every claim is tagged with a stable question ID ("what is light?", "why do heavy bodies fall?", "does the earth move?", "can water become earth?"), independent of each snapshot's topics. It is designed before any 1575 reading, since it is the hardest part to retrofit.
- Claims files: one machine-readable claims file per entry, stored in the project (format proposed 2026-11-16 in claude/claims/README.md).
- Visuals: the MVP includes one or two cross-snapshot visuals: lifelines of the answers to three or four persistent questions across 1575-1675-1775, and a diagram of the boundaries of natural philosophy shifting.
- Layer: taught consensus is the product. Journals used only to date when a claim entered or left the textbooks.
- Sources: primary only, public scans. Every claim cited to edition, page, and scan link. 3-5 leading textbooks per region to start; adjust as we go. Bruce may be able to get an occasional paywalled item if really needed.
- Choice of textbooks: Bruce has no opinion; Claude chooses by a fixed, stated rule applied the same way in every snapshot (for example, the most-reprinted course in each region's main teaching tradition), so that later counts mean something (2026-11-16). The rule is proposed in claude/claims/README.md. The pilot's choices were made earlier by period evidence of importance (reprint record; textbook authors' own statements of which books they drew on).
- Review: Bruce trusts the translations and will review lightly. Claude verifies its own readings against page images.
- Bruce does not read French.
- People: key players' first names, and birth and death years in parentheses on first use, per entry. Life dates come from modern reference works.
- Entry format: Bruce approved it ("really good", 2026-11-13) and said to do the rest of natural philosophy c. 1775, in batches; he answers "go" to continue.
- Bruce runs more than one chat tab on this project at once (2026-11-14: one tab did water while another did gravity and the system of the world). Before publishing or writing this charter, read the current version again and change only your own parts.
- Bruce asks one tab to review another's entry for consistency and likeness to the rest, then to fix what it found (water on 2026-11-14, motion on 2026-11-15). The conventions below under "Entry conventions" came out of those reviews.

## Method that works

1. Find the edition on Internet Archive (advancedsearch API) and note the identifier.
2. Download the OCR text (`/download/<id>/<id>_djvu.txt`) to locate passages by keyword. OCR is for finding, not quoting.
3. Download `<id>_djvu.xml` or `<id>_page_numbers.json` to map a passage to its scan leaf number; confirm the printed page from the image, since offsets drift and the page-number files are sometimes off by one or more. A quick way to find an offset: fetch several leaves, crop the top strip of each, stack the strips into one image and read the page numbers. (Nollet's EPFL scans show the book edge at the top; crop from about 5% to 16% of the height.) For items with a page_numbers.json, the `/page/n` index in URLs is leafNum - 1 (checked on Para du Phanjas, 2026-11-17).
4. Fetch the page image (`/download/<id>/page/n<leaf>_w600.jpg`; Google-scan items ignore the width, so downscale locally before reading) and read the scan directly. Quote and translate from the image, never from OCR. Pasting two small pages side by side into one image halves the number of reads.
5. Cite as author, title, year, section and printed page, with the scan link `archive.org/details/<id>/page/n<leaf>/mode/1up`.

Multi-file IA items (the 1771 Britannica) do not serve `/page/n` images. Use `<file>_page_numbers.json` for nearby leaves, then `/download/<id>/<file>_jp2.zip/<file>_jp2%2F<file>_<NNNN>.jp2&ext=jpg&reduce=1` (file stems "Volume 1, A-B", "Volume 2, C-L" and "Volume 3, M-Z", URL-encoded). At reduce=1 a page is about 1240-1980 px wide; a vol. 1 page is legible whole at reduce=1, a vol. 3 page at reduce=2. In the vol. 1 OCR a page is only about 150 lines. Dictionary pages carry the page number in the running head as "( 502 )"; grep the OCR for that pattern near an entry to find its page. These jp2 requests are slow; give them a 110-second timeout and crop to the column needed.

Munich (MDZ, digitale-sammlungen.de) items, found through the Europeana search API (`api.europeana.eu/record/v2/search.json?wskey=api2demo&query=...`) because the MDZ search page needs JavaScript:
- word search with hit locations: `api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/services/search/v1/<bsbID>?q=<word>` (returns canvas numbers);
- page OCR as hOCR: `api.digitale-sammlungen.de/ocr/<bsbID>/<canvas>` (the first line usually carries the printed page number, which makes offsets quick to check);
- page image: `api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/image/v2/<bsbID>_<5-digit canvas>/full/900,/0/default.jpg`;
- reader link for citations: `www.digitale-sammlungen.de/en/view/<bsbID>?page=<canvas>`;
- catalogue data (date, extent): `api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/presentation/v2/<bsbID>/manifest`.

Uploaded Google Books PDFs carry a text layer: `pdftotext -layout` to find passages, `pdftoppm -r 90` to render a page for reading.

Rule kept so far: anything inside quotation marks has been read from the page image. Paraphrases may rest on OCR, and the entry footer says so. Keep reads targeted (grep, then print a few pages); printing whole chapters is wasteful.

Reachable from the cloud workspace: archive.org, e-rara.ch, digitale-sammlungen.de, gdz.sub.uni-goettingen.de, wellcomecollection.org, api.europeana.eu. Not reachable: gallica.bnf.fr, babel.hathitrust.org, digital.onb.ac.at; Google Books refuses automated text requests.

## The site

Since 2026-11-24 the site is built from the repository by `tools/build_site.py` and served by GitHub Pages at https://history.bsmuseum.org/ (front page, `c-1775/`, `c-1675/`), rebuilt on every push to `main`. Each entry is a fragment `entries/<snapshot>/<topic>.html`; the page shell, style and router are in `site/templates/`; `site/pages.json` gives each year page's entry order and tab titles. The markup conventions below still hold; the artifact instructions describe how the pages were made before the move and are kept for the record.

Artifact "Natural Philosophy c. 1775", https://claude.ai/artifact/3ZnZZeJVGKYPy582ehrreu (slug 14c627fb-e753-4737-ab22-723793622c82), in the Artifacts folder "Behrens Science History" since 2026-11-17 (before that, "History of Science"). One index.html with views routed on the hash: home (topic list with status), #electricity, #magnet, #light, #fire, #air, #water, #gravity, #world, #motion, #body, #earth, #meteors, #globe. The home eyebrow and every entry footer name the project as "Behrens Science History". The artifact is the source of truth; workspace copies are temporary. To add an entry: add an `<article class="view" id="v-NAME">`, add NAME to the `views` and `titles` objects in the script, switch its row in the home table to a "Read the entry" pill, and update the count in the home status line. Section ids are prefixed per entry (h- electricity, m- magnet, l- light, f- fire, a- air, w- water, g- gravity, s- system of the world, o- motion, b- body, r- earth, m- meteors, k- terraqueous globe, t- home).

Every article must sit inside `<div class="wrap">`, which closes just before the script; an article placed after that `</div>` loses the page width and padding. The safe insertion point is immediately before `<article class="view" id="v-electricity">`. Order in the file does not matter.

Two ways to publish:
- Small additions: edit_version with a few anchored replacements (the status-line count, the home-table row, the last key of each of the two script objects, and the new article inserted before the electricity article). Nothing has to be read into the conversation.
- New entries and larger repairs: read_file with `paths: ["index.html"]` saves the page as a local file without putting it in the conversation. Copy it under /home/claude, edit it with a Python script that asserts each replacement matches exactly once (for a new entry, build the article as a string with small helper functions that turn a printed page into its linked citation chip, so offsets are applied in one place), check tag balance with html.parser, render it in headless Chrome (`/usr/bin/google-chrome` and Python playwright are both installed; load `file://...index.html#NAME` and screenshot), then publish_files with base_version. publish_files only accepts files under /home/claude. Check with get_artifact just before publishing that the live version is still the one you read.

Entry sections: the texts read (one card per region with a siglum); where the subject sat among the sciences; what the texts taught in common; the dispute(s); a table of where each text stood; why they differed, from the texts' own words; questions the texts left open; terms for modern readers (the only place modern concepts appear); scans, method, and what has not yet been read.

Entry conventions:
- Common ground is a `<ul class="plain">` list; each item opens with a bold sentence stating the agreed point. Where an item rests on fewer than four texts, say which were not read on it.
- Dissent from common ground (2026-11-22): an item moves to the disputes, with its own subsection and table row, when a regional text holds or leans to an answer the item excludes. Silence (not taken up, not found) does not move it, and neither does a difference of emphasis or classification that contradicts nothing (Martin's graded fixity of metals, Ferguson's rain by the air growing light); say the difference inside the item. Supplementary texts never move an item. Applied the same day to the earth (quicksilver frozen), meteors and electricity (lightning) and fire (whether its nature is settled).
- Every row of the "where each text stood" table has its own narrated subsection under "The disputes". A cell that does not answer the row's question says "Not taken up" or "Not found in the parts read".
- "Why they differed" gives reasons the texts themselves give. A generalization of the compiler's own must hold for all four texts or be dropped, and a gloss on one author must match the page cited for it.
- "All four" only when all four are cited.
- Citation chips in prose are links to the page scan; chips inside tables stay plain.
- Biwald's references link to the Munich scans (below) even where the reading was done from a PDF.
- Names: "Father Roger Joseph Boscovich (1711-1787)" is the form used across the site. The Munich library is "Bayerische Staatsbibliothek". Dates follow a name, not a possessive.
- Where a topic's text for a region differs from the other entries (R1 clause 5), the "four texts" section says why, in a second key paragraph (first done in the earth entry).

## Pilot c. 1775, natural philosophy: topic list and status

Topics follow Biwald (Physica generalis 2nd ed. 1769; Physica particularis 1768) and Erxleben (1772; sections: 1 introduction, 2 bodies in general, 3 motion, 4 gravity, 5 cohesion, 6 fluids, 7 air, 8 light, 9 warmth and cold, 10 electricity, 11 magnetic force, 12 world-system and earth in general, 13 the earth in particular).

1. Body in general and its properties - published 2026-11-15 (extension, impenetrability, pores, divisibility, density as common ground; disputes: does division end, what bodies are finally made of, empty space inside bodies, the cause of cohesion. Not covered: figure, hardness, elasticity, compressibility; Erxleben's section 5 on cohesion in detail)
2. Motion and the balance of forces - published 2026-11-14, repaired 2026-11-15 (quantity of motion, balance, action and reaction, collision rules as common ground; disputes: is inertia a force, how motion passes between bodies, the measure of force. Not covered: pendulum, inclined plane, projectiles, central forces, centre of gravity, the machines)
3. Gravity - published (cause of gravity; laws of fall as common ground; lighter weight near the equator. Not covered: pendulum, machines, Biwald's proofs for the moon)
4. The system of the world - published (certainty of the Copernican arrangement, Scripture, cause of the planets' motion, inhabitants of the planets. Not covered: fixed stars, comets, moon, eclipses, tides, figure of the earth)
5. Water and other fluids - published, repaired 2026-11-14 (nature of water, fluidity, internal motion, pressure, capillary tubes, compressibility, water into earth; efflux, waves, ice and vapour not covered)
6. Air - published (weight, spring, air from bodies; winds and sound not covered)
7. Fire, warmth and cold - published (nature of fire and heat only)
8. Light and colours - published as "Light" (nature of light; colours touched on)
9. Electricity - published
10. The magnet - published (Nollet only partly read)
11. Earth and the bodies drawn from it - published 2026-11-17, with claims file 1775-earth.json (earths and stones, salts, inflammables, metals, semimetals. Texts: Biwald, Physica particularis Part IV; for Göttingen, Erxleben's Anfangsgründe der Naturgeschichte (2nd ed. 1773), §§664-745, since his Naturlehre leaves minerals to natural history (proposed R1 clause 11 in the claims README); for Paris, Para du Phanjas, Théorie des êtres sensibles (1772), since Nollet has no lesson on minerals (clause 5); the Britannica's Chemistry and entry Alchemy. Not covered: petrifactions, figured stones, strata, mountains, earthquakes and volcanoes, left to the terraqueous globe; salts and each metal in detail)
12. Meteors - published 2026-11-17, with claims file 1775-meteors.json (common ground: meteors made of what rises from the earth, clouds as raised fog, rain, snow, prodigious rains as natural, the rainbow, fiery meteors as exhalations, winds from a disturbed equilibrium; disputes: what lightning is (electricity for three; fermenting effluvia for Ferguson, moved from common ground 2026-11-22), what raises vapours, does dew fall, hail, waterspouts, lightning upward, iron rods, the northern light. Texts: Biwald Part V and his winds in Part II; Erxleben §§664-701; Nollet's applications in Lessons XI, XIII, XVII and XX, kept as the Paris text under a proposed reading of clause 5 (claims README); the Britannica's entries and treatises. Not covered: the barometer as a weather sign, rain gauges, halos and mock suns only as open questions; Para du Phanjas's treatise on meteors, t. 4 §784ff, for widening)
13. The terraqueous globe - published 2026-11-17, with claims file 1775-globe.json (common ground: the earth flattened at the poles; the moon, with the sun, raises the tides; shells and fishes in the rocks were sea creatures; the strata do not lie by weight (Britannica not found); volcanoes from matter kindled by fermentation; earthquakes from the same fires; springs fed at least in great part by rain and snow. Disputes: does the sea feed springs from below; where mountains come from; how the shells reached the mountains; a central fire; electricity in earthquakes; why the sea is salt; is the sea growing less; was the Flood universal; can it be explained naturally; the theories of the earth; the age of the earth. Texts: Biwald, Physica particularis Part VI, pp. 390-403, with Generalis pp. 341-346 (figure) and 444-460 (tides); Erxleben §§578-582 and §§702-763; for Paris, Para du Phanjas t. 2, third treatise, first section (§§491-520, pp. 3-75) with §610 and §§667-679 from his treatise on water, since Nollet has no lesson or heading on the globe (his springs t. 4 pp. 9-18, figure of the earth t. 2 pp. 148-156 and volcanoes t. 4 pp. 95-97 are named in the entry, not encoded); the Britannica's entries Terraqueous, Mountain, Sea, Earthquake, Vulcano, Deluge, Petrifaction and passages of Mechanics, Astronomy, Pneumatics and Hydrostatics. Not covered: the weather and climates, rivers and lakes in detail, the measuring of mountains; Erxleben's Naturgeschichte §§732-744 on petrifactions and figured stones and Para's §600ff; Para's chronology §§521-530 and his physical astronomy on the figure and the tides)

All thirteen published entries rest on one text per region. After the breadth pass, widen each to 3-5 texts per region. The five entries written before the review (electricity, magnet, light, fire, air) have not been checked against the entry conventions above.

## Findings so far

Electricity. Graz (Biwald) and Edinburgh (Britannica) teach Franklin's system; Göttingen (Erxleben) weighs Franklin and Nollet and prefers Franklin case by case; Paris (Sigaud) defends Franklin on the bottle but would choose Nollet's as the general system.

The magnet. Properties agreed; no agreed cause. Paris (Nollet): circulating magnetic matter. Göttingen: reports Descartes and Euler, doubts them, points to Tobias Mayer's inverse-square law. Graz: cause unknown; rejects both fluid and Boscovich's buried iron, citing the Styrian mines. Edinburgh: a kind of attraction, no cause.

Light. Graz and Edinburgh teach Newton's emission of particles; Göttingen teaches Euler's vibrating aether; Paris keeps Descartes's medium.

Fire. Paris and Edinburgh teach a matter of fire, the same as the matter of light (the Britannica's chemistry makes phlogiston fire fixed in bodies). Graz teaches that fire is a vehement internal motion of a body's own parts. Göttingen poses both as questions. Open: whether fire has weight (calcination weight gains; some attribute the gain to particles from the air).

Air. Weight, the Boyle-Mariotte proportion, and its necessity for life are common ground. Cause of its spring: Paris, coiled elastic filaments (Nollet gives hundred-to-one odds against the shape being true); Graz, a standing repulsion between molecules; Britannica, its expansion by heat. Air drawn from bodies (Hales's factitious air): Göttingen calls it air; Paris calls it true air that was fixed in the body; Graz, after Musschenbroek, says the fluid from burning and fermentation is a foul compound not to be called air; the Britannica's pneumatics says air cannot be fixed while its chemistry makes air a principle of bodies, and it speaks of a "vivifying spirit" in air destroyed by lungs and fire.

Water and other fluids. Common ground: water's sensible qualities; almost never pure; pressure as height, Archimedes; capillary facts (inverse to bore, independent of length, same in vacuo, mercury depressed). Disputes: (1) Is water a melted solid? Paris (Nollet, with Mariotte, Mairan, Boerhaave) and Edinburgh (Chemistry: "Solidity therefore seems to be its most natural state") yes; Graz (with Musschenbroek) fire alone does not make it fluid, fluidity from particle shape and Boscovich forces; Göttingen: heat loosens cohesion, no talk of natural state. The split does not follow the texts' views on attraction, since the Britannica teaches attraction and still calls water naturally solid. (2) Perpetual internal motion of a fluid's parts: Graz (Musschenbroek's reasons) and Göttingen ("not a single observation shows such an internal motion") report the opinion and reject it; Paris and Edinburgh do not take it up. (3) Capillary rise: Graz, Göttingen ("a powerful proof of the attractive force of matter") and Edinburgh (Jurin's ring) teach attraction; Paris says not yet explained, unequal pressure of some subtle fluid probably fundamental, and likens attraction-as-quality to occult qualities. Biwald refutes air, aether, electric fluid, and the old French "snake-shaped particles". (4) Compressibility: Graz (Florentine experiment) and Edinburgh say water cannot be compressed; Göttingen says now proven compressible (Canton 1762) and the Florentine experiment decides nothing. (5) Water into earth: Paris with Boerhaave says no (dust in the still); Graz gives five serious grounds for yes (lime, gypsum, plants/Boyle's willow, Eller, Marggraf) without a verdict; Göttingen calls it still doubtful, in the Swedish diminution-of-water debate (Linnaeus). Britannica: not found.

Gravity. Common ground: all bodies heavy, none light of itself; weight as quantity of matter; uniformly accelerated fall (15 Paris feet 1 inch in the first second in both Biwald and Erxleben), Galileo credited; equal fall in a vacuum; gravity reaches the moon and weakens with distance; Richer's pendulum at Cayenne (1672). Disputes: (1) The cause. Paris (Nollet): none of the known explanations is satisfying and intelligible; Newton's gravitation "abandons the cause for the effect", attraction is a novelty Newton disowned, Descartes's vortex is "less just than ingenious" and fails his own whirled-globe experiment; "let us keep to the phenomena". Göttingen (Erxleben): rejects Descartes, Huygens, Bilfinger and Wolff's gravific matter; "not afraid" to give attraction at a distance some probability; attraction explains as much as "impact" or "inertia" do; cites mountains deflecting the direction of gravity. Graz (Biwald): gravity is the last arm of the curve of forces, a universal mutual attraction; subtle-matter systems refuted in three classes and "almost antiquated"; quotes Cotes that simplest causes have no mechanical explanation and are not occult; also rejects Newton's Query 21 aether and Bertier's revived vortices; offers Bertier's hanging-strips experiment (from Musschenbroek) as terrestrial evidence. Edinburgh: Mechanics gives gravitation as a kind of attraction "rather infused than inherent", calls vortices absurd from the whirling table, names no rival; the entry "Æther" says Newton's attempt to assign the cause of gravity was conjecture, "the foible of a great man". (2) Why weight is less near the equator: Nollet, centrifugal force of the daily rotation; Erxleben, a new proof that the earth turns; Biwald, cause not yet known, the earth's figure likeliest, Bouguer's centrifugal table does not fit the pendulum measures.

The system of the world. Common ground: the Copernican arrangement (sun in the middle, six primary planets, moon, four satellites of Jupiter, five of Saturn); fixed stars are distant suns; Kepler's ellipses and period law; stations and retrogradations as appearances; Ptolemy abandoned; no vortices (Nollet silent). Disputes: (1) How certain the earth's motion is. Edinburgh: "the only true and rational scheme", demonstrated by Newton, "can never be shaken". Graz: the disposition that obtains in nature, "almost no one" now doubts it; weightiest proof the aberration of the stars; answers Riccioli's 77 arguments, and on Joshua says Scripture accommodates itself to common understanding. Göttingen: cannot well be settled by experiment but "far more probable"; introduced as a supposition ("Let the sun be set at ☉"). Paris: the hypothesis "generally received today", preferred to Ptolemy's for its simplicity. Only the Jesuit text raises Scripture. (2) What moves the planets. Graz: mutual gravity as the inverse square plus a projectile force; vortices cannot exist (comets) and Bertier is answered. Göttingen: universal gravity, with a centrifugal force God "seems to have impressed" at the creation. Paris: Newton went about it wisely and his attraction is a successful hypothesis and a convenient word, but as a virtue in matter it is an occult quality returned, "which has nothing to do with mechanism"; an unresisting heaven is "a matter that is not matter". (3) Inhabitants of the planets. Edinburgh: "little less than a positive proof, that all the planets are inhabited", argued from divine purpose. Graz: "very probable" for the primary planets, argued from analogy with the earth (Lalande's sheep; Genovesi's logic). Göttingen: mentions the inhabitants and declines the subject. Paris: only that the heavens were not made for man alone.

Motion and the balance of forces. Common ground: quantity of motion = mass x velocity (EB: "Upon this easy principle depends the whole of mechanics"); balance when quantities of motion are equal (Biwald and the Britannica only); action equals reaction; the same collision rules for hard and elastic bodies in the three continental texts (the Britannica's "Mechanics" does not treat collision). Disputes: (1) Is inertia a force? Paris (Nollet): "a real resistance", proved by colliding hanging balls, proportional to mass, not air and not weight. Edinburgh: "inactivity, or passiveness", and the entry "Inertia" gives two Newton definitions ("passive principle", "power implanted") without remark. Göttingen: "does a thing need a force of its own to remain what it is?"; inertia is the principle of sufficient reason applied to bodies. Graz: bodies do not positively resist change of state; they have a vis motrix, a perpetual striving to change place, and an opposite striving cannot coexist with it; the first law can be proved neither a priori (against Euler's sufficient-reason argument) nor from phenomena (no absolute rest, no uniform straight motion in nature); what mechanics calls resistance is "the mutual action of bodies". Biwald departs here from Boscovich himself, and his second-edition preface says critics "swore by the words" of Boscovich and that the question concerns the essence of body, not a word. (2) How motion passes between bodies: Paris by contact (no perfectly hard matter; a dent at the contact); Göttingen "an unsearchable mystery", and doubts whether the law of continuity rightly forbids perfectly hard bodies; Graz no mathematical contact at all, a repulsion growing without limit stops bodies short (Boscovich's law of continuity: speed cannot jump from 6 to 3); Edinburgh not taken up. (3) Vis viva: Graz a question of a name, given the number of demonstrations on both sides; Paris undecided, majority against Leibniz, agreement on effects, of little consequence for mechanics (cites du Châtelet for, Mairan against); Göttingen passes it over as too long for an elementary course; Edinburgh gives mv only.

Body in general. Common ground: body is extended and impenetrable (Biwald: "a solid, extended, divisible, movable substance"; EB: what "has length, breadth, and thickness, and resists the touch"); apparent penetration is entry into pores; every body has pores, gold included; division goes far beyond the senses (gold leaf, scents, Leeuwenhoek's animalcules); density is comparative. Disputes: (1) Does division end? Edinburgh: "Mechanics" says divisibility has "no end", while the entry "Atom" defines atoms as admitting "of no division". Göttingen: experience cannot say; the question belongs "before the tribunal of metaphysics". Paris: "only a matter of system", probabilities both ways; Nollet leans to atoms but finds the argument from the constancy of species (an oak is always an oak) specious. Graz: an end is probable from the same argument (via Musschenbroek), and certain on his own view, since the first elements are unextended. (2) What bodies are finally made of. Graz reviews and rejects Descartes's three elements ("an ingenious fiction", compared to Ovid), atoms (an atom could be as big as a mountain and still not be cut), Leibniz's monads (they blur spirit and matter), the four elements and the Peripatetic matter and form; his own proposition: "The principles of bodies are simple substances endowed with motive force", Boscovich's unextended points, agreeing with Leibniz on simplicity and with Newton on active forces; a body is to its points as an army to its soldiers. Paris: perhaps atoms. Göttingen: not taken up. Edinburgh: atoms "conceived as the first principles". (3) Empty space inside bodies. Paris: pores are a "void" only in name, mostly filled with fluids. Göttingen: a scattered vacuum "can be defended on strong physical grounds", whatever metaphysics objects. Graz: bodies are points at distances; continuous extension is "removed from nature". Edinburgh: defines vacuum and plenum, affirms neither. (4) Cause of cohesion. Paris: the outside pressure of a subtle air (t. 2 p. 471), and Biwald names Nollet with Pourchot and Bertier for this opinion. Göttingen: not glue, hooks or outer pressure (what would hold that matter's parts together?) but probably "a real inner force" of the particles. Graz: points resting at the limits of cohesion in the curve of forces; rejects rest (Descartes), conspiring motions (Johann Bernoulli, Leibniz), hooks (Gassendi), pressure, and Newtonian attraction in contact (there is no contact). Edinburgh: attraction of cohesion, shown not to be the air. Also: Biwald opens Part I saying "we are wholly ignorant of the inmost essences of things" and omits inertia and attraction from the attributes of body because some doubt them, then argues to the elements from avowedly metaphysical propositions in Part II; the Britannica's entry "Body" calls body "of itself utterly passive and inactive", the position Biwald argues against.

Earth and the bodies drawn from it. Placement differs: inside physics at Graz (Biwald, Part IV) and in Para's course (third treatise, "Theory of the earth", with the three kingdoms); natural history at Göttingen (Erxleben: "mineralogy without chemistry I cannot conceive"); chemistry in the Britannica; absent from Nollet. Common ground: earths and stones, salts, inflammables, metals and semimetals, told apart by fire, water and acids; stones differ from earths only in firmness (Biwald, Erxleben, Para); gold and silver withstand fire, the other metals burn to a calx; a calx becomes metal again when its phlogiston is restored; quicksilver hardens only in extreme cold (Para cites Petersburg, 25 December 1759). Disputes: (1) Kinds of earth: Graz, Pott's four (calcareous, gypseous, argillaceous, vitrescible), tested against Justi with the Graz college museum's gypsum; Göttingen, presumably four (siliceous, clay, calcareous, alum; gypsum is lime with vitriolic acid); Paris, five classes of stone; Edinburgh undecided between one impure earth and several. (2) Quicksilver: a metal (Erxleben; Biwald "seems"), a class apart (Para), a metallic body of a particular kind (Britannica). (3) Platina: a new metal (Biwald), a semimetal perhaps not its own (Erxleben), the third perfect metal (Para). (4) What metals are made of: earth, phlogiston and probably a mercurial earth (Britannica, after Becher and Stahl; Erxleben); phlogiston and elementary earth (Para); Biwald reports the chemists' three earths and gives Boerhaave's objection the last word. (5) Do minerals still form: Erxleben, always, by daily experience, metals perhaps from mine vapours, but no seed; Para, the destructible ones yes, gold, silver and platina only regather particles existing since the creation, and physics should seek no mechanism in the globe's first forming; Biwald, stones from a petrifying juice. (6) Transmutation: Para, impossible to created agents by the stability of nature the Creator decreed; Britannica, among alchemy's "many other things equally ridiculous". Open: Para on the divining rod ("moyen chimérique") and potable gold (no virtue); Erxleben on lime from shells and corals, chalk and flint; Biwald on borax.

Meteors. Placement: a part of its own at Graz (winds left with the air, and the part cut short "for want of room"); within the earth in particular at Göttingen, winds first; no lesson in Nollet, who treats meteors as applications in four lessons; in the Britannica an old dictionary definition (formed by the action of the heavenly bodies out of the common elements) with the substance in Pneumatics, Electricity and Optics. Common ground: meteors are made of vapours and exhalations from the earth; clouds are fog raised higher (Biwald from his own climbs in Upper Styria); rain is vapour gathered into drops; snow is frozen vapour, its flakes six-rayed (Biwald, Erxleben); rains of blood, sulphur and frogs are natural; the rainbow by one reflection and two refractions in drops (de Dominis, Descartes, Newton); lightning is electricity (Nollet claims the conjecture of 1749; his lesson on fire still prints the exhalation account with its difficulties, and a later footnote says the conjectures "have become almost certainties"); fiery meteors are inflammable exhalations; winds restore a disturbed equilibrium, chiefly by the sun's heat. Disputes: (1) what raises vapours: heat and internal motion plus the electric fluid (Biwald), heat and fermentation (Erxleben), the matter of fire leaving the warmer earth (Nollet), heat, with Derham's vesicles and Beccaria's electricity reported (Britannica). (2) Does dew fall: yes (Nollet, Britannica; Nollet makes the drops on plants transpiration), no (Erxleben, after Gersten), three kinds (Biwald, after Musschenbroek). (3) Hail: frozen drops (Erxleben, Nollet), snow turned to ice and the electric fluid after Beccaria (Biwald), nitrous particles (Britannica entry). (4) Waterspouts: electricity after Beccaria and Scherffer (Biwald), a whirlwind (Erxleben), probably contrary winds, not well known (Nollet), contrary winds in the entry and electricity in the treatise (Britannica). (5) Lightning upward: both ways (Biwald, citing Maffei; Britannica after Beccaria); not all from the ground, against Maffei (Erxleben); Maffei reported, not adopted (Nollet). (6) Iron rods: hardly to be doubted, against Nollet (Biwald); full instructions (Britannica); "much recommended", bells and guns may disperse or draw a storm (Erxleben); not in Nollet's lessons. (7) The northern light: Mairan's solar atmosphere "uncommonly well" (Erxleben); electricity after Franklin, Mairan rejected, Musschenbroek's doubt quoted (Biwald); not in Nollet; no cause in the Britannica. Biwald and the Britannica's Electricity, both after Beccaria, make electricity act throughout the air; Erxleben keeps it to thunderstorms and leaves its source open; Nollet calls the causes of natural electricity unknown.

The terraqueous globe. Placement: the last part of particular physics at Graz, with the figure of the earth and the tides in the general physics; the second half of "the earth in particular" at Göttingen, ending on the origin and end of the earth as "properly not even a subject of natural philosophy"; the first section of Para's theory of the earth (the earth as a planet left to astronomy); no article in the Britannica, whose matter is spread over short entries and the treatises. Common ground: the earth flattened at the poles; the moon raises the tides; shells in the rocks were sea creatures (Para against an anonymous author who grew them in the earth: then the furniture of Herculaneum grew there too); the strata not by weight; volcanoes and earthquakes from fermenting matter underground; springs at least largely from rain. Disputes: (1) Sea water under the land to springs: rejected by Biwald ("chemical laboratories, alembics") and Para (salt left behind, capillary tubes), possible in part for Erxleben ("mehrere Ursachen"), held by the Britannica's "Sea". (2) Mountains: many raised by underground fires (Biwald, "satis probabilia"); many original, others laid by water (Erxleben); the great chains by "the creative action of the Almighty", lesser ones heaped later (Para); a definition only (Britannica). (3) Shells on mountains: eruptions (Biwald); the land was once sea floor, against Moro (Erxleben); the universal Flood and shifts of the sea (Para). (4) Central fire: no (Biwald, Para, "chimerical"); "not unimportant grounds", with Mairan (Erxleben). (5) Electricity in earthquakes: Biwald "verissima"; Erxleben reports it and Mayer's change in the direction of gravity. (6) Sea salt: created salt (Biwald, Para); salt mines in the sea, with Halley's rivers reported (Britannica); undecided (Erxleben). (7) The sea shrinking: displaced, not diminished (Para); doubted against Linnaeus (Biwald); "not yet decided" (Erxleben). (8) Universal Flood: yes (Para, Britannica); both views reported (Erxleben). (9) Natural causes for the Flood: impossible, a miracle (Para); attempts "render their authors ridiculous" (Britannica); Burnet, Whiston and Woodward reported (Erxleben). (10) Theories of the earth: "romances" (Para); Burnet and Woodward refuted (Biwald); "theories enough", choose none (Erxleben). (11) Age: "5808, or 7572 years" from Scripture, more antiquity founded on no solid proof (Para); declined (Erxleben, "kann und darf ich hier nicht erweisen"); Halley's saltness clock reported (Britannica); not taken up (Biwald). Why they differed: where each draws the line of physics (Para at the creation, Erxleben at what can be known, Biwald nowhere, the Britannica at the Flood); Moses as historian (Biwald against Burnet, Para's chronology); the same warm cellars read for and against a central fire; purposes (Para, Britannica); one cause or several (Para's fifth wheel against Erxleben). Here sacred history and chronology, outside natural philosophy elsewhere, enter physics in Paris and Graz.

Pattern across the ten topics: Biwald (Graz, Jesuit, follows Boscovich) prefers forces between particles to special fluids or shapes, and teaches Copernicus and Newton without reserve; on inertia he parts even from Boscovich; he is the only one of the four who reviews rival systems at length and answers objections by name. Nollet (Paris) keeps Cartesian media, subtle matters and mechanical pictures, often with frank admissions of their weakness, and treats attraction as at most a name for an unexplained fact; on gravity he abandons the Cartesian picture too and stops at the phenomena; in the heavens he praises Newton's method and still refuses attraction as a cause; on motion and on divisibility he limits himself to what experiment can show. Erxleben (Göttingen) reports rival systems and often declines to choose, leaning toward Euler, but is firm on attraction in capillarity, gravity, cohesion and the planets; he speaks in probabilities, sends some questions to metaphysics, and on motion reasons from the principle of sufficient reason. The Britannica states one view per treatise without mentioning rivals, and its treatises and entries do not always agree with one another (air fixed or not; divisibility without end against atoms); its "Æther" entry gives a Baconian reason for not assigning causes, while its "Astronomy" argues freely from design.

Károly Simonyi, A Cultural History of Physics (Hungarian 1978; English 2012; open access from Routledge/CRC, 2024), checked 2026-11-25: a narrative of physics from antiquity to the twentieth century, told through its discoverers and with long excerpts from primary sources, set in its philosophical and artistic context. It differs from this project in four ways. It follows the frontier, not what was taught (the 1775 textbook authors are unlikely to appear). Its topics are chosen backward from modern physics, so meteors, minerals, the terraqueous globe and the Flood fall outside it. Superseded views appear as steps toward the theory that won, not side by side at one date. Its excerpts illustrate the story, not claims cited to a page scan. Useful as a secondary reference for what was at the frontier at a snapshot, alongside the journals.
