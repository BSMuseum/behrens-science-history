## Questions

A question is worded so it can be put, in the same words, to a text of any snapshot without a later period's concepts ("Why does a body keep moving after whatever set it moving has stopped acting on it?", not "Is inertia a force?"). The period's own terms go in `names`; terms expected but not yet seen in our texts go in `expected_names`. Each question lists its answers as codes; codes are added as texts are read and never change meaning. IDs are kebab slugs, never renamed or reused.

`status`: persistent (askable of every MVP snapshot), local (a 1775 debate with no earlier form, kept so every claim has a question), anticipated (not yet drawn from a published entry). `parent` links a narrower question to a broader one (water-into-earth under element-change). Three persistent questions carry a note that they cannot arise before a date (force-measure after 1686; air-spring and capillary-rise after c. 1660); for 1575 they are "not taken up" by construction.

Themes: matter, motion, heaviness, heavens, light and fire, air and water, electricity and the magnet, method, earth, meteors, life. Themes are for display only; they are not topics.

## Claims

One claim = one text's position on one question, in one sentence. A text that holds one answer and rejects another gets two claims. Fields:

- `id` (`<entry>-NNN`), `question`, `text` (the entry's siglum), `part` (common, dispute, open, why, or place for a claim the text makes where it places the topic, such as the Britannica's water among the four elements).
- `stance`: holds, rejects, leans, undecided (with `between`: the codes weighed), reports (gives a view without a verdict), declines (mentions the question and refuses to treat it), not_taken_up (the parts read do not raise it), not_found (the relevant parts have not been read). "Declines", "not taken up" and "not found" stay distinct, as in the entry tables.
- `answer`: a code from the question (required for holds, rejects, leans).
- `statement`: the position in a sentence, in the period's terms.
- `confidence`: `level` coded from the printed wording (certain, probable, possible, doubtful, asserted = stated flatly without a modal, none), plus `printed` (the original words) and `translation` where the text marks its confidence. Never fill `printed` from memory or OCR.
- `reasons`: each with a `kind` (experiment, observation, mathematics, authority, consensus, scripture, design, analogy, metaphysics, sufficient-reason, simplicity, explanatory-scope, refutation, other) and a short `detail`.
- `persons`: people the text names, with `role` (followed, opposed, reported, credited). This is the raw material for the borrowing network.
- `cites`: page (and section), scan URL, and `read_from` (image, ocr, unknown).
- `quote` (optional): original, translation, `from_image: true`. A quotation requires a citation read from the image.

Coverage rule: for every common-ground question and every row of the "where each text stood" table, each of the entry's regional texts has a claim, if only `not_found` or `not_taken_up`. The checker enforces this for common ground. Supplementary texts are exempt.

The entry block also carries `placement` (where each text puts the topic), which feeds the boundaries diagram, and `verification` (status, by, date), filled by the independent checking pass.

## Rule R1 for choosing texts

Revised 2026-11-16 after Bruce's comments on what counts as one work, coverage by topic, and the edition read.

1. **Regions.** Seven standing regions, by place of printing: France; Italy; Iberia; Catholic German lands (with Austria, Bohemia, Hungary and Poland); Protestant German lands (with Switzerland and Scandinavia); Low Countries; British Isles. The MVP reads four in every snapshot, the regions of the 1775 pilot: France, Protestant German lands, Catholic German lands, British Isles. Iberia and Italy are next in line.
2. **What qualifies.** A course in natural philosophy written for teaching (textbook, compendium, school commentary, or the physics part of a philosophy course), by an author who taught or wrote within the 100 years before the window opens. Excluded: single disputations and theses, editions of ancient and medieval texts, encyclopedias and dictionaries, journals, and single-topic treatises. Excluded works may be read as supplementary texts but do not fill a slot.
3. **One work or two.** Decided by the text, not the title. A later book is an edition of an earlier one if most of the earlier wording reappears in it in the same order: a reissue under a new title, an abridgment cut from the same text, a translation. Test: compare at least five passages spread through the book; if, allowing for OCR noise, at least two-thirds of each earlier passage's words reappear in order, it is the same work. A rewritten book is a new work, even under the old title. Record the test in `texts.json`. Example: Biwald's Institutiones physicae (Graz 1774) keeps 77-79% of the wording of his Physica generalis (1769) on four sample pages, so it is the same work; 's Gravesande's Physices elementa mathematica and Philosophiae Newtonianae institutiones would be one work if the test bears out their near-identity.
4. **Count.** Editions printed within 15 years either side of the snapshot's centre, by region of printing. An edition is a year and place of printing; issues of one printing count once; translations count. Where the volumes of a work were reprinted separately, count the printings of its first volume. Source: the USTC for editions to 1700; after 1700, ESTC, VD18 and the French and Italian union catalogues. Record the query, the date and the counts in `texts.json`.
5. **Choose, topic by topic.** For each topic of the snapshot, a region's text is the most-reprinted qualifying course in that region that treats the topic in a section of its own. A course treats a topic "in a section of its own" when it gives the topic passages of their own, under its own heading or classification, even if they sit inside lessons or chapters on other subjects (reading approved 2026-11-18; see below). Usually one course covers every topic and is the region's text throughout; where the leader covers only some topics, the next course in the count supplies the rest, and the entry names both. A text fills one region only: if it leads in two, it takes the one where it has more editions. If the runner-up has at least 80% as many editions, it is a tie, decided by period evidence of prescribed use (statutes, the Ratio studiorum, reading lists, later textbooks naming it as standard), then by a text that adds a confession or order not yet represented, then by scan quality. The runner-up becomes the region's second text when the entry widens.
6. **Edition read.** The edition with a complete public scan printed nearest the centre year, preferring one printed in the region.

7. **Minimum evidence** (approved 2026-11-16; prompted by Daneau, Britain c. 1575). A region is filled only if its leading course has at least two editions in that region in the window. Daneau has one: the 1580 London book is the second part of the 1578 translation, not a reprint (clause 4). The region is recorded as empty, which is itself a finding.
8. **Fallback** (approved 2026-11-16; same case). An empty region's slot passes to the region outside the four whose leading course has the most editions in the window, with clause 5's tie-breaks. For 1575: Low Countries (Valerius 8) against Iberia (Soto 7) is a tie; prescribed use does not separate them (each was its own university's course); Soto adds an order not yet represented (Dominican) and Valerius, a lay Catholic, adds neither, so Soto fills the slot.
9. **No slots by order or confession; widening by tradition** (approved 2026-11-16; prompted by the absence of a Jesuit text c. 1675). Slots are regional. When an entry widens, a region's second text is the runner-up in the count, unless a confession or religious order that taught in the region has no text yet; then that tradition's most-reprinted course in the region comes first. For the Catholic German lands c. 1675, the first widening brings in the leading Jesuit course printed there.
10. **Rule changes** (approved 2026-11-16). Every edge case that needs a decision becomes a numbered clause, with the case that prompted it and the date. The rule carries a version (this is R1 v6: v1 the first draft, v2 Bruce's changes on one work, topics and editions, v3 clauses 7-10, v4 the reading of clause 5 approved 2026-11-18, v5 clause 12 approved 2026-11-21, v6 clause 13 set 2026-11-22). Every text in `texts.json` records the version that chose it; when a clause changes, the selections are re-run.

12. **In existence by the centre year** (approved 2026-11-21; prompted by Nicholson, proposed as the British text c. 1775 for fire, electricity, the magnet, the earth and meteors). A course qualifies for a snapshot only if its first edition was printed no later than the snapshot's centre year. Editions printed after the centre year still count under clause 4, and the edition read may still be a later one under clause 6: the course must have existed and been available to teach from at the date, but the copy read need not have been printed by then. So Ferguson's Lectures on Select Subjects (first edition London 1760) qualify for 1775 and are read in the fifth edition (1776), while Nicholson's Introduction to Natural Philosophy (first edition London 1782) does not qualify for 1775. Numbered 12 because clause 11 is still a proposal. Re-run under clause 10 (2026-11-21): every other course chosen so far passes. For 1775: Nollet (t. 1 first printed 1743), Biwald (1767-68), Erxleben's Naturlehre (1772) and Naturgeschichte (1768), Para du Phanjas (1772), Ferguson (1760). For 1575 and 1675, every chosen course was first printed before its centre year (first-edition dates as generally recorded; not yet checked in the USTC). Daneau's English translation (1578) postdates 1575, but Britain c. 1575 was already empty under clause 7. Removing Nicholson sends the five topics to the next British courses in the count, Rowning and Martin (3 editions each in the window, both in print long before 1775). Their contents were checked the same day, and Martin's Philosophical Grammar became the British text for all five (see the note on Rowning and Martin under the 1775 table).

13. **One text, one slot** (Bruce, 2026-11-22; prompted by Rohault's gaps c. 1675: under clause 5 the next French course in the count was Goudin, who already fills the Catholic German slot, and "duplicating him just gives him two votes"). A text fills one region's slot in a snapshot, never two. Where a region's text has no section on a topic, clause 5 passes the topic to the next course in that region's count that fills no other slot and meets clause 7's minimum (two editions printed in the region in the window). If no course qualifies, the region's cell is left blank for that topic, and the blank is recorded as a finding. Applied to 1675 (USTC counts of 2026-11-16): France, where Rohault has no section (causes, plants, animals, the rational soul, magic), has Goudin next (3, taken), then Gautruche (1) and Du Hamel (1), none qualifying, so France is blank on those topics: Rohault's Cartesian physics stops at the human body. In the Catholic German lands, where Goudin has no section (the magnet, magic), Heinlein (1) and Gautruche (1) do not qualify, so the cells are blank. In the Protestant German lands and the British Isles the clause applies the same way if gaps appear; for Britain, Sennert's Epitome (3) is next in line. Re-run under clause 10: no 1775 selection changes (no 1775 text fills two slots).

Known blind spot: clause 4 counts print. Jesuit philosophy in the German colleges was dictated, and its printed trace is mostly theses, so the rule understates it (see 1675 below).

Proposed clause 11 (2026-11-17, not yet approved; prompted by Erxleben on minerals). An author's companion volume counts as part of the same course when the author says so in print, even if its title or faculty differs: Erxleben's Naturlehre preface (leaf 15) makes his Anfangsgründe der Naturgeschichte the "particular physics of the three kingdoms" and, with the Naturlehre, one handbook. On that reading the Naturgeschichte supplied Göttingen's text for the earth entry. Without the clause, clause 5 would pass the topic to the next Protestant German course in the count that treats it (Eberhard, 3 editions; not checked).

Reading of clause 5 (proposed 2026-11-17, prompted by Nollet on meteors; approved by Bruce 2026-11-18, prompted by the British text for the terraqueous globe; R1 v4). Headed passages count as a section of their own even inside lessons on other subjects. Consequences: Nollet stays the Paris text for meteors, since he names the three classes of meteors and treats them at length as "applications" in his lessons on air, fire and light (Para du Phanjas, who gives meteors a treatise of their own, is the first Paris text to add when the entry widens). Nicholson, Introduction to Natural Philosophy, is the British text for the terraqueous globe. No British course in the count gives the globe a section of its own, but Nicholson has headed passages on the structure of mountains, strata and volcanoes (opening ch. XVI, "Of mines and metals in general"), on earthquakes (as electrical discharges), on intermitting springs, and on the tides and the figure of the earth; Ferguson has only the tides and the figure. Re-run under clause 10: the earth entry is unaffected (Nollet has no passages on earths, salts or metals at all). The globe's Paris text is open: Para was chosen because Nollet treats springs (t. 4 pp. 9-18), the figure of the earth (t. 2 pp. 148-156) and volcanoes (t. 4 pp. 95-97) inside lessons on water and gravity; if those passages carry headings of their own, the reading makes Nollet the Paris text for those parts of the globe and Para the text for the rest (strata, mountains, the Deluge, the age of the earth). To be checked from the images of those pages. Superseded for the British globe: on 2026-11-21 Bruce gave the globe to Ferguson, whose tides and earthquakes have headed passages (see `british-texts.md`), and Nicholson fails clause 12 for 1775 in any case. The description of Nicholson above (ch. XVI, mountains, strata) comes from his second edition (London 1787); the first edition (1782) has none of it (see the note on Nicholson under the 1775 table).
